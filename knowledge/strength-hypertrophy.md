# Strength and hypertrophy training

> Part of the coach's knowledge base. Loaded on demand by the coach commands (/plan, /review, /menu, /setup). Rules, not a literature review. Source IDs refer to knowledge/sources.md. Evidence checked on 2026-10-24.

## How to read this file

Each rule carries a grade. **Strong:** consistent findings across several meta-analyses, umbrella reviews or major guidelines; apply by default. **Moderate:** supported by at least one meta-analysis or guideline, but with imprecision, inconsistency or limited populations; apply, and adjust to the client's response. **Emerging:** single or small trials, subgroup findings, narrative reviews or expert consensus; use as a reasonable default and say it is tentative. Most of this evidence comes from young adults, often untrained, and mostly men; where a rule is applied to women, older adults or long-term trained lifters, it is partly extrapolated, and the client's own progress outranks the averages.

## Key rules at a glance

- Every adult does resistance training covering all major muscle groups on at least 2 days per week; clients 65+ also do balance and functional work on 3+ days per week (strong) [S016].
- For muscle gain, build each priority muscle toward roughly 10 or more hard weekly sets, with diminishing returns as volume rises (strong) [S001], [S002], [S003].
- Count a set as 1.0 for the main mover and 0.5 for a clear synergist (moderate) [S003], [S072].
- Use 2-4 sets per exercise; one hard set is far better than nothing (strong) [S001], [S004], [S005].
- Weekly volume matters more than frequency for size; train each muscle as often as the schedule allows (strong) [S001], [S003], [S011].
- For strength, practise each main lift at least twice a week (moderate) [S001], [S003], [S012].
- Any load from about 6 to 30 reps builds muscle if sets end close to failure; heavier work (about 3-8 reps) is needed to maximise strength (strong) [S001], [S005], [S021], [S022].
- Train hard but not to failure; failure is not required for strength or size (strong) [S001], [S024], [S025], [S026].
- Take most working sets to about 1-3 reps in reserve (moderate) [S001], [S025], [S027].
- Use double progression: add load once the top of the rep range is beaten at the target effort (moderate) [S001], [S039], [S043].
- Periodisation models and special tempos are not muscle-building upgrades (strong) [S001], [S041], [S044].
- Lengthened partials are an option, not an upgrade over full range of motion (moderate) [S053], [S054].
- Keep a stable core of exercises; avoid "muscle confusion" (emerging) [S001], [S059].
- Cardio does not cancel muscle gain; lift first, and separate the two when power or maximal strength is the priority (moderate) [S089], [S090], [S097].
- Women get the same relative hypertrophy from the same programme (strong) [S139], [S140].
- Use one prescription for both sexes and individualise by response, not sex (moderate) [S001], [S140].
- Do not periodise training by menstrual-cycle phase (emerging) [S152], [S155], [S156], [S157].
- Older adults: start at 2-3 reps in reserve without failure or breath-holding, progress to 1-2 RIR after about 8 symptom-free weeks, and steer by RIR, not %1RM; ICFSR's 70-80% 1RM is a supervised prescription with a measured 1RM (moderate) [S001], [S110].
- Older adults: use fast concentric intent on suitable lifts (moderate) [S104], [S107], [S108], [S109].
- For bone, resistance training gives men and adults 65+ a small hip benefit, not a spine one; heavy loading in low bone mass only after screening and under supervision (moderate) [S792], [S793], [S790].
- Older adults and anyone with a falls history: balance work, not lifting alone, prevents falls (strong) [S116], [S117], [S118].
- Expect strength to rise fast in weeks 1-6 and size to need 8-12+ weeks; individual response varies widely (moderate) [S039], [S123], [S127], [S129].
- Screen before the first session with the ACSM algorithm: any symptom means stop and get medical clearance; known cardiovascular, metabolic or renal disease in an inactive client means clearance before any programme (moderate) [S119].
- All-out sprint intervals are not for sedentary beginners; they need all PAR-Q+ answers "no" and at least 4 weeks of symptom-free moderate cardio first (moderate) [S641].

## Baseline and general approach

### Public-health minimum
**Rule:** For every client, the non-negotiable baseline is at least 2 days per week of resistance training at moderate or greater intensity that covers all major muscle groups. For clients aged 65+, also program balance and functional-strength work on 3 or more days per week; it can be combined with the strength sessions. Volume beyond these minimums is a body-composition or performance choice, not a health requirement.
**Evidence:** WHO 2020 makes a strong recommendation (moderate-certainty evidence) for muscle-strengthening activity at moderate or greater intensity, involving all major muscle groups, on 2 or more days per week. It found no evidence for a dose-response with higher volumes of muscle-strengthening activity for its health outcomes. For older adults (65+) it adds a strong recommendation for varied multicomponent activity emphasising functional balance and strength training at moderate or greater intensity on 3 or more days per week, to improve functional capacity and prevent falls.
**Grade:** strong
**Applies to:** All adults for health outcomes, with the extra multicomponent recommendation for adults 65+. These are not hypertrophy targets.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** WHO's "no dose-response" refers to health outcomes and does not contradict the volume-hypertrophy dose-response in training studies.
**Sources:** [S016]

### Keep it simple and individualised
**Rule:** For beginners, prescribe a simple full-body or upper/lower plan they will actually follow: 2-3 sessions per week, major movement patterns, controlled full range of motion, sets ending a few reps short of failure, then progress. Let the client pick frequency, split and equipment. Do not let "optimal" details get in the way of adherence.
**Evidence:** ACSM 2026 (overview of 137 systematic reviews, >30,000 participants) concludes that few prescription variables materially change primary adaptations: training to failure, equipment type, exercise complexity, set structure, time under tension and periodisation did not consistently affect outcomes. Its authors consider individualising programmes to increase participation more important than meeting specific prescription criteria. Resistance training is safe for healthy adults of all ages; in >38,000 participants exercise did not increase serious adverse events.
**Grade:** strong
**Applies to:** Healthy adults 18+, novice to experienced; much of the evidence comes from inexperienced trainees.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Trained lifters chasing maximal strength may benefit more from specific variables (loads ≥80% 1RM, undulating loading). Individual preference legitimately drives the choice.
**Sources:** [S001]

## Training volume

### Weekly sets per muscle (dose-response)
**Rule:** For a client whose priority is muscle gain and who has time, move each major muscle toward roughly 10 or more hard weekly sets over time, split over 2 or more sessions, counting sets with the fractional rule (see "Counting sets"). Treat 10 as a better-than-lower reference point, not a hard minimum; fewer sets still work. Raise volume gradually and only while recovery and adherence hold. For strength or health goals, fewer sets suffice because returns diminish quickly.
**Evidence:** ACSM 2026 found hypertrophy enhanced by ≥10 sets per muscle group per week (dose-response) compared with lower volumes. In Schoenfeld 2017 (15 studies, 34 groups) each additional weekly set added about 0.37% muscle growth (ES +0.023), and higher versus lower volume gave 3.9% more growth (ES difference 0.241). Pelland 2026 (67 studies, n=2058, 79% male, mean age 25) found a 100% posterior probability that more volume increases both size and strength, with diminishing returns that are much stronger for strength.
**Grade:** strong
**Applies to:** Healthy adults 18+. Trials mostly used young, often untrained men. The direction generalises well; the size per set is less certain for women and older adults.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Studies agree on direction but the effect per set is imprecise; Schoenfeld 2017's 3-category analysis (<5, 5-9, 10+ sets) was only a trend (p=0.074). The useful upper limit is individual and is capped in practice by recovery, time and enjoyment.
**Sources:** [S001], [S002], [S003]

### Multiple sets versus one set
**Rule:** Start beginners at 1-2 sets per exercise if needed for adherence, then progress to 2-4 sets per exercise for muscles the client wants to grow. Do not tell a client that one set does nothing.
**Evidence:** Krieger 2010 (8 studies, 19 groups) found effect sizes of 0.24 for 1 set, 0.34 for 2-3 sets and 0.44 for 4-6 sets; multiple sets gave about 40% larger effects than one set (ES difference 0.10, p=0.016), and 2-3 versus 4-6 sets did not differ (p=0.29). In Currier 2023 (Bayesian network meta-analysis, 119 hypertrophy studies, n=3364, 47% women) all prescriptions beat no exercise; the top-ranked was higher-load, multiset, twice weekly (SMD 0.66 vs control), but all prescriptions promoted hypertrophy comparably. ACSM 2026 concludes two sets are superior to one and advises at least two sets per exercise, with likely diminishing benefit beyond that.
**Grade:** strong
**Applies to:** Trained and untrained adults; the network meta-analysis included roughly half women.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** The benefit of extra sets is real but modest at group level. Krieger's individual dose comparisons (2-3 or 4-6 vs 1) were only trends (p=0.09 and p=0.096).
**Sources:** [S001], [S004], [S005]

### Diminishing returns, especially for strength
**Rule:** For a strength-focused or time-limited client, keep volume moderate (about 2-3 hard sets per main lift per session) and put effort into load, frequency and technique. Add sets mainly when muscle size is the goal and recovery allows.
**Evidence:** Pelland 2026 states that diminishing returns for strength are "considerably more pronounced" than for size. ACSM 2026, summarising that meta-regression, describes a plateau beyond about 2-3 sets per exercise for strength and about 18-20 weekly sets for hypertrophy. In an RCT of trained men (3x/week, 8 weeks), 1, 3 or 5 sets per exercise gave similar strength gains, while hypertrophy favoured higher volume at the elbow flexors, mid-thigh and lateral thigh but not significantly at the elbow extensors.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Mostly young males; the RCT used resistance-trained young men only (n=34). Plateau points are model estimates, not fixed thresholds.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** The exact inflection points come from one meta-regression as reported by ACSM and are imprecise. Strength is not completely flat with volume (see next rule).
**Sources:** [S001], [S003], [S006]

### Volume for strength goals
**Rule:** For strength goals, prescribe 2-3 working sets per main lift per session, with heavy loads (≥80% 1RM, about 8 reps or fewer) once technique is solid, and put the priority lift first in the session. Add volume only after load and frequency are in place.
**Evidence:** Ralston 2017 (9 studies, 61 groups) found high versus low weekly sets gave ES +0.18 (95% CI 0.06-0.30; ES 1.01 vs 0.82) and medium versus low ES +0.15. ACSM 2026 lists 2-3 sets per session, loads ≥80% 1RM (dose-response), full range of motion and doing the lift early in the session as enhancing strength.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Healthy adults; Ralston's data are mostly novice and intermediate men, ACSM's mostly novices.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** In tension with Schoenfeld 2019 (no strength difference across 1, 3 and 5 sets in trained men) and Pelland's strong diminishing returns. The volume effect on strength is small compared with load.
**Sources:** [S001], [S008]

### Trained lifters: 12-20 weekly sets
**Rule:** For an experienced recreational lifter who has plateaued, 12-20 weekly sets per prioritised muscle is a reasonable working range. Go above 20 only for one or two lagging muscles, for limited blocks, and only while recovery and joint comfort hold up.
**Evidence:** Baz-Valle 2022 (7 RCTs, 6 meta-analysed) found >20 weekly sets did not beat 12-20 sets for the quadriceps (p=0.19) or biceps (p=0.59); the triceps favoured higher volume (p=0.01). The authors suggested 12-20 weekly sets as a standard recommendation for this population.
**Grade:** emerging
**Applies to:** Men aged 18-35 with at least 1 year of training. Not for beginners or older adults; largely untested in women.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Small evidence base, results differ by muscle. Continuous dose-response models (Pelland 2026) show no hard ceiling in their abstracts. Some individuals respond more to higher volume.
**Sources:** [S007]

### Minimum effective dose
**Rule:** When a client is short on time or motivation, prescribe a minimal programme rather than nothing: 1 hard set of about 6-15 reps, close to failure, on 4-6 multi-joint exercises (push, pull, squat or leg press, hinge), aiming for 2 sessions per week. If only 1 session is realistic, it still beats nothing. Present it as effective for strength, then add sets and sessions if hypertrophy goals, time and adherence allow.
**Evidence:** A systematic review in resistance-trained men (6 studies, 5 pooled) found a single set of 6-12 reps at about 70-85% 1RM to failure, 2-3 times per week for 8-12 weeks, raised 1RM by 12.09 kg overall (95% CI 8.16-16.03), squat by 17.48 kg (8.51-26.46) and bench by 8.25 kg (0.68-15.83); the authors call this "suboptimal, yet significant". In an 8-week RCT in trained men, 1 set per exercise in three 13-minute sessions per week gave strength gains similar to 3 or 5 sets, but less hypertrophy. Nuzzo 2024 (overview, people not currently resistance training) found all minimal-dose strategies increase strength, with one weekly session and single-set training best supported and "exercise snacks" and eccentric-only minimal doses emerging. ACSM 2026 states minimal doses bring substantial gains and that individualising to increase participation matters more than meeting prescription criteria. WHO 2020 recommends ≥2 days per week for health.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** General adults. The 1RM meta-analysis and RCT cover resistance-trained men only (squat and bench); applicability to the deadlift, trained women and athletes is unclear. Hypertrophy with minimal doses is less certain than strength.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Minimal doses are suboptimal for hypertrophy, which shows a volume dose-response. Taking sets close to failure is part of what makes single sets work. Pooled estimates come from few small studies with wide CIs. Older adults may need more to maintain muscle (see "Maintenance dose").
**Sources:** [S001], [S006], [S009], [S016], [S061]

### Time-efficient floor
**Rule:** For a time-poor beginner, build sessions around a squat or leg press, an upper-body push and an upper-body pull. Aim for at least 4 hard weekly sets per muscle group as a practical floor. Supersets can roughly halve session time, mainly when the goal is hypertrophy.
**Evidence:** A narrative review on time-efficient training recommends at least 4 weekly sets per muscle group with a 6-15 RM load (or 15-40 reps if taken to volitional failure), prioritising bilateral multi-joint exercises with full dynamic movements, including at least one leg-pressing exercise, one upper-body pull and one upper-body push. It concludes weekly volume matters more than frequency, and notes supersets, drop sets and rest-pause roughly halve training time while maintaining volume.
**Grade:** emerging
**Applies to:** General adults seeking time-efficient training. Expert synthesis, not a pooled estimate.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** The 4-set floor is an author recommendation. Time-saving techniques are probably better for hypertrophy than strength, and longitudinal data are limited.
**Sources:** [S010]

### Do not sell set caps as fact
**Rule:** Do not present specific set caps, "junk volume" thresholds or required frequencies as scientific fact. Say that more volume tends to help growth with diminishing returns, and that the practical limit is individual recovery and schedule. If a single session is getting very long for one muscle (roughly 10 or more sets), splitting it across days is a sensible, low-cost hedge.
**Evidence:** ACSM 2026 notes its 2009 recommendation of 2-3 full-body sessions per week for novice hypertrophy was graded at the highest quality of evidence but rested on 3 studies (n=59), 2 of which did not test frequency. Pelland 2026 (67 studies, 2058 participants) reports rising hypertrophy and strength with weekly sets, with diminishing returns, and no weekly-set ceiling in its abstract. The only per-session cut-offs come from a non-peer-reviewed preprint by the same group: added sets stop showing a detectable benefit at about 2 direct sets per session for strength and about 11 fractional sets per muscle per session for hypertrophy, and its authors urge caution because data at very high per-session volumes are sparse.
**Grade:** emerging
**Applies to:** All populations for the general message. The curves come mostly from young men (79% male, mean age about 25).
**Individual variation / conflicts:** The preprint (Remmert et al., SportRxiv 537, posted 2025-04-02) was not found in PubMed as of 2026-10-24 and is not peer reviewed. The authors of both meta-regressions are fitness-industry coaches and writers (declared in Pelland 2026).
**Sources:** [S001], [S003], [S020]

### Individual response to volume
**Rule:** Treat volume targets as a starting point. If a client's strength or measurements stall over 8-12 weeks despite good adherence, nutrition and sleep, try a controlled increase in sets for the lagging muscles. If extra volume brings no progress and more fatigue, scale back.
**Evidence:** In a 12-week within-subject trial (34 untrained young adults, 16 men and 18 women), 3 sets per exercise gave more growth (CSA +5.2% vs +3.7%) and more strength than 1 set on average, but only 13 of 34 participants showed a clear hypertrophy benefit and 16 of 34 a clear strength benefit.
**Grade:** emerging
**Applies to:** Untrained young adults of both sexes; untested in trained or older people.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Single trial; "responder" classification depends on measurement error.
**Sources:** [S019]

## Counting sets

### Default counting rule: direct 1, indirect 0.5
**Rule:** Count a set as 1.0 for a muscle that is the main mover and 0.5 for a muscle that is a clear synergist. Example classifications (the coach's own, not from the paper): bench or overhead press for triceps and front delts, rows and pulldowns for biceps and rear delts. Use these fractional totals when checking the ≥10 sets per week target. Do not credit synergists with full sets (12 bench sets and 0 triceps sets is about 6 triceps sets, not 12), and do not tell a client their arms get zero work from pressing and pulling. Treat 0.5 as a starting estimate and lower it for muscles that other evidence shows get little stimulus from a lift.
**Evidence:** Pelland 2026 (67 studies, 2,058 participants, 79.1% male, mean age about 25) compared counting indirect sets as 1 ("total"), 0.5 ("fractional") or 0 ("direct"). Fractional had the strongest relative evidence for both outcomes. In preprint v2, for weekly volume and hypertrophy, fractional beat total with 2xLog(BF) = 9.48 and direct with 10.29; for frequency, 9.96 and 10.82; for volume and strength, 18.21 and 45.96. The paper's example: 5 sets of curls plus 5 sets of rows counts as 7.5 weekly biceps sets.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Mostly young men, untrained and trained, over a few weeks to months. A modelling choice by the analysts, not a measured constant.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** The 0.5 weight was assumed, not estimated, and only 0, 0.5 and 1 were compared. The authors say the direct/indirect classification "was not wholly objective" and that the method "suffers from the assumption that all indirect training should be quantified as half a set". The true weight very likely differs by muscle and exercise. This is a between-study model comparison, not a head-to-head experiment.
**Sources:** [S003], [S072]

### Guidelines are silent on counting
**Rule:** Treat the ≥10 sets per week guideline as silent on set counting. Pick the fractional rule, state it to the user, and apply it the same way every time. Never claim that ACSM endorses counting compound sets 1:1 or 0.5.
**Evidence:** ACSM 2026 concludes hypertrophy is enhanced by ≥10 sets per muscle group per week but does not define how multi-joint sets count toward synergists, and states there were "insufficient data to determine if muscle hypertrophy was influenced by ... single-joint versus multijoint RT".
**Grade:** strong
**Applies to:** Healthy adults ≥18; the position stand covers RCTs of ≥6 weeks.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** The strong grade covers only the fact that the guideline gives no rule. The meta-analyses behind the ≥10 sets target counted sets in different ways, so its exact meaning is uncertain.
**Sources:** [S001]

### Earlier 1:1 advice versus the newer 0.5 rule
**Rule:** Use 0.5 as the default and say it is current best practice, not settled science. Where a user or another source counts 1:1, explain the difference instead of calling it wrong. For synergists with clearly reduced activation in a lift (hamstrings in squats or leg press, rectus femoris in leg press or squat), count at most 0.5, do not rely on those lifts for that muscle's weekly target, and program direct work instead.
**Evidence:** A 2019 evidence review (Schoenfeld, Grgic, Haun, Itagaki, Helms) argued multi-joint sets should count fractionally for some muscles, citing moment arms, motor abundance and EMG: hamstring EMG about twofold lower than quadriceps in squats and leg press; rectus femoris EMG about 39% MVC in leg press and 27% in squat versus 68% in leg extension; pectoralis major EMG about twofold higher than triceps in bench press. Because longitudinal evidence was limited to the elbow flexors and "somewhat conflicting", it provisionally recommended counting 1:1 with professional judgement. Pelland 2026 now supports 0.5 on average.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Healthy adults; the EMG data are acute and the longitudinal data come from young adults.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Explicit disagreement between expert positions. The 2019 paper also said a 1:1 ratio "cannot be assumed" and that the contribution "likely depends on the individual and the technical execution". Acute EMG does not translate directly into a set weight.
**Sources:** [S003], [S073]

### Pressing: chest 1, triceps and front delts 0.5
**Rule:** Count bench and other horizontal presses as 1 set for chest and 0.5 for triceps and front delts, and 0 for the side delts and biceps. A pressing-only program should not be assumed to train the side delts.
**Evidence:** In a 10-week MRI trial (24 healthy men; bench group n=13, 3x/week, 3-4 sets of 12 at 50-55% 1RM), the bench group gained more CSA than controls in pectoralis major, pectoralis minor, anterior deltoid and triceps; pec major grew more than triceps and pec minor, and the medial deltoid grew least and did not differ from controls. In a 24-week ultrasound study (7 untrained men, bench 3x10 at 75% 1RM, 3x/week) pec major thickness rose from week 1 and triceps from week 5, while biceps did not change. The 2019 review cites about twofold higher pec than triceps EMG.
**Grade:** emerging
**Applies to:** Young men in small samples; generalisation to women, older adults and well-trained lifters is unverified.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** No trial gives a precise triceps stimulus per bench set; 0.5 is borrowed from the pooled model. The fraction likely depends on technique such as grip width and elbow path.
**Sources:** [S073], [S074], [S075]

### Compounds miss the triceps long head and rectus femoris
**Rule:** Treat compound pressing as a poor stimulus for the triceps long head. Count leg press and squat as 1 for the vasti and about 0 for the rectus femoris. When a user wants full arm or thigh development, or a head lags, add an overhead or lying triceps extension, or a leg extension.
**Evidence:** In Brandão 2020 (43 young men, MRI) the triceps long head grew more in groups doing lying extensions than in the bench-only group, the lateral head grew more in groups doing bench, and total triceps CSA did not differ between groups. In a 12-week within-subject MRI trial (17 untrained adults, 11 men, 6 women) the rectus femoris grew +13.2% with knee extension but +1.1% with leg press (non-significant, P=0.379), while the vasti and whole quadriceps grew similarly. Full or half squats (Kubo 2019, 17 men, 10 weeks) produced no rectus femoris change.
**Grade:** emerging
**Applies to:** Young adults over 10-12 weeks, MRI measures; no data in older adults. Whether this matters for health-focused clients is a matter of preference.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Single small trials, though the rectus femoris finding is consistent across leg press and squat. Rosa 2023 found similar whole-muscle hypertrophy for single- and multi-joint training and noted single-joint work "may" preferentially grow subdivisions.
**Sources:** [S076], [S077], [S078]

### Squats, leg press and hip thrusts barely train the hamstrings
**Rule:** Count squats, leg press and hip thrusts as about 0 (at most 0.25) toward the hamstrings and prescribe direct knee-flexion work (leg curl) and/or hip-hinge work (for example an RDL). Count deep squats and leg press as at least 0.5, and plausibly closer to 1, for gluteus maximus and adductors; count half squats lower for the glutes.
**Evidence:** Hamstring volume did not change after 10 weeks of full or half squats (Kubo 2019, 17 men). Nine weeks of hip thrusts or squats gave "little to no" hamstring growth (34 untrained young adults). Leg press gave a small significant hamstring increase (+3.1%), far below the 12-19% reported with leg curls, and the authors say it should not be a primary hamstring exercise. Full squats grew gluteus maximus +6.7% and adductors +6.2% (half squats +2.2% and +2.7%); leg press grew gluteus maximus +15.4% and adductor magnus +6.2%; hip thrusts and squats gave similar gluteal growth.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Young adults over 9-12 weeks; no data in older adults or trained lifters; lunges not studied.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Consistent direction across three small MRI trials. Glute effects vary widely (2.2-15.4%) with depth and exercise. The RDL recommendation is not tested in these sources.
**Sources:** [S077], [S078], [S079]

### Rows and pulldowns for the biceps
**Rule:** Count rows and pulldowns as 0.5 biceps sets. If a user with arm goals has no direct curls, expect real but probably slower biceps growth, and add 2-4 weekly sets of curls if time allows.
**Evidence:** In a within-subject trial (10 untrained men, 8 weeks), elbow-flexor thickness grew +11.06% with curls versus +5.16% with unilateral dumbbell rows (p=0.009). In Gentil 2015 (29 untrained men, 10 weeks), lat pulldown only grew elbow-flexor thickness +6.10% versus +5.83% for curls only, with no difference.
**Grade:** emerging
**Applies to:** Untrained young men, 8-10 weeks, ultrasound at one site.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Directly conflicting results, possibly due to exercise, design or lab. Gentil 2015 comes from a group with several retracted papers on single- versus multi-joint training, so give it less weight.
**Sources:** [S080], [S081]

### Isolation work is optional for most clients
**Rule:** Tell users that compound-only programs build the arms meaningfully and that isolation adds at most a modest extra amount of whole-muscle size in the short term. For a beginner with 2-3 sessions of about 45 minutes per week, build the program from compounds covering push, pull, squat and hinge. Add isolation only once compound volume is met and time allows: first a leg curl, then (coaching judgement, not tested here) lateral raises for the side delts; arm isolation is optional. Recommend isolation for users who care about arm size or a lagging head. Never drop compounds for isolation-only training.
**Evidence:** Rosa 2023 (7 studies, 10 comparisons) found a trivial pooled difference in whole-muscle hypertrophy between single- and multi-joint training, unchanged when comparing multi-joint plus single-joint with multi-joint alone. Adding curls and extensions to bench and pulldown gave elbow-flexor thickness +7.04% versus +6.5% (29 untrained men, 10 weeks); in trained men arm circumference rose +3.17% versus +1.33% (not significant). One trial in untrained men found a larger arm-circumference gain with added isolation (+5.2% vs +4.0%). A review of 23 studies concluded people "may not need to include SJ exercises" for equivalent results, except for the lumbar extensors and imbalances. A 6-month trial in trained women found multi-joint groups gained more gluteus maximus, quadriceps and pectoralis thickness than a single-joint-only group, while single-joint work gave better leg-curl strength gains.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Healthy young adults, mostly men, untrained and trained, 8 weeks to 6 months; arm data measured by ultrasound or circumference. Evidence in older adults is lacking.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Most primary trials and the 23-study review come from one group (Gentil/Barbalho); PubMed lists four of its papers as retracted (two on adding single-joint work) and a 2026 expression of concern covers another; Gentil 2013 and 2015 may share samples; the Barbalho 2021 trial carries an erratum. The independent Rosa 2023 meta-analysis agrees but is small. The "compounds are enough" literature did not address hamstrings, rectus femoris or side delts.
**Sources:** [S081], [S082], [S083], [S084], [S085], [S086], [S087], [S088]

### Strength is specific to the lift trained
**Rule:** For a strength target on a specific lift, count sets of that exact lift as 1 and related lifts training the same muscles as about 0.5. Do not expect isolation work to move the main lift much. To get better at a lift, train that lift.
**Evidence:** In a row-versus-curl study (10 untrained men, 8 weeks) 10RM rose more in the exercise each arm trained. In a 9-week squat-versus-hip-thrust RCT (untrained college-aged adults), 3RM gains favoured the trained lift (squat +14 ± 2 kg for the squat group; hip thrust 26 ± 5 kg more for the hip-thrust group), while deadlift improved similarly. In Pelland, where "direct" for strength meant the exact tested exercise, fractional counting fitted strength much better than total (2xLog(BF) = 18.21) or direct (45.96).
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Mainly young adults in short trials; extrapolation to older adults and long-term trainees.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** The 0.5 weight was not estimated from data. Schoenfeld 2019 recommends 1:1 by default. Transfer probably varies by exercise and skill.
**Sources:** [S072], [S079], [S080]

### Counting rule outside young men
**Rule:** For adults over about 60, women and long-term trainees, apply the same 0.5 counting rule as a working default and say plainly it is extrapolated from young, mostly male samples. Adjust volume based on observed progress, not set arithmetic alone.
**Evidence:** Pelland pooled 2058 participants, 79.1% male, average age 25.16 ± 5.22. The 2019 review found longitudinal multi- versus single-joint research limited to the elbow flexors and "somewhat conflicting". Neither source has data on counting sets for older adults.
**Grade:** emerging
**Applies to:** Older adults, women and long-term trained lifters.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Schoenfeld 2019 recommends 1:1 by default. Older adults may differ in recovery and synergist contribution, but there are no data either way.
**Sources:** [S003], [S073]

## Frequency and splits

### Frequency for hypertrophy
**Rule:** Choose how often to train each muscle by schedule, recovery and preference. Use frequency to fit in the weekly volume, not as a goal in itself. Splitting volume across 2 or more sessions is practical once one session would need too many sets for one muscle.
**Evidence:** Schoenfeld 2019 (25 studies) found no significant difference between higher and lower frequencies when volume was equated, including in trained people and upper- and lower-body subgroups. ACSM 2026 found no difference between 1 day per week and more than 5 days per week when volume was equated. Pelland 2026 found frequency's effect on hypertrophy "compatible with negligible effects". Without volume equation, higher frequency helped modestly because it adds volume.
**Grade:** strong
**Applies to:** Healthy adults, trained and untrained; mostly young adults.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** An earlier meta-analysis (Schoenfeld 2016) found twice weekly beat once weekly (ES 0.49 vs 0.30); the larger later analyses do not support an independent frequency effect.
**Sources:** [S001], [S003], [S011]

### Frequency for strength
**Rule:** For strength goals, practise each main lift at least twice a week. Three or more exposures is a reasonable option for intermediate lifters, especially in upper-body compound lifts, but do not present it as necessary.
**Evidence:** Grgic 2018 (22 studies) found effect sizes of 0.74, 0.82, 0.93 and 1.08 for 1, 2, 3 and 4+ sessions per week, but no significant effect in volume-equated studies. Pelland 2026 found strength rising with frequency (posterior probability 100%) with diminishing returns. ACSM 2026 recommends ≥2 sessions per week for strength, with insufficient evidence for a dose-response and a smaller effect when volume is equated.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Mostly untrained young adults. Grgic's subgroups suggest a larger effect in women and young adults and a non-significant effect in middle-aged and older adults (p=0.093).
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Disagreement on whether frequency acts independently of volume (Grgic null when equated, Pelland positive slope). A skill-practice benefit is plausible but not isolated.
**Sources:** [S001], [S003], [S012]

### Full-body versus split
**Rule:** Let the client pick full-body, upper/lower or another split based on available days and enjoyment. For 2-3 days per week, full-body sessions are the simplest way to hit each muscle twice. Check that weekly sets per muscle are covered whichever split is chosen.
**Evidence:** Ramos-Campo 2024 (14 studies, n=392) found no significant volume-equated differences between split and full-body routines in bench press (MD 1.19 [-1.28, 3.65]), lower-limb strength (MD 2.47 [-2.11, 7.05]), elbow extensor size (MD 0.30 [-2.65, 3.24]), elbow flexor size (MD 0.17 [-2.54, 2.88]), vastus lateralis size (MD -0.08 [-1.82, 1.66]) or lean mass (MD -0.07 [-1.59, 1.44]). In an RCT of 67 untrained adults (8 weeks), each muscle trained 2x per week (split) or 4x per week (full body) at equal weekly sets gave no differences in strength or muscle thickness.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Healthy adults, trained and untrained; mostly 8-12 week studies in young adults; limited evidence in older adults.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Carneiro 2024 (23 well-trained males, 75 sets per week) found more fat-mass loss with full-body than a 1x per week split; a different outcome and small study, not a reason to override preference. Two Evangelista 2021 authors declared selling clinics and workshops on the topic.
**Sources:** [S013], [S014], [S015]

## Load and effort

### Load range for hypertrophy
**Rule:** For a hypertrophy or body-composition goal, let the client choose loads anywhere from about 6 to 30 reps per set based on preference, joint comfort and equipment. Default to moderate loads (about 6-15 reps). Light loads work only if the set ends close to failure.
**Evidence:** Schoenfeld 2017 (21 studies, sets to failure) found similar hypertrophy for loads ≤60% and >60% 1RM. Lopez 2021 (network meta-analysis, 28 studies, n=747, to failure) found no difference between low (>15RM), moderate (9-15RM) and high (≤8RM) loads (P=0.113-0.469). Currier 2023 (119 studies, n=3364) found all prescriptions comparably promoted hypertrophy. ACSM 2026 concludes loads from 30% to 100% 1RM did not differ for hypertrophy.
**Grade:** strong
**Applies to:** Healthy adults 18+, mostly young and untrained or recreationally trained; women 47% of the network meta-analysis. The equivalence may not hold for low-load sets stopped far from failure.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Equivalence assumes high effort; very light loads (>25-30 reps) are more uncomfortable and harder to judge. In older adults (Csapo 2016, mean age 68) heavier (~80% 1RM) loads gave only marginally larger size gains than ~45% 1RM (mu 0.056-0.136). Lopez 2021 has a published erratum (MSSE 2022;54(2):370).
**Sources:** [S001], [S005], [S021], [S022], [S023]

### Load range for maximal strength
**Rule:** If the client wants to get stronger, include regular heavier work in the 3-8 rep range (about 75-85%+ 1RM) on main lifts once technique is solid. Beginners make large gains with moderate loads at first; introduce heavier sets gradually as technique becomes consistent (a coaching choice, often after the first several weeks, not an evidence-derived threshold).
**Evidence:** ACSM 2026 lists intensity ≥80% 1RM (dose-response) as enhancing strength. Lopez 2021 found high (≤8RM) and moderate (9-15RM) loads superior to low loads for strength (SMD 0.60-0.63 and 0.34-0.35), with a non-significant edge for high over moderate (SMD 0.26-0.28, P=0.068). Schoenfeld 2017 found greater 1RM gains above 60% 1RM, while isometric strength did not differ. Currier 2023 (178 strength studies, n=5097, 45% women) found higher-load (>80% 1RM) prescriptions maximised strength, with higher-load, multi-set, 3x per week ranked highest (SMD 1.60, 95% CrI 1.38-1.82 vs control).
**Grade:** strong
**Applies to:** Healthy adults, mostly young.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Part of the heavy-load advantage is skill specificity. For health-oriented clients the extra 1RM gain may matter less than adherence and joint comfort.
**Sources:** [S001], [S005], [S021], [S022]

### Training to failure is not required
**Rule:** Do not prescribe routine training to failure. Make "hard but not to failure" the default. Failure is optional, at most on the last set of safe exercises (machines, isolation work) for clients who enjoy it and have good technique. Avoid failure in older or deconditioned clients.
**Evidence:** Grgic 2022 (15 studies, young adults) found no difference for strength (ES -0.09, 95% CI -0.22 to 0.05) or hypertrophy (ES 0.22, -0.11 to 0.55). Refalo 2023 found no hypertrophy advantage of momentary failure (ES 0.12, -0.13 to 0.37). Wu 2026 (20 RCTs, n=556) found non-failure equal for hypertrophy, endurance and power and slightly better for dynamic strength (SMD 0.24, 0.06-0.42). ACSM 2026: completing sets to momentary failure "does not enhance gains in strength, hypertrophy, and power, and so is not necessary".
**Grade:** strong
**Applies to:** Healthy adults; trials almost all in young adults, many trained men; few data in older adults.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** A small possible hypertrophy edge for training closer to failure in trained people (Grgic trained subgroup ES 0.15, 0.03-0.26; Refalo any-definition ES 0.19, 0.00-0.37); trivial for the target population.
**Sources:** [S001], [S024], [S025], [S026]

### Target reps in reserve
**Rule:** Prescribe most working sets at about 1-3 RIR for hypertrophy and 1-4 RIR for strength-focused heavy sets. Warm-up and technique sets do not count as working sets. A set ended with 5+ RIR is probably too easy for hypertrophy.
**Evidence:** ACSM 2026 proposes sufficient effort can come from near-failure sets or a target of 2-3 RIR. Robinson 2024 (exploratory meta-regressions with estimated RIR) found hypertrophy increased as sets ended closer to failure, while strength was similar across a wide RIR range. Refalo 2023 suggested going from "close" to absolute failure adds little.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Healthy adults; RIR was estimated, not measured, in mostly young samples.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** The curve may flatten near 0 RIR; the authors call the exact RIR-hypertrophy relationship unclear.
**Sources:** [S001], [S025], [S027]

### Costs of failure
**Rule:** Use RIR rather than failure, especially on heavy multi-joint lifts (squat, deadlift, bench) and when the muscle is trained again within 48 h. Avoid failure early on with adherence-sensitive beginners, because lower discomfort helps them keep training.
**Evidence:** In 10 trained men, 3x10 to failure caused larger acute drops in jump height and lifting velocity than 6x5 with the same load, and recovery was slower up to 24-48 h. In an 8-week within-subject trial (n=18 trained), failure produced more velocity and rep loss, higher discomfort (RPD 5.1 vs 4.1) and session RPE (5.4 vs 4.3), and less positive feelings (0.3 vs 1.2) than 2-RIR/1-RIR, with similar quadriceps hypertrophy.
**Grade:** emerging
**Applies to:** Small samples of trained young adults; generalisation to novices is plausible but untested.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Fatigue resistance may improve over time (Refalo 2024). Some clients enjoy failure training.
**Sources:** [S028], [S029], [S030]

### Effort limits for older adults and cardiovascular risk
**Rule:** For older beginners (over about 60) and anyone with hypertension or cardiovascular risk, start at about 2-3 RIR (about 13-15 on the Borg 6-20 scale), avoid training to failure, stop a set when form breaks down, and avoid breath-holding (Valsalva). Older adults may progress to about 1-2 RIR after about 8 weeks without symptoms. Steer load by RIR or RPE, not by %1RM, because the coach has no measured 1RM. Never take free-weight compound lifts to failure without a spotter or safety pins. For blood-pressure thresholds that stop or limit training, follow the bands in recovery-habits.md. The age cut-off, RIR numbers, Borg mapping and 8-week step are practical conventions, not trial-derived.
**Evidence:** ACSM 2026 states failure is not necessary and adds, hedged: "It may also be that lifting to fatigue is inadvisable for certain populations (e.g., older individuals) due to risks to vascular health and an increased risk of injury resulting from poor form." It also states resistance training is safe for healthy adults of all ages. Grgic 2022 reports all included failure studies were in young adults. ICFSR 2021 advises avoiding Valsalva and breath-holding to minimise haemodynamic excursions (listed for stroke) and always avoiding concentric failure in power training.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Older adults and people with hypertension or cardiovascular risk. Precautionary expert reasoning, not trial data showing harm.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** No direct RCT evidence of harm from failure in older adults; the ACSM wording is tentative. ICFSR targets Borg 15-18 ("hard to very hard") with supervision and a measured 1RM; this coach starts lower and works unsupervised, so it uses the more conservative cap.
**Sources:** [S001], [S024], [S110]

### RIR accuracy
**Rule:** Treat client-reported RIR as roughly ±1-2 reps. Expect people to stop with more in reserve than they think, especially in high-rep sets. Use RIR targets with moderate loads (about 5-12 reps), where judgement is better, and rely more on later-set ratings.
**Evidence:** Halperin 2022 (12 studies, 414 participants) found people underpredicted reps to failure by 0.95 reps on average (95% CI 0.17-1.73), with very large heterogeneity (I2 97.9%). Accuracy improved slightly closer to failure and trivially in later sets, was much better in sets of ≤12 reps (β 0.06) than >12 (β 0.47), and did not depend on training status (β -0.006). Remmert 2023 (58 young adults, machines at 72.5% 1RM) found the same pattern, unaffected by sex or experience.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Healthy adults, mostly young; trained and untrained.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Training status not predicting accuracy runs against the belief that only experienced lifters can use RIR. What error is "acceptable" is unclear.
**Sources:** [S031], [S032]

### Calibrating clients to RIR
**Rule:** Teach the RIR/RPE scale explicitly. Periodically calibrate with an occasional set to technical failure on a safe exercise (machine, isolation, or with safeties). Ask for an RIR rating after each working set and adjust the next set by about 2-5% (or ±1-2 reps) when the rating misses the target.
**Evidence:** A scoping review (31 studies, N=855, mostly men) found RIR scales feasible for prescribing and adjusting load, with predictions best made close to failure. The RIR-based RPE scale correlated with bar velocity in experienced (r=-0.88) and novice squatters (r=-0.77; 29 people). In an 8-week RCT (42 trained young adults), estimation was more accurate for bench than squat and improved over time.
**Grade:** emerging
**Applies to:** Healthy adults; calibration via failure sets and the 2-5% adjustment are practical inferences.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Accuracy differs by exercise; the benefit of calibration in novices is extrapolated.
**Sources:** [S033], [S034], [S035]

### Self-selected loads are often too light
**Rule:** Do not let "pick a weight that feels right" be the prescription. Give a rep range plus an RIR target, progress load when the top of the range is reached at the target RIR, and check that sets are genuinely hard.
**Evidence:** Trainees self-select about 53% 1RM on average (95% CrI 49-58%; 18 studies, 368 participants), with little moderation by experience, age or sex, and somewhat heavier loads when fewer reps are prescribed. The authors judge this can suffice for novices with 5-15 reps, may not for advanced trainees, and with high reps near failure works for hypertrophy but less for maximal strength.
**Grade:** emerging
**Applies to:** Healthy adults of varied experience; mostly single sessions with vague instructions.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Exploratory meta-analysis; clearer instructions may change self-selection.
**Sources:** [S036]

### Autoregulation
**Rule:** Use RIR/RPE as a practical tool for choosing loads and handling day-to-day readiness, but do not present it as superior to a sensible fixed plan. For hypertrophy, let sets go reasonably close to failure; for strength, stopping further from failure is enough. Teach RIR gradually to beginners.
**Evidence:** In trained individuals, autoregulated (RIR-based RPE or velocity) and fixed percentage prescription gave similar 1RM gains (MD 2.07 kg, 95% CI -0.32 to 4.46; 6 studies). With volume autoregulation, velocity-loss thresholds ≤25% gave greater 1RM gains (MD 2.32 kg) but less hypertrophy than >25%; the authors conclude thresholds above 20-25% favour hypertrophy. ACSM 2026 found insufficient data on intrasession autoregulation.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Trained, mostly young adults; untested in novices and older adults.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Few studies, imprecise estimates; transferring velocity-loss findings to RIR is an inference.
**Sources:** [S001], [S060]

## Rest and tempo

### Rest intervals for hypertrophy
**Rule:** Default to about 1.5-3 min between sets for hypertrophy, longer on heavy compound lifts. Shorter rests (60 s or supersets) are acceptable when time is limited, but add a set or accept slightly fewer reps. Rest long enough that the next set's reps do not collapse.
**Evidence:** Singer 2024 (Bayesian, 9 studies) suggests a small benefit for rests >60 s: arm 0.13 (95% CrI -0.27 to 0.51), thigh 0.17 (-0.13 to 0.43), whole body -0.08 (-0.45 to 0.29), with no appreciable difference beyond 90 s. Grgic 2017 (6 studies) found both short (≤60 s) and long (>60 s) rest produce hypertrophy, with a possible advantage for long rest in trained people. ACSM 2026 found insufficient data for hypertrophy. ACSM 2009 recommended 1-2 min for hypertrophy.
**Grade:** emerging
**Applies to:** Healthy adults in small studies, mostly young; all credible intervals include zero.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** ACSM 2009 (1-2 min) and newer data leaning to >60-90 s differ. Effects are small, so time-poor clients can use short rest with little loss if they keep volume up.
**Sources:** [S001], [S037], [S038], [S039]

### Rest intervals for strength
**Rule:** For beginners, 1-2 min between sets is fine. For trained clients doing heavy sets (≤6 reps) on main lifts, allow 2-3+ min. Use shorter rests on accessory work to save time.
**Evidence:** Grgic 2018 (23 studies, 491 participants, 413 male) found robust strength gains even with <60 s rest; >2 min seemed needed to maximise strength in trained people, while 60-120 s sufficed for untrained people. ACSM 2026 found strength unaffected by <1 min versus >1 min. ACSM 2009 recommended 3-5 min for heavy loading in intermediate and advanced lifters.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Healthy adults, predominantly men; systematic review without pooling.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** ACSM 2026 and Grgic 2018 differ in emphasis; the difference likely matters mostly for advanced lifters.
**Sources:** [S001], [S039], [S040]

### Tempo and time under tension
**Rule:** Do not prescribe special slow tempos or time-under-tension targets for growth. Cue a controlled lowering (about 1-3 s) and a purposeful, fast-as-practical lift through full range of motion. Avoid super-slow (>10 s) reps.
**Evidence:** Enes 2025 (14 studies, Bayesian) compared faster (0.3-2 s per rep) and slower (1.7-4.5 s) tempos: within-group gains ES 0.43 vs 0.34, pooled difference trivially favouring faster (0.09, 95% CrI -0.04 to 0.22). Schoenfeld 2015 (8 studies, to failure) found similar hypertrophy from 0.5 to 8 s per rep and suggested >10 s may be inferior. ACSM 2026: hypertrophy was not influenced by fast versus slow reps, and time under tension did not consistently affect outcomes.
**Grade:** strong
**Applies to:** Healthy adults of any training status; individual trials are small.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** The >10 s finding is tentative. Slower tempos still have a place for technique learning, joint tolerance or rehab. ACSM 2026 reports power training (fast concentric) enhanced physical function, relevant for older adults.
**Sources:** [S001], [S041], [S042]

## Exercise selection and range of motion

### Free weights versus machines
**Rule:** Choose equipment by preference, access, safety and skill, not by a belief that one builds more muscle. Machines are an excellent default for novices, older adults and anyone wanting low-skill, easily loaded progression. If a goal is tied to a free-weight lift, include that lift because strength is specific.
**Evidence:** Haugen 2023 (13 studies, n=1016) and Heidel 2022 found no hypertrophy difference (SMD -0.055, CI -0.397 to 0.287; ES -0.01). Strength gains were specific to the mode trained (Haugen SMD 0.21, Heidel ES 0.66 on free-weight tests) and similar on neutral tests. ACSM 2026 found mode did not affect strength, with insufficient data for hypertrophy. In older adults (network meta-analysis, 102 trials, n=4754, mean age 70) every modality worked: machines SMD 1.34 (smallest variance), free weights 1.15, bands 0.93, mixed 0.88, bodyweight 0.71.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Adults 18-60 (Haugen, mostly men); the older-adult analysis covers ages 63-87 and strength only.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** The hypertrophy comparison rests on few studies (5 in Haugen) and ACSM rated it insufficient. Preference and injury history should dominate.
**Sources:** [S001], [S048], [S049], [S050]

### Full range of motion by default
**Rule:** Default to full, controlled range of motion on every exercise. If a client cannot use full range (mobility, pain), keep the part where the muscle is stretched (the bottom of a squat, curl or press) rather than the short top half. Avoid top-half partials as a main stimulus.
**Evidence:** Pallarés 2021 (16 studies) found full ROM gave more strength (ES 0.56) and more lower-limb hypertrophy (ES 0.88) than partial ROM. ACSM 2026 lists full ROM as enhancing strength, with insufficient data for hypertrophy. Kassiano 2023 (11 studies) found full ROM and initial (long-length) partials produced more growth than final (short-length) partials (ES 0.20-0.90), and at a few sites initial or mid-range partials outperformed full ROM (proximal rectus femoris ES 0.35-0.38; triceps ES 1.21).
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Healthy adults, mostly young and untrained; limited muscles studied.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Pallarés pooled long- and short-length partials; Kassiano shows short-length partials drive the deficit. Kassiano is a systematic review without pooling.
**Sources:** [S001], [S051], [S052]

### Lengthened partials are an option, not an upgrade
**Rule:** Use lengthened partials when full range is uncomfortable or impractical, or as end-of-set extensions. Do not replace full-range training with them on the promise of extra growth. Whatever the range, load the muscle in the stretched position.
**Evidence:** A pre-registered 15-site cluster RCT (Gschneidner 2025; n=163 vs 134, 12 weeks) found interactions of -0.032 (arm CSA) and 0 (thigh CSA), "small and practically equivalent", though formal equivalence did not reach their strict alpha. Wolf 2025 (n=30 trained, 8 weeks) found similar arm thickness and 10RM changes (Bayes factors 0.16-0.30). Plotkin 2026 (16 trained men, MRI) found a difference of -1.4 cm² (95% CI -6.1 to 3.8). McMahon 2026 (n=45) found similar thickness gains for lengthened partials at 55% 1RM and full ROM at 80% 1RM. Varovic 2025 (12 studies) found only trivial differences between training at longer and shorter muscle lengths (SMD 0.05-0.09).
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Mostly young, trained men; no direct data in older adults and few in novices.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** The trials show similarity, not superiority. Wolf 2026 (narrative systematic review, 8 studies) suggests longer muscle lengths may give more growth than shorter ones, but calls the evidence mixed, and that is not a comparison with full ROM. McMahon found greater fascicle-length gains with lengthened partials, an architecture change, not extra size.
**Sources:** [S053], [S054], [S055], [S056], [S057], [S058]

### Exercise variation
**Rule:** Keep a stable core of exercises for blocks of several weeks (6-12 weeks is a practical heuristic) so progress is measurable and skill builds. Change exercises deliberately, for example to train a muscle at a different length or angle, or to work around discomfort. Avoid "muscle confusion" (new exercises every session), including as a plateau fix.
**Evidence:** A systematic review (8 studies, N=241, all young men) found systematic variation may improve regional hypertrophy and dynamic strength, while excessive random rotation or redundant exercises may compromise gains. ACSM 2026 found insufficient data on varied exercise selection.
**Grade:** emerging
**Applies to:** Evidence only in young men.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Enjoyment and adherence may justify more variety for some clients.
**Sources:** [S001], [S059]

## Progression and periodisation

### Starting point for novices
**Rule:** Start beginners at about 8-12 reps per set, 2-3 RIR, 2-3 sessions per week. Once technique is solid, include some heavier work (≥80% 1RM, about 6-8 reps or fewer) on main lifts if strength is a goal.
**Evidence:** ACSM 2009 recommends novices use 8-12 RM loads 2-3 days per week. ACSM 2026 supports a much broader range of programmes for novices through experienced trainees: strength is enhanced by ≥80% 1RM, full ROM, 2-3 sets and ≥2 sessions per week; hypertrophy by ≥10 weekly sets, with load (30-100% 1RM) not affecting it.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Healthy adult novices.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** The 2026 stand downplays rigid prescriptions and says the 2009 recommendations may be less relevant for most adults.
**Sources:** [S001], [S039]

### Double progression
**Rule:** Use double progression. Pick a rep range (for example 8-12). When the client beats the top of the range by 1-2 reps on all work sets at the target RIR, add the smallest practical increment within about 2-10% (smaller jumps for upper-body and isolation lifts, larger for big lower-body lifts). Adding reps or sets at the same load is equally valid when the next jump is too big. Training each lift at least twice a week is enough; intermediates do not need 3-4 days. For content health-focused clients, holding a challenging routine steady is acceptable; do not frame constant load increases as mandatory.
**Evidence:** ACSM 2009 recommends adding 2-10% when the person can do 1-2 reps over the target, and 3-4 days per week for intermediates. ACSM 2026 states progression is not necessary to benefit; increasing the stimulus is needed for continued long-term progress, and relative load can be kept by testing or perceived-exertion scales. In an 8-week RCT in trained adults (n=43, lower body), progressing reps or load gave similar hypertrophy and strength (strength slightly favoured load by 2.0 kg, 90% CI -2.4 to 7.8).
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Healthy adults, novice to trained; much of ACSM 2026's evidence comes from inexperienced trainees.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** The 2-10% rule is 2009 expert consensus, not trial-derived. The 2009 frequency tiers are not supported as necessary by the 2026 overview. Rate of progression varies widely; older and smaller people need smaller absolute jumps.
**Sources:** [S001], [S039], [S043]

### When progress stalls
**Rule:** Treat a lift as stalled when load or reps have not increased for about 3 consecutive sessions or weeks despite good sleep, food and attendance (a heuristic). For hypertrophy stalls, first check that work sets are genuinely hard (failure not required) and that the muscle gets at least 10 hard weekly sets, then try a modest increase of about 10-20% over current weekly sets for the lagging muscle, spread across sessions, rather than jumping to a generic high-volume template. For strength stalls, add heavy work (≥80% 1RM) through full range and keep each lift at least twice a week. Check effort, volume and recovery before changing exercises.
**Evidence:** ACSM 2026 found few variables matter much: ≥10 weekly sets enhance hypertrophy, with diminishing returns beyond about 18-20 in one meta-regression; ≥80% 1RM, full ROM, 2-3 sets and ≥2 sessions per week enhance strength; failure did not consistently change outcomes. In a small within-subject trial in trained people (n=16), 1.2 times their own logged weekly sets gave more vastus lateralis growth over 8 weeks than a standard 22 sets per week (MD 1.08 cm², 95% CI 0.04-2.11; ES 0.75).
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Healthy adults; the individualised-volume trial was small and in trained people; Benito's meta-analysis included only healthy adult men.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** More volume is not always better; Benito 2020 found a negative interaction with sets per workout. Scarpelli's CI lower bound is close to zero.
**Sources:** [S001], [S132], [S133]

### Periodisation for hypertrophy
**Rule:** For body-composition or health goals, do not sell a periodisation model as a muscle-building advantage. A simple progressive plan with adequate weekly sets is enough. Use planned variation only for adherence, fatigue management or scheduling.
**Evidence:** With volume equated, periodisation did not add growth (Moesgaard 2022, 35 studies: ES 0.13, 95% CI -0.10 to 0.36). Linear and undulating models gave similar hypertrophy (Moesgaard ES 0.05; Grgic 2017, 13 studies, d -0.02). ACSM 2026 found periodisation did not influence hypertrophy and is "less important than previously hypothesized" with appropriate progressive overload.
**Grade:** strong
**Applies to:** Healthy adults, mostly young, many untrained.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Definitions of periodisation vary between studies.
**Sources:** [S001], [S044], [S045]

### Periodisation for maximal strength
**Rule:** For beginners and returning lifters, use simple linear progression and do not spend effort choosing a model. For a trained recreational lifter who explicitly wants a bigger squat, bench or deadlift, rotating heavy, moderate and light days or weeks (undulating) is a reasonable low-cost option; present it as a modest, uncertain benefit.
**Evidence:** Williams 2017 (18 studies, ES 0.43, 95% CI 0.27-0.58) and Moesgaard 2022 (volume-equated, ES 0.31, 0.04-0.57) favour periodised over non-periodised training for 1RM. Undulating beat linear in Moesgaard (ES 0.31) only in trained participants (ES 0.61, 0.00-1.22), not untrained (ES 0.06). Harries 2015 (17 studies) found no linear versus undulating difference. ACSM 2026 says the impact on strength "could not be determined".
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Healthy adults; mostly short trials in young men.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Effects are small to moderate with CIs near zero. Williams did not require volume equation and found larger gains in untrained people, unlike Moesgaard.
**Sources:** [S001], [S044], [S046], [S047]

### Deloads
**Rule:** Do not prescribe routine deloads as a way to boost gains. Offer a reactive deload of about 1 week when two or more of these persist together for 1-2 weeks: performance stalls or drops on several lifts, persistent joint aches or soreness, poor sleep, low motivation. Keep the main exercises and reduce stress rather than stopping: as a coaching convention, cut volume (sets) by about 30-50% for one week, keep the loads, and stop 3 or more reps short of failure. If the signs look like overreaching (performance dropping despite rest, plus poor sleep or mood), use the same deload plus 1-3 extra rest days. A lifter who likes scheduled deloads may use them, as an option, not a requirement; the default for recreational lifters is reactive.
**Evidence:** In trained young adults (29 men, 10 women), a 1-week complete break mid-way through a 9-week programme did not change quadriceps or calf hypertrophy, endurance or power but gave some evidence of smaller strength gains (squat 1RM +13.0 vs +16.4 kg; posterior probability favouring continuous training 0.85 for 1RM, 0.92 for isometric strength). In untrained young men, cutting to 1 session and 2 sets in weeks 4 and 8 neither hindered nor helped thickness or 10RM. A Delphi consensus of coaches defined deloading as reduced training stress to manage fatigue, agreed volume is reduced, and stated "it is currently unknown if deloading enhances the adaptive response". Surveyed competitive athletes (n=246) deloaded 6.4 ± 1.7 days every 5.6 ± 2.3 weeks.
**Grade:** emerging
**Applies to:** Young adults over 8-9 weeks; coach and athlete practices are opinion from competitive lifters, mostly men. No data in older adults.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Two small RCTs with different designs; the strength cost in Coleman is uncertain. The "resensitisation" rationale is untested. Popular "deload every 4 weeks" advice outruns the evidence.
**Sources:** [S134], [S135], [S136], [S137]

## Expected progress

### Early timeline: neural then hypertrophic gains
**Rule:** Tell novices strength will rise quickly in weeks 1-6, mostly from learning the lifts and better muscle activation, before visible size changes. Do not judge whether a programme works for hypertrophy until at least 8-12 weeks of consistent training.
**Evidence:** ACSM 2009 says hypertrophy "becomes evident within the first 6 wk". In a classic 8-week study (n=15) hypertrophy became the dominant contributor after 3-5 weeks. Size changes can be detected from about 3 weeks (Seynnes 2007, flywheel training, n=7), but part of early CSA increase is oedema (Damas 2016).
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Untrained young adults; small, older data endorsed by ACSM. Older adults probably follow the same sequence more slowly (not studied here).
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Hypertrophy may start earlier than older models assumed; the exact crossover is uncertain.
**Sources:** [S039], [S122], [S123], [S124]

### Realistic rate of muscle growth
**Rule:** For a novice, expect a few percent more size per trained muscle in the first 1-2 months and roughly 5-10% over about 10 weeks, slowing after about 6-12 weeks. Do not treat measurements from the first 3 weeks as real muscle. Use body-weight and circumference trends over months.
**Evidence:** Thigh CSA rose +3.5-5.2% at 20 days and +6.5-7.4% at 35 days (n=7, flywheel), and +2.7% at week 3 and +10.4% at week 10 (n=10 men), the week-3 gain coinciding with oedema. In 15 weeks of bench press, gains in the last 6 weeks were smaller than in the first 6.
**Grade:** emerging
**Applies to:** Untrained young adults, mostly men, n=7-15. Slower for trained and likely older adults.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Studies differ on how much early CSA gain is oedema; flywheel rates may be at the high end. Individual variation is very large.
**Sources:** [S123], [S124], [S125]

### Novice strength gains and plateau
**Rule:** Tell beginners on a twice-weekly programme to expect about 20% strength gains (with a wide range) on trained lifts over the first 5-6 months, possibly 30-50% in the first year, then much slower progress. Frame slowing after 6-12 months as normal.
**Evidence:** In untrained adults aged 19-78 (n=287 plus 72 controls), 20-24 weeks of twice-weekly training raised leg-press 1RM 21.1 ± 11.5% (range -8 to +60%) and quadriceps size 4.8 ± 6.1%. In records of 14,690 adults on a once-weekly single-set programme, strength rose about 30-50% in year one and about 50-60% above baseline by year 6, practically plateauing by about 1-2 years.
**Grade:** emerging
**Applies to:** Untrained adults of both sexes, including middle-aged and older. One Finnish lab; one low-dose commercial model.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** The Steele data are retrospective company records with company-affiliated co-authors; whether the plateau can be overcome is unclear.
**Sources:** [S126], [S127]

### Trained lifters progress more slowly
**Rule:** Tell users progress slows the more trained they are. For recreationally trained lifters, measure gains over months to years and progress them with heavier loads (more work at or above about 80% 1RM for strength) and adequate volume.
**Evidence:** Rhea's meta-analysis (140 studies) found maximal strength gains came from about 60% 1RM and 3 days per week per muscle in untrained people but about 80% 1RM and 2 days per week in trained people. ACSM 2009 says improvement depends on training status. ACSM 2026 reports diminishing returns beyond about 18-20 weekly sets for hypertrophy and 2-3 sets per exercise for strength.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Healthy adults; trained data mostly from young men; exact rates for trained recreational lifters are not well quantified.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Rhea 2003 is older and effect-size based; ACSM says the exact optimal number of sets cannot be determined. "Trained" in research often means moderately trained.
**Sources:** [S001], [S039], [S128]

### Large individual variability
**Rule:** Do not declare a user a non-responder, or rewrite the plan, because one metric stalls in the first 4-8 weeks. Track several outcomes (load or reps, circumferences, body weight, function). If strength rises but size does not, keep going and check volume, effort and nutrition. Allow at least 12 weeks, preferably 24.
**Evidence:** In 12 weeks of elbow-flexor training (n=585), biceps CSA changed -2 to +59% and 1RM 0 to +250%. In untrained adults on 20-24 week programmes (n=287), 29% were low responders for size but only 7% for strength. In adults over 65 (n=110 at 12 weeks, 85 at 24 weeks), every subject improved on at least one outcome, more so with longer training.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Untrained adults of all ages and both sexes.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Part of the apparent variability is measurement error. Whether true non-responders exist is debated; longer duration and more dose increase the share of responders.
**Sources:** [S127], [S129], [S130]

## Breaks, maintenance and returning

### Maintenance dose during busy periods
**Rule:** When life gets busy, cut frequency and sets before cutting load. For a younger client, 1 session per week with 1-2 sets per major movement at the usual working load is a reasonable maintenance minimum. For clients over about 60, aim for 2 sessions per week with 2-3 sets per exercise to keep muscle. Return to the full programme as soon as possible.
**Evidence:** In an RCT (n=70), after 16 weeks of training, 32 weeks at one-third or one-ninth of the dose preserved hypertrophy in young adults (20-35) but not older adults (60-75). A narrative review concludes strength and size (at least in younger people) can be kept up to 32 weeks with 1 session and 1 set per exercise if relative load is maintained, while older people may need up to 2 sessions and 2-3 sets.
**Grade:** emerging
**Applies to:** Adults who had just completed supervised training; no data specific to women or long-term trained lifters.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Few RCTs and a narrative review; the threshold for older adults is uncertain.
**Sources:** [S062], [S063]

### Short breaks cost little
**Rule:** Reassure users that a holiday or illness break of a few weeks is not a meaningful setback. As a heuristic, after under about 2 weeks off restart at about 90% of previous working loads; after longer breaks start 10-20% below previous working loads and rebuild over 1-3 weeks (see "Returning lifters"). Expect to regain previous levels within a few weeks. Never use fear of losing gains to push training through illness or injury.
**Evidence:** In untrained young men doing bench press, 3-week breaks after 6-week blocks caused no significant loss of CSA or 1RM, and total gains matched continuous training over 15 and 24 weeks (n=15 and n=14). In trained young adults, a 1-week full break left hypertrophy unchanged but slightly reduced lower-body strength gains.
**Grade:** emerging
**Applies to:** Untrained young men, one upper-body exercise; trained young adults for the 1-week break.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Breaks are non-inferior, not superior, and do not justify scheduling breaks to boost gains.
**Sources:** [S125], [S134], [S138]

### Longer detraining
**Rule:** Tell clients that strength decays more slowly than they fear, and that the longer the break, the larger the loss. For breaks of several months, especially over 65, expect noticeable loss of muscle and strength, and offer a minimal home or maintenance routine to blunt it.
**Evidence:** Bosquet 2013 (103 studies) found stopping training reduces submaximal strength (SMD -0.62, 95% CI -0.80 to -0.45), maximal force (-0.46) and maximal power (-0.20), with a dose-response by duration, larger losses over 65, and for force and power larger losses in inactive people than recreational athletes. In older adults (6 studies), muscle size did not fall significantly after 12-24 weeks (d -0.60, -1.21 to 0.01) but did after 31-52 weeks (d -1.11, -1.75 to -0.47). A 2026 meta-analysis (86 RCTs, n=5,670, mixed exercise types) found body mass, waist, VO2max and sometimes systolic blood pressure stay improved after stopping, while muscle and lean-mass gains were no longer significant.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Mixed populations; older-adult size data come from 6 small studies.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Rates vary with age, training status and break length; the sources give a dose-response, not exact cut-offs.
**Sources:** [S064], [S065], [S066]

### Returning lifters and "muscle memory"
**Rule:** Tell returning lifters that breaks do not erase their investment and that strength comes back faster than size. As a coaching heuristic, after under about 2 weeks off resume at about 90% of previous working loads; after longer breaks restart with fewer sets and loads 10-20% below previous working loads and rebuild over 1-3 weeks, then progress as usual. Do not promise "muscle memory" as guaranteed accelerated regrowth.
**Evidence:** In an RCT of untrained adults (42 completers, 45% women), 10 weeks of training, 10 weeks off and 10 weeks back produced final results matching 20 weeks of continuous training, with rapid regain. After 20 weeks of detraining, muscle size returned to baseline while strength remained elevated (n=19). Myonuclei gained in training were retained after 16 weeks off (n=12), but in that study and in Psilander the previously trained muscle did not respond clearly better to retraining than the untrained limb.
**Grade:** emerging
**Applies to:** Previously untrained young and middle-aged adults after about 10 weeks of training; no data on long-term lifters returning after years off.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Popular muscle-memory claims outrun human evidence; Psilander could not test the hypothesis rigorously because myonuclei did not increase. Restart percentages are heuristics.
**Sources:** [S064], [S065], [S067], [S068], [S069]

## Combining cardio and lifting

### Cardio does not cancel muscle gain
**Rule:** Actively correct the "cardio kills gains" myth. Never advise a client seeking body-composition change or health to skip cardio. At trial doses (about 2-3 aerobic sessions per week alongside 2-3 lifting sessions), keep lifting volume and progression unchanged and watch recovery and energy intake. If maximising size is the main goal, avoid very long or very frequent endurance sessions and manage running volume when leg training volume is high (see "Cardio modality").
**Evidence:** Schumann 2022 (43 studies, 15 for hypertrophy) found no whole-muscle hypertrophy difference (SMD -0.01, 95% CI -0.16 to 0.18). Monserda-Vilaro 2023 (25 studies) found no whole-muscle difference for continuous, HIIT or mixed protocols (SMD <0.03). The Held 2026 umbrella review (17 meta-analyses, 144 studies) found comparable hypertrophy, strength and power versus resistance training alone and better aerobic capacity (SMD 0.77). Wilson 2012 (21 studies) reported smaller within-group hypertrophy effects with concurrent training (0.85 vs 1.23), with decrements for running but not cycling and negative correlations with endurance frequency and duration.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Healthy adults, mostly untrained to recreationally active young men, 4-24 week supervised trials at moderate aerobic volumes. Not marathon-level volumes or advanced bodybuilders.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Wilson 2012 and fibre-level data suggest a small cost in some conditions. ACSM 2026 found insufficient data to judge concurrent training, which is why this is moderate.
**Sources:** [S001], [S089], [S090], [S091], [S092]

### Maximal strength with cardio
**Rule:** For general clients, prescribe cardio and lifting together without fear of losing strength, keeping lifting intensity and progression unchanged. For trained men chasing lower-body maximal strength, separate cardio from lifting and keep cardio volume moderate.
**Evidence:** Schumann 2022 found SMD -0.06 (95% CI -0.20 to 0.09), with no moderation by modality, frequency, training status or age. Held 2026 found strength comparable. Subgroups show a small lower-body deficit in men (Huiberts 2024, SMD -0.43), trained individuals (Petre 2021, ES -0.35) and with HIIT (Sabag 2018, ES -0.248).
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Healthy adults, mostly untrained to recreationally active men, mostly lower-body 1RM.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** ACSM 2026 found insufficient data. Subgroup deficits are small and context-specific.
**Sources:** [S001], [S089], [S090], [S093], [S094], [S095]

### Explosive strength is the most vulnerable outcome
**Rule:** If a client prioritises jumping, sprinting or sport power, separate cardio from power and strength work by at least 3 h or use different days. If HIIT is used, sprint-interval or repeated-sprint formats may interfere less with jumping than classic HIIT (tentative). For general-health clients this is low priority.
**Evidence:** Schumann 2022 found SMD -0.28 (95% CI -0.48 to -0.08) for explosive strength, significant when modes were ≤20 min apart (SMD -0.31) but not ≥3 h apart. Wilson 2012 power effects were 0.91 for strength only versus 0.55 concurrent. Held 2026 found power comparable. Kikuchi 2026 (21 studies) found no overall jump deficit (SMD -0.12), but short and long HIIT reduced jump gains (SMD -0.53) while sprint formats did not (SMD 0.14).
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Mostly young, untrained to recreationally active adults.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Held and Kikuchi found no overall deficit; subgroup findings rest on few studies; the effect is small and imprecise.
**Sources:** [S089], [S090], [S092], [S096]

### Lift first in combined sessions
**Rule:** In combined sessions, put resistance training first and cardio after, especially for strength goals. If cardio must come first, accept it: hypertrophy and aerobic gains are not affected by order and the strength benefit is small.
**Evidence:** Lifting first gave more lower-body 1RM: +3.96 kg (Murlasits 2018, 95% CI 0.81-7.10), +6.91% (Eddens 2018, 1.96-11.87), SMD 0.19 (Gao and Yu 2023, 19 RCTs, 0.02-0.37). Order did not affect hypertrophy (Eddens +1.15%, ns), VO2max or lower-limb power. ACSM 2026 concludes strength is enhanced when trained at the start of the session.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Healthy adults in same-session programmes of ≥5 weeks; in Gao and Yu the benefit appeared in older adults, women and programmes over 8 weeks.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** The Held 2026 abstract is internally inconsistent ("no significant effects of training sequence" yet RT-first trends), though the direction agrees. ACSM's "exercise order" category also pools order of resistance exercises. Gao and Yu has a corrigendum.
**Sources:** [S001], [S090], [S097], [S098], [S099]

### Spacing cardio and lifting
**Rule:** If the schedule allows, separate cardio and lifting by at least about 3 h or put them on different days; this matters for trained clients chasing strength or power. For beginners and general-health clients, convenience comes first and same-session training is fine, lifting first.
**Evidence:** In Schumann 2022, the explosive-strength deficit was significant with ≤20 min between modes (SMD -0.31) and not with ≥3 h; maximal strength and hypertrophy did not differ by arrangement. In Petre 2021, the lower-body 1RM deficit in trained people occurred in same-session training (ES -0.66) but not separate sessions (ES -0.10).
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Healthy adults; the spacing benefit is shown mainly in trained people and for explosive or maximal strength.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** No meta-analysis compared intervals such as 6 h versus 24 h; thresholds beyond "at least 3 h" are not evidence-based.
**Sources:** [S089], [S094]

### Training status and sex as moderators
**Rule:** For beginners and returning lifters, moderate cardio (about 2-3 sessions per week) needs no restriction. For clients in a block where lower-body maximal strength is the priority, especially trained men, do cardio in separate sessions and keep endurance volume moderate. Women can combine cardio and lifting with less concern; apply the same separation if leg strength is a priority goal, because the female estimate is imprecise.
**Evidence:** Petre 2021 (27 studies) found concurrent training reduced lower-body 1RM gains in trained (ES -0.35), not significantly in moderately trained (ES -0.20) and not in untrained individuals (ES 0.03). Huiberts 2024 (59 studies, 1346 adults aged 18-50) found lower-body strength blunted in men (SMD -0.43, 95% CI -0.64 to -0.22) but not women (0.08, -0.34 to 0.49; p=0.03), with no sex difference for upper-body strength, power or VO2max, and insufficient hypertrophy data.
**Grade:** emerging
**Applies to:** Healthy adults aged about 18-50; "trained" in Petre means ≥3 months of structured training; data on trained women are scarce.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Schumann 2022 and Huiberts 2024 found no moderation by training status. The sex finding is a single meta-analysis with an imprecise female estimate.
**Sources:** [S089], [S093], [S094]

### Cardio modality
**Rule:** Do not prescribe a cardio modality to protect muscle gain. Walking is the base for everyone. Cycling or other low-impact options (rowing, incline walking) suit beginners, heavier clients and people with joint pain, for tolerance, not for proven anti-interference reasons. For bone, walking alone does little; resistance training plus some impact is what helps (see "Bone health" in cardio-health.md and "Resistance training and bone in men and adults 65+" below). Otherwise the client's preference wins, because adherence matters most. Manage running volume when leg training volume is high.
**Evidence:** Wilson 2012 found running but not cycling reduced hypertrophy and strength; Lundberg 2022 found a type I fibre deficit with running (SMD -0.81) but not cycling. Schumann 2022 found no modality moderation for maximal strength or hypertrophy, with a fragile cycling signal for explosive strength (SMD -0.44, losing significance after removing one study). Sabag 2018 found a trend for cycling HIIT to impair lower-body strength (ES -0.377, p=0.074) but not running HIIT. For bone, a meta-analysis of 10 trials found walking alone did not change spine BMD in women, and the UK osteoporosis consensus recommends resistance plus impact exercise.
**Grade:** emerging
**Applies to:** Healthy, mostly young adults; indirect subgroup comparisons. The bone evidence comes from peri- and postmenopausal women and people with osteoporosis.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Meta-analyses disagree in direction.
**Sources:** [S089], [S092], [S095], [S100], [S789], [S784]

### How much cardio is compatible
**Rule:** For a client prioritising muscle or maximal strength, 2-3 moderate cardio sessions per week of about 30 min (the typical trial dose) is compatible with full gains; power may be slightly blunted. Add more endurance gradually, keep the hardest or longest cardio away from leg sessions, and if lifting stalls, reduce endurance duration or frequency before cutting lifting.
**Evidence:** Wilson 2012 found negative correlations between endurance frequency (r -0.26 to -0.35) and duration (r -0.29 to -0.75) and gains. Schumann 2022 found no significant moderation by frequency of concurrent training (more than 5 vs fewer than 5 weekly sessions; subgroup means 6.1 vs 4.1 weekly sessions, against 3.1 vs 2.0 sessions in the strength-only groups). Petre 2021 trials averaged 2.6-2.9 endurance sessions per week of 29-37 min. No meta-analysis identifies a threshold.
**Grade:** emerging
**Applies to:** Healthy recreational adults; high-volume endurance training is outside the evidence.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** The dose direction rests on 2012 correlational data; "~30 min, 2-3 times per week" describes typical protocols, not a tested limit.
**Sources:** [S089], [S092], [S094]

### HIIT and sprint intervals with lifting
**Rule:** HIIT and short sprint intervals are a time-efficient way to add fitness with little or no cost to size or maximal strength. Keep sessions short, place them after lifting or on separate days, and avoid hard lower-body HIIT right before heavy leg training. For power goals, prefer very short sprint intervals over longer HIIT bouts. All-out sprint formats (SIT, REHIT) are not for sedentary beginners: they require all PAR-Q+ answers "no" and at least 4 weeks of regular moderate cardio without symptoms, and are options for already-active clients who lack time. Anyone over 45, or with any PAR-Q+ "yes", gets a qualified exercise professional or clinician review before vigorous or maximal work (see "PAR-Q+ specific thresholds" in recovery-habits.md).
**Evidence:** Sabag 2018 found similar hypertrophy and upper-body strength with a small lower-body deficit (ES -0.248, p=0.049). Ferraro-Farro 2026 (9 RCTs) found sprint intervals plus resistance training did not differ for strength, jumps or sprints and improved VO2max (SMD 0.78). Kikuchi 2026 found no squat 1RM deficit (SMD -0.06). Monserda-Vilaro 2023 found reduced fibre-level but not whole-muscle hypertrophy with HIIT. The 2023 PAR-Q+ clears people who answer "no" to every question to start slowly and build up gradually, and sends any "yes" to further questions or a qualified exercise professional.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Healthy, mostly young adults in short trials; sprint-interval samples are small. The sprint prerequisites apply to everyone.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Sabag's small strength cost is not seen in later analyses. Ferraro-Farro 2026 has an erratum (Int J Sports Med 2026;47(4):e3) whose content was not reviewed. The 4-week moderate-cardio prerequisite and the review for any PAR-Q+ "yes" are conservative product choices, not PAR-Q+ text.
**Sources:** [S091], [S095], [S096], [S101], [S641]

### Fibre-level hypertrophy
**Rule:** Treat fibre-level interference as small and mostly theoretical; it does not justify removing cardio or choosing a modality for the client. For a client whose only goal is maximal size, avoid frequent HIIT or high-volume running, and otherwise choose the modality as in "Cardio modality".
**Evidence:** Lundberg 2022 (15 studies) found overall fibre hypertrophy SMD -0.23 (95% CI -0.46 to -0.00, p=0.050), with non-significant type I (-0.34) and type II (-0.13) estimates and a type I deficit with running but not cycling. Monserda-Vilaro 2023 found more fibre hypertrophy with resistance training alone versus HIIT-based concurrent training (SMD >0.33) but no clear difference with continuous endurance only. Whole-muscle size was unaffected in both.
**Grade:** emerging
**Applies to:** Healthy adults, biopsy measures; clinical relevance unclear.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Borderline results conflict with null whole-muscle findings; subgroup analyses.
**Sources:** [S091], [S100]

### Concurrent training after 50
**Rule:** For clients over 50, combined aerobic and resistance training is the preferred programme; interference is not a reason to drop either. Lift first or on separate days, keep at least 2 resistance sessions per week, and include some power or fast-concentric work.
**Evidence:** Khalafi 2022 (49 randomised trials, 2,587 adults over 50, study mean ages 55-88) found no difference versus resistance training alone in lower-body (SMD 0.07, p=0.48) or upper-body strength (SMD -0.07, p=0.38), a larger VO2max/peak gain (SMD 0.77, p=0.005), and larger strength gains than aerobic training alone (lower SMD 0.60, upper 0.57). In Schumann 2022, age (<40 vs >40) did not moderate maximal strength, explosive strength or hypertrophy. In Gao and Yu 2023, lifting before endurance gave slightly more lower-limb strength (SMD 0.19, 95% CI 0.02-0.37), evident in the elderly subgroup (p=0.039), with no difference in VO2max or power.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Mostly healthy, community-dwelling adults over 50, for strength and VO2max. Hypertrophy in older adults was not pooled in Khalafi.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Older-adult trials mainly measured strength and VO2max, not size. The elderly order subgroup is hypothesis-generating. Frail people and those with comorbidities need individual medical screening.
**Sources:** [S089], [S099], [S102]

### Cardio right before lifting
**Rule:** If cardio must come before lifting, keep it to a short, easy warm-up rather than 30+ min or hard intervals. Expect lower leg loads on a day the client has done a long or hard ride or run beforehand.
**Evidence:** Markov 2022 (15 studies, trained men) found prior aerobic exercise reduced lower-limb strength (SMD 0.79), not significantly at low intensity (SMD 0.65, p=0.157), numerically more after >30 min (1.02 vs 0.59, ns), and more after cycling than running (0.79 vs 0.28). Power was unchanged.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Acute lower-limb strength in trained men; not direct evidence of long-term losses.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Long-term, sequence did not significantly affect hypertrophy (Held 2026). Acute cycling being worse contrasts with chronic fibre data where running looked worse.
**Sources:** [S090], [S100], [S103]

## Power and plyometrics

### Plyometric dose
**Rule:** If a client wants to jump higher or be more explosive, add plyometric jumps for at least 7 to 10 weeks (more than 14 to 20 sessions in total), aiming for 3 sessions a week where possible; fewer weekly sessions gave smaller gains in one subgroup analysis. Mix jump types (squat jumps, countermovement jumps and drop jumps) instead of using one kind. Bodyweight jumps are enough: adding weight to plyometric jumps gave no extra benefit. More total jumps over a block is not clearly better. Treat these dose cut points as tentative.
**Evidence:** de Villarreal 2009 (56 studies, 225 effect sizes, vertical jump height): more than 10 weeks and more than 20 sessions, with high-intensity programmes of more than 50 jumps per session, seemed to maximise gains (p < 0.05); combining squat, countermovement and drop jumps beat a single form (p < 0.05); added weight gave no extra benefit. Ramirez-Campillo 2023 (61 studies, 2576 participants aged 8.1 to 73.1, reactive strength index): gains were greater for adults than youth (p = 0.023) and after more than 7 weeks, more than 14 sessions and three versus fewer than three weekly sessions (p = 0.027 to 0.060), with similar gains after 1080 or fewer versus more than 1080 total jumps. Certainty was moderate for the main analysis and low to moderate for the moderator analyses.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Healthy people, mostly male (about 78%) and largely young: about 60% of the Ramirez-Campillo sample was under 18 and 42 of 61 studies had a sport background. Adults improved more than youth. Ramirez-Campillo measured reactive strength (mostly drop-jump RSI), not jump height. Not tested specifically in recreational gym-goers whose main goal is hypertrophy.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** In Ramirez-Campillo the meta-regression found that no training variable explained the effect (p = 0.714 to 0.984, R2 = 0.0), so the cut points come from subgroup comparisons. The 50 jumps per session (de Villarreal) and 1080 total jumps per block (Ramirez-Campillo) measure different things, and the 3 sessions a week rests on one subgroup analysis. The no-added-weight point applies to plyometric jumps; light loaded jump squats are a different comparison (see "Loads for power and jumping").
**Sources:** [S830], [S831]

### Plyometrics alone and combined with lifting
**Rule:** Plyometrics alone give small to moderate gains in jump height, sprint and leg strength. For a lifter who wants more athleticism, keep the lifting and add jumps or other explosive work (for example in the same session, as complex or contrast training) rather than swapping lifting for jumps. Expect strength gains at least equal to lifting alone; any extra gain in jumping or short sprints is uncertain because meta-analyses disagree. The evidence covers performance outcomes only, so this is optional for clients whose goals are hypertrophy or health.
**Evidence:** Oxfeldt 2019 (25 studies, adults 18+, 4 to 12 weeks of plyometrics): SMD 0.45 (0.16 to 0.75) for jump height, -0.59 (-1.01 to -0.17) for sprint time and 0.33 (0.03 to 0.63) for lower-body strength. Thapa 2024 (32 studies, 726 participants): complex and resistance training improved 1RM squat and bench press similarly; complex training favoured 5 m sprint (ES 0.96), 20 m sprint (ES 0.52), change of direction (ES 0.39) and CMJ (ES 0.36), with low to very low certainty. Bauer 2019 (33 studies, 1064 participants): versus traditional training, complex training was superior only for 1RM squat and 20 m sprint, findings influenced by single studies. Thapa 2026 (19 studies, 537 participants): complex training beat ballistic-only training for 1RM (ES 1.12), squat jump (ES 0.37), 10 m (ES 0.67) and 40 m sprint (ES 0.72), low to very low certainty. Zhao 2026 network meta-analysis (34 studies, 1,057 participants): versus resistance training, only complex training significantly improved CMJ height (MD 2.50, 95% CI 0.85 to 4.10). Brandt 2023 network meta-analysis (24 studies): all combined formats beat control (MD 2.87 for complex to 3.43 for traditional training) and none beat another. Cowley 2026 network meta-analysis: strength and power adaptations were similar across all methods, including complex and contrast training.
**Grade:** emerging
**Applies to:** Healthy adults who are recreationally active or athletes (Oxfeldt); the complex-training reviews span sedentary to highly trained participants, and Bauer framed its conclusion for athletes. The plyometrics-alone sentence would merit moderate; the grade is emerging because the complex-training meta-analyses disagree.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Thapa 2024 and Zhao 2026 found extra gains in CMJ and short sprints; Bauer 2019 only in 1RM squat and 20 m sprint; Brandt 2023 found no combined format superior for CMJ; Cowley 2026 found complex and contrast training no different from traditional training. Certainty is low to very low throughout.
**Sources:** [S832], [S833], [S834], [S835], [S836], [S837], [S838]

### Loads for power and jumping
**Rule:** Heavy loads build maximal strength best (see "Load range for maximal strength"). If a client wants more power, include some sets with light to moderate loads (about 40 to 70% of 1RM) lifted with maximum intent, and for jump height lighter loads (about 30% of 1RM, such as jump squats). Velocity-based training with bar-speed devices works about as well as percentage-based loading but no better, so the equipment is optional (see also "Autoregulation").
**Evidence:** Swinton 2024 (295 studies, 535 groups, 6,710 participants, time points up to 26 weeks, Bayesian dose-response modelling): improvements depended mainly on load intensity and the outcome; maximal strength was likely maximised with the heaviest loads, vertical jump with about 30% 1RM and power with 40 to 70% 1RM, and loads lifted with maximum intent that do not limit velocity may transfer best to jumping. Liao 2021 (6 studies, 124 participants aged 16 to 30): no significant difference between velocity-based and percentage-based training for back squat 1RM (MD 3.03 kg, 95% CI -3.55 to 9.61) or jump (SMD 0.27, -0.15 to 0.7). Orange 2022 (4 trials, 88 participants): trivial, imprecise differences for strength (SMD 0.06, -0.51 to 0.63), power (SMD 0.11, -0.28 to 0.49) and sprint speed (SMD -0.10, -0.72 to 0.53), low to very low quality evidence.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Healthy trained and untrained adults (Swinton); only 12% of its studies used female-only groups. The velocity-based trials were small, in people aged 16 to 30 (Liao) or apparently healthy participants (Orange).
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Swinton did not require control groups, so the load optima are model estimates from a largely uncontrolled database. The velocity-based evidence is small (6 and 4 trials) and low quality. The jump-squat suggestion sits alongside de Villarreal 2009 (no extra benefit from adding weight to plyometric jumps); these are different comparisons. The older-adult rule "How to prescribe power training" uses 40 to 60% 1RM, consistent with this range.
**Sources:** [S839], [S840], [S841]

## Balance and trunk training

### Optional balance training for younger adults: dose
**Rule:** For healthy adults under 65 who want better balance, or to cover the ACSM neuromotor component, offer balance work on 2-3 days per week. Clients aged 65 and over follow "Older adults: multicomponent balance and strength" (cardio-health.md) instead. A practical starting template is about 3 sessions per week of 11-15 min, roughly 4 exercises of 2 sets each, held 21-40 s, for 11-12 weeks. Present these figures as a reasonable template drawn from studies in 16-40 year olds, not as proven optima, and do not promise large balance gains to recreational exercisers.
**Evidence:** ACSM 2011: on 2-3 days per week adults should perform resistance exercise and neuromotor exercise involving balance, agility and coordination. Lesinski 2015 (25 RCTs, healthy adults aged 16-40): balance training improved steady-state balance (ESdb 0.73) and proactive balance (ESdb 0.92); effects were 1.29 in elite athletes, 0.32 in sub-elite athletes, 0.69 in recreational athletes and 0.82 in untrained subjects. The most effective parameters for steady-state balance were 11-12 weeks (ESdb 1.09), three sessions per week (mean ESdb 0.72), at least 16-19 sessions in total (ESdb 1.12), 11-15 min per session (ESdb 1.11), four exercises per session (ESdb 1.29), two sets per exercise (ESdb 1.63) and 21-40 s per exercise (ESdb 1.06). Six sessions per week (ESdb 1.84) rests on a single study, so the template uses 3.
**Grade:** emerging
**Applies to:** Healthy adults aged 16-40 (elite, sub-elite, recreational and untrained) in the dose meta-analysis. Applying it to adults aged 41-64 is extrapolation. The age 65 cut-off only hands clients over to the older-adult rules; it is not a study finding.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** The dose figures come from subgroup analyses of low-quality trials (mean PEDro 5), and the authors say one parameter is hard to separate from the others. Navaratnarajah 2026 (17 RCTs, n = 716, recreational athletes) found small balance effects with confidence intervals crossing zero (single-component SMD 0.15, 95% CI -0.05 to 0.34; multicomponent SMD 0.20, -0.01 to 0.41), hence emerging. See also "Balance gains are task specific".
**Sources:** [S842], [S396], [S843]

### Balance gains are task specific
**Rule:** Tell clients that balance training mainly improves the exact tasks they practise. Pick balance drills that look like what the client wants to improve (their sport, hiking, single-leg work). Do not sell balance training as a way to build strength or sprint speed; use strength and plyometric training for those. Effects on jumping and agility are mixed. Do not judge progress with a generic one-leg stance test; retest the trained task or the target task instead.
**Evidence:** Kummel 2016 (6 RCTs, 102 subjects, healthy people): balance training improved the trained tasks (SMDbs 0.79, 95% CI 0.48-1.10, I2 0%) but had no effect on non-trained balance tasks (SMDbs -0.07 to 0.18, CIs crossing zero); the authors conclude generic tasks such as one-leg stance may have little value as overall balance measures. Zech 2010 (20 RCTs, physically active people up to 40): balance training improved postural sway and functional balance versus untrained controls, with controversial findings but indications of benefit for jumping and agility; versus plyometric or strength training, results for strength and sprint were conflicting or showed no effect.
**Grade:** emerging
**Applies to:** Healthy people in Kummel (small samples); healthy, physically active adults up to 40 in Zech.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Kummel included only 6 small studies, and Zech rates trial quality as low. Because Zech reports indications of benefit for jumping and agility versus untrained controls, the rule does not claim balance training fails to build power.
**Sources:** [S844], [S845]

### Trunk (core) training: an add-on for trunk endurance and balance
**Rule:** Dedicated trunk training (planks, side planks and similar) is a reasonable add-on, mainly for trunk endurance and balance, where several meta-analyses agree that it helps. Do not sell it as a way to boost strength, sprinting or jumping, because meta-analyses disagree on that transfer, and it does not replace the main lifts. More than two sessions per week was linked to larger balance gains, and in another review more than 18 sessions in total gave larger effects on power and sprint. These reviews do not compare exercise types (planks, anti-rotation work, loaded carries), so choose exercises for variety and preference.
**Evidence:** Saeterbakken 2022 (31 controlled trials, 693 athletes aged 11 to 37, PEDro 5): local muscular endurance SMD 1.29; maximal strength 0.39, lower limb power 0.30, linear sprint 0.66 and change of direction 0.70, with larger power and sprint effects after more than 18 sessions (SMD 0.45 to 0.84). Yu 2025 (29 RCTs, 956 athletes aged 15 to 23): core endurance SMD 1.32, balance SMD 0.99, but no significant effect on speed (SMD -0.28, p = 0.28), maximal strength (p = 0.27), change of direction (SMD 0.10, p = 0.69) or vertical jump (SMD 0.90, p = 0.1). Rodriguez-Perea 2023 (21 studies meta-analysed, healthy subjects over 12): balance ES 1.17, vertical jump ES 0.69, horizontal jump ES 0.84; throwing velocity not significant (ES 0.30, p = 0.14). Liu 2026 (22 studies, 588 team-sport athletes): balance SMD 0.67, larger with more than 2 sessions per week (SMD 0.97 vs 0.20); vertical jump SMD 0.35, sprint SMD -0.52 and change of direction SMD -0.40 (negative means faster), low to very low certainty. Zhong 2025 (11 studies, healthy adults aged 60 and over): Functional Reach Test SMD 0.82, Gait Test SMD 0.32.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Mostly young competitive or team-sport athletes aged 11 to 37, plus healthy subjects over 12 (Rodriguez-Perea) and, for balance only, healthy adults aged 60 and over (Zhong, which mixes single-group trials and RCTs). Applying this to non-athlete gym-goers is an extrapolation.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Yu 2025 found no significant transfer to speed, strength, change of direction or jumping, while Saeterbakken, Rodriguez-Perea and Liu reported positive transfer. Quality is low (PEDro 5, low to very low GRADE certainty, I2 37 to 100% in Yu). The grade is moderate for endurance and balance only; the performance transfer would be emerging.
**Sources:** [S849], [S850], [S851], [S852], [S853]

### Compound free-weight lifts load the trunk
**Rule:** Heavy free-weight lifts (squats, deadlifts, hip thrusts) produce some of the highest trunk muscle activity measured in the erector spinae, rectus abdominis and external oblique, and more lumbar multifidus activity than ball or device core exercises. A programme built on these lifts with heavy loads therefore already works the trunk. Direct core work is optional: choose it for preference, trunk endurance or balance, or to target the internal oblique, which was most active in plank-type stability exercises. This is acute EMG evidence only and does not show long-term strength or size gains.
**Evidence:** Martuscello 2013 (17 studies, 252 participants): moderate evidence that lumbar multifidus EMG activity is greater during free-weight exercises than ball or device exercises; the authors recommend multijoint free-weight exercises rather than core-specific ones. Rogan 2014 (16 studies): exercises with a vertical starting position, such as the deadlift or squat, activated significantly more core muscles than exercises in a horizontal starting position. Oliva-Lozano 2020: free-weight exercises gave the greatest rectus abdominis, external oblique and erector spinae activity (rectus abdominis in the Bulgarian squat and back squat at six maximum repetitions; erector spinae in the deadlift, hip thrust and back squat); core stability exercises gave the greatest internal oblique activity and traditional exercises the greatest multifidus activity.
**Grade:** emerging
**Applies to:** Healthy adults in acute EMG studies. There are no training studies.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** For multifidus, Martuscello found free weights beat ball or device exercises while Oliva-Lozano ranked traditional core exercises highest; these are different comparisons. Transverse abdominis is under-researched and quadratus lumborum was not studied. The rectus abdominis result rests on near-maximal (6RM) squats, so lighter loads may load the trunk less. EMG methods were inconsistent, and activation is a surrogate, not a measure of strength or hypertrophy gains.
**Sources:** [S854], [S855], [S856]

## Older adults

### Core prescription for adults 65+
**Rule:** For clients over about 65: start with 2 resistance sessions per week covering major muscle groups and progress to 3 if recovery allows; 2-3 sets of about 7-12 reps, starting at about 2-3 RIR and progressing to about 1-2 RIR after about 8 weeks without symptoms (see "Effort limits for older adults and cardiovascular risk"). Steer load by RIR, not %1RM: the 70-80% 1RM targets in the older-adult guidelines come from supervised settings with a measured 1RM. Add balance or multicomponent work so total activity reaches ≥3 days per week. Machines are a good starting point. Prioritise consistency over clever programming. Screen for medical conditions and refer when needed.
**Evidence:** WHO 2020 strongly recommends multicomponent activity emphasising balance and strength on ≥3 days per week for older adults, in addition to muscle strengthening ≥2 days per week. In healthy adults ≥65 (Borde 2015, 25 RCTs), resistance training improved strength substantially (SMD 1.57) and morphology modestly (SMD 0.42, 9 studies). In sarcopenic older adults (14 studies, n=528, mean age 73), resistance training increased muscle mass (g 0.38, 95% CI 0.18-0.58); manipulating variables did not improve gains, and older age reduced the response. The NSCA position statement endorses resistance training against loss of strength, mass and function.
**Grade:** strong
**Applies to:** Healthy and sarcopenic community-dwelling older adults (mean ages about 70-73). Frail or multimorbid people need clinical supervision; this coach should not program for them alone.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Specific numbers from Borde come from low-quality, heterogeneous trials and are a guide only. Response declines with age and varies widely. ACSM 2026 says its recommendations apply across ages but did not separate reviews by age.
**Sources:** [S001], [S016], [S017], [S070], [S071]

### Volume and frequency in older adults
**Rule:** Start older clients with 2 full-body sessions per week, 2-3 sets per exercise and moderate loads steered by RIR, progressing gradually. Progress to a third session if recovery allows; it gives small extra strength gains.
**Evidence:** In Borde 2015, the most effective single values for strength were 2 sessions per week (SMD 2.13), 2-3 sets (SMD 2.99) and 7-9 reps (SMD 1.98); for morphology, 3 sessions, 2-3 sets and 7-9 reps. Kneffel 2021 (adults over 60, 14 studies) found the strength effect rose 0.14 per extra weekly session (CI 0.08-0.21) with no frequency effect on hypertrophy (p=0.67), concluding more than two sessions is unlikely to add much.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Healthy, community-dwelling older adults; not frail or clinical populations without adaptation.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Borde's data are low quality (mean PEDro 4.6, I2=80%) and variables were analysed independently. Borde's 3 per week for morphology only partly agrees with Kneffel's null.
**Sources:** [S016], [S017], [S018]

### Loads for older adults
**Rule:** Start older clients at moderate loads (8-15 reps at 2-3 RIR) and, as tolerance and technique allow and after about 8 weeks without symptoms, progress main lifts to heavier sets of fewer reps at about 1-2 RIR. Choose loads by reps and RIR, not %1RM, because the coach has no measured 1RM; the %1RM figures below come from supervised trials. Lighter loads are valid when joints or confidence limit heavier work, provided sets are reasonably close to fatigue.
**Evidence:** In Borde 2015, 70-79% 1RM gave the largest strength effect and 51-69% 1RM with 7-9 reps the largest size effect. In Csapo 2016 (15 studies, n=448, mean age 67.8), heavy (~80%) versus light-moderate (~45%) loads tended to give larger strength gains (mu=0.43, P=0.06), smaller when work-matched (mu=0.30), with only marginally larger size gains (0.056-0.136).
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Healthy, community-dwelling adults with mean age about 65-70; not frail or sarcopenic clinical populations.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Low-quality, heterogeneous data; single-variable subgroups cannot capture interactions.
**Sources:** [S017], [S023]

### Starting load and ramp-up (ICFSR)
**Rule:** Use the first session or two at light loads (easy sets of 8-12 reps, far from failure) to learn technique, then raise load gradually until working sets of 8-12 reps sit at about 2-3 RIR (Borg 6-20 about 13-15). After about 8 weeks without symptoms, older adults may work at about 1-2 RIR. Progress again when the client exceeds the rep range at that effort. Without a measured 1RM, steer by reps and RIR, not %1RM. ICFSR's faster ramp-up to 70-80% 1RM at Borg 15-18 applies to supervised settings with a measured 1RM, not to this coach. Leave at least one rest day before training the same muscle group again.
**Evidence:** ICFSR 2021 prescribes 2-3 days per week, 1-3 sets of 8-12 reps on 8-10 major muscle groups, 1-3 min rest, starting at 30-40% 1RM and progressing to 70-80% 1RM (Borg 15-18 on the 6-20 scale), with power training at 40-60% 1RM and at least one rest day between sessions for the same muscle group.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Adults 65+, including frail and institutionalised people; extrapolated to untrained 50-65-year-olds.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Expert consensus, not head-to-head tested. The guideline's text also describes 50-80% 1RM within the first four sessions under supervision; the coach's slower, RIR-capped ramp-up (2-3 RIR first, 1-2 RIR after about 8 weeks) is a conservative product convention for unsupervised clients. People with cognitive impairment may need observer-rated exertion.
**Sources:** [S110]

### Do not keep older clients on light weights forever
**Rule:** Once technique is sound and the client is screened, do not leave older clients on easy loads: build them to genuinely challenging sets at about 2-3 RIR in the first weeks, and to about 1-2 RIR after about 8 weeks without symptoms, especially for strength goals. The 70-80% 1RM (Borg 15-18) reached within the first weeks in ICFSR applies to supervised settings with a measured 1RM; this coach has neither, so it steers by RIR. For purely functional goals, moderate loads plus power work are an acceptable alternative.
**Evidence:** Steib 2010 (29 trials, n=1,313, age 65-81) found high-intensity training gave larger strength gains than low (SMD 0.88) and moderate intensity (SMD 0.62), while functional gains were independent of intensity. ICFSR 2021 states there is "no need to wait months" before high-intensity training in frail older adults, describes 50-80% 1RM over the first four sessions, and states "even extreme frailty is not a contraindication to robust exercise".
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Adults 65+, including frail adults in supervised settings.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** The fast ramp-up in the sources assumes supervision and a measured 1RM; the RIR caps are a conservative product convention (see "Effort limits for older adults and cardiovascular risk").
**Sources:** [S110], [S112]

### Expected gains in older adults
**Rule:** Tell older users strength gains of about 25-30% are realistic over the first 3-6 months, with modest lean-mass gains (about 1 kg). Make strength and function (for example chair-rise time) the main progress markers, progress intensity gradually, and stress that longer training gives more people a positive response.
**Evidence:** In adults 50+ (47 studies, n=1079, about 18 weeks), strength rose about 24-33% (leg press 29 ± 2%, chest press 24 ± 2%, knee extension 33 ± 3%, lat pull 25 ± 2%), more at higher intensity. In adults over 65, supervised training added about 0.9 kg lean mass at 12 weeks and 1.1 kg at 24 weeks, and leg press rose about 33 kg and 50 kg.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Mostly community-dwelling adults 50+ in 6-52 week programmes; not clinical populations without medical input.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** High heterogeneity; lean mass ranged -3.3 to +5.4 kg and leg press -36 to +87 kg at 12 weeks.
**Sources:** [S130], [S131]

### Power training for function
**Rule:** For clients 60+ whose goal is function (rising from a chair, getting up and walking, independence), include fast-concentric lifts in most sessions. Do not present them as clearly superior: the extra gain over well-done traditional training is small and uncertain. If a client cannot or will not lift fast, traditional progressive training still delivers most of the benefit.
**Evidence:** Balachandran 2022 (13 RCTs, n=383, low certainty) found SMD 0.30 (95% CI 0.05-0.54) for performance-based function, "modest". Tschopp 2011 found ES 0.32 on follow-up values but a non-significant 0.38 (-0.51 to 1.28) on change values. Morrison 2023 (19 trials, n=1,055) found SPPB SMD 0.27 and TUG SMD 0.35, both low quality, and concluded effects are "similar". Self-reported function did not differ.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Adults 60+, mostly healthy community-dwelling, in about 12-week trials.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** The meta-analyses overlap heavily and are low certainty. Lopez 2023 (79 trials) found no significant head-to-head difference on five function tests.
**Sources:** [S104], [S105], [S106]

### Velocity and specific function tests
**Rule:** Both fast and traditional training improve chair rises, TUG and walking tests in older adults; do not promise test-specific advantages from velocity. A reasonable default is some fast-concentric work for single explosive tasks plus a traditional, higher-volume component for repeated or endurance tasks, framed as a hypothesis.
**Evidence:** A network meta-analysis (79 trials, n=3,575, untrained adults 60+) found both improved all five tests versus control (for example TUG -0.76 vs -0.64; 30-s sit-to-stand 0.89 vs 1.01), with high-velocity ranked first for fast walking, TUG and 5-times sit-to-stand and traditional first for 30-s sit-to-stand and 6-min walk, but no significant head-to-head difference (SMDs -0.12 to 0.28), low to very low certainty. Balachandran found no gait-speed difference (SMD -0.03).
**Grade:** emerging
**Applies to:** Untrained adults 60+, mixed health status; most studies at high risk of bias.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Rankings suggest velocity specificity that head-to-head tests do not confirm.
**Sources:** [S104], [S107]

### Fast intent builds power without costing muscle or strength
**Rule:** In older clients, use fast concentric reps as the default intent on suitable lifts (leg press, chair rise, machine chest press). Power improves while muscle and strength gains are kept.
**Evidence:** Power training improved muscle power more than traditional training (Balachandran SMD 0.44, 0.21-0.66; Lopez leg-press power SMD 0.55, 0.19-0.92; Straight 2016 Δ 0.62 vs 0.20 versus control). Strength (SMD 0.01) and mass (SMD 0.0004) did not differ. Orssatto 2020 found hypertrophy similar to moderate-velocity training (SMD 0.07, 7 studies) and greater than control (SMD 0.31).
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Adults 50+, mostly healthy 60-80-year-olds in about 12-week trials; not established for younger adults.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Equivalence for strength and mass means no detectable difference in low-certainty data, not proven equivalence.
**Sources:** [S104], [S107], [S108], [S109]

### How to prescribe power training
**Rule:** Template: 2-3 sets of 6-10 reps with a light-to-moderate load the client can move fast (ICFSR's 40-60% 1RM; without a measured 1RM, choose it by bar or body speed and effort), well away from failure (about RPE 5-7 as a practical heuristic). Intent: up as fast as safely possible, 2-3 s down. Stop the set when speed visibly drops. Prioritise leg press, sit-to-stand and step-ups. Put power sets early in the session, after the warm-up and before heavy or fatiguing work.
**Evidence:** ICFSR 2021 defines power training as a fast concentric phase with a controlled eccentric, lower-limb focused, at 40-60% 1RM, "always avoiding concentric failure". Peak power occurs at about 30-45% 1RM for the upper body and 60-70% for the leg extensors, and power improved similarly at 20%, 50% and 80% in one dose-response trial. Trials in Balachandran mostly used 12 weeks, 2 sessions per week, 3 sets of 8-10 reps at 40-70% with a 2-3 s eccentric. Byrne 2016 (44 studies) found power a marginally better predictor of function than strength.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Adults 60+, including frail older adults per ICFSR.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** The best load is uncertain; heavier loads gave more strength and endurance and may lower injury risk by reducing end-range velocity. Trials were short and low quality.
**Sources:** [S104], [S110], [S111]

### Safety of power training
**Rule:** Screen for shoulder, knee and hernia history before adding explosive work. Teach the movement slowly first and add speed only once technique is consistent. Prefer machines and sit-to-stand for beginners. For clients with arthritis, consider moderate-to-heavier loads. Avoid fast overhead pressing and ballistic movements with shoulder or knee complaints or hernia. Ask about pain every session and regress load or speed if joint pain appears.
**Evidence:** Balachandran 2022 found low, similar adverse-event rates: 3.27 (95% CI 1.76-6.09) versus 2.08 (0.99-4.36) per 1,000 person-sessions, mainly arthritis flare-ups and soreness, more in low-functioning participants, with no serious events. Tschopp 2011 concluded "no firm conclusion can be made for safety". ICFSR 2021 flags possible tendon, cartilage and hernia issues, advises screening for rotator-cuff and meniscus tears, and caution with heavy repetitive and overhead loading in rotator-cuff disease.
**Grade:** emerging
**Applies to:** Adults 60+; adverse-event reporting was poor and trials small and short.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Meta-analyses reach different safety conclusions; rare injuries cannot be excluded.
**Sources:** [S104], [S105], [S110]

### Frail older adults
**Rule:** For frail or very old clients, combine slow-controlled and fast-intent reps at moderate loads (40-60% 1RM) 2-3 times a week inside a programme that also trains balance and gait. Expect function to improve versus doing nothing, but do not promise power training beats ordinary training in this group.
**Evidence:** Sklivas 2022 (14 RCTs) found power training improved function versus control in prefrail, frail or diseased people (ES 0.41, 0.12-0.71) but was not clearly better than conventional training (ES 0.10, p=0.061). ICFSR 2021 recommends explosive training "should always be included, if possible". A 12-week RCT in frail nonagenarians (n=24, mean age 92) with power training plus balance and gait retraining improved TUG, chair rise, balance, power, strength and muscle CSA and reduced falls.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Prefrail and frail older adults, mostly mobile at baseline; small trials.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** The guideline wording is stronger than the comparative meta-analysis.
**Sources:** [S110], [S113], [S114]

### Vivifrail stratification
**Rule:** For anyone who looks frail or very deconditioned, run an SPPB (balance, 4 m gait speed, 5 chair rises) at intake and every 4-8 weeks, and choose the starting level from the score. Begin with a load the client can lift about 30 times with some effort, add the smallest increments available, and pair each session with balance work plus near-daily walking.
**Evidence:** Vivifrail bands SPPB as disability 0-3, frailty 4-6, prefrailty 7-9, robust 10-12. The RCT version used resistance, balance and flexibility 3 days per week and walking 5 days per week, starting at about 30 reps "with some effort" and progressing in 0.5 kg steps. In 188 frail or prefrail adults over 75 with mild cognitive impairment or dementia, 12 weeks improved SPPB by 1.40 points versus usual care (95% CI 0.82-1.98), with no exercise-related adverse effects.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Frail or prefrail adults over 75 and institutionalised older adults; robust older gym-goers (SPPB 10-12) use standard progressive training.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** One multicentre RCT plus guideline endorsement; falls, admissions and mortality did not differ.
**Sources:** [S110], [S115]

### Falls prevention needs balance work
**Rule:** Every programme for clients 60+, or with a falls history, includes balance work that genuinely challenges stability on at least 3 days per week (home sessions count). Aim for about 3 h per week of total exercise and continue beyond 12 weeks. Resistance and power training are added to balance work, not used instead of it. Never tell a client that lifting alone will stop them falling.
**Evidence:** In community-dwelling people 60+, exercise reduces fall rate by 23% (RaR 0.77, 0.71-0.83; high certainty); balance and functional exercise by 24% (RaR 0.76; high certainty); multicomponent programmes by 34% (RaR 0.66; moderate certainty). Resistance exercise alone has uncertain effects. Programmes that challenge balance and total more than 3 h per week reduced falls by 39% (IRR 0.61). The World Falls Guidelines recommend balance-challenging and functional exercise 3+ times weekly for at least 12 weeks (1A), with progressive resistance training and/or Tai Chi added when feasible (1B), and say walking alone is unlikely to prevent falls.
**Grade:** strong
**Applies to:** Community-dwelling adults 60+ (Cochrane mean age 76, 77% women). No clear falls effect of exercise alone in residential care, after stroke or right after hospital discharge.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Effects on fall-related fractures are low certainty. The guidelines still recommend supervised exercise in long-term care (1B). See also the next rule.
**Sources:** [S116], [S117], [S118]

### Resistance or power training alone for falls
**Rule:** Frame strength and power work as building capacity, and balance and functional training as the proven falls intervention. Programme both.
**Evidence:** Cochrane (108 RCTs, 23,407 people 60+) is "uncertain of the effect of resistance exercise (without balance and functional exercises), dance, or walking on the rate of falls". Claims that power training reduces falls rest largely on small multicomponent trials in frail, institutionalised very old adults (for example n=24, mean age 92, power plus balance and gait retraining), where the power component cannot be isolated.
**Grade:** emerging
**Applies to:** Older adults 60+; the power-falls trials generalise poorly to robust older adults.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** ICFSR 2021 states combining power and slow-velocity training "reduces the incidence of falls" and that resistance exercise alone reduced falls in some trials; these rest on small or multicomponent trials. Strength still matters for people too weak to do balance work safely.
**Sources:** [S110], [S114], [S116], [S117]

### Integrating and progressing balance training
**Rule:** Put 5-15 min of balance work in each session, after the warm-up or between strength sets. Progress along one axis at a time: base of support, then vision or surface, then perturbation, then dual-task. Always keep a stable support within reach. Move up once the client holds the current level steadily for the target time.
**Evidence:** ICFSR 2021 recommends balance training 1-7 days per week, 1-2 sets of 4-10 exercises covering static and dynamic postures, progressed by narrowing the base of support, reducing sensory input, perturbing the centre of mass and dual-tasking (examples: tandem walking, single-leg stance, stepping over objects, heel and toe standing, turning, Tai Chi). People who cannot support their body weight should start with resistance, power and balance before weight-bearing aerobic work.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Adults 65+, frail to robust; the progression ladder is expert consensus.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Vestibular, visual or neurological conditions need individual adaptation.
**Sources:** [S110]

### Weekly template for older adults
**Rule:** Default older-adult week: 2 full-body sessions to start, progressing to 3 if recovery allows, each with (a) a warm-up, (b) 2-3 power sets on 1-2 leg-dominant moves, (c) 4-6 strength exercises at 8-12 reps at about 2-3 RIR (about 1-2 RIR after about 8 weeks without symptoms; ICFSR's Borg 15-18 assumes supervision), (d) 10 min of progressive balance work. Add short home balance and sit-to-stand practice on other days and walking most days. Re-test SPPB, gait speed or chair rise periodically (every 4-8 weeks is a practical choice, not a sourced interval).
**Evidence:** Combining ICFSR 2021 and the World Falls Guidelines: resistance 2-3 days per week, 1-3 sets of 8-12 reps, 30-40% progressing to 70-80% 1RM; power at 40-60% 1RM avoiding concentric failure; balance 1-7 days per week with falls-oriented work ≥3 times weekly for 12+ weeks; aerobic 3-7 days per week, 20-60 min at Borg 12-14, starting at 5-10 min if deconditioned. ICFSR states combining power with slow-velocity training optimises function and recommends multicomponent training for frailty.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Adults 65+, robust to frail; extrapolated to deconditioned 50-65-year-olds.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Each component is evidence-based but the package is consensus. ICFSR suggests sedentary adults may adhere better starting with one modality, and that in frail people strength/power, then balance, then aerobic may be the best sequence.
**Sources:** [S110], [S117]

### Resistance training and bone in men and adults 65+
**Rule:** For men and for adults 65+, present progressive resistance training (ideally with some weight-bearing or impact work, and heavier loads rather than more volume) as likely to give a small hip benefit; do not promise spine gains. Show the hip estimate as uncertain.
**Evidence:** In 14 RCTs of progressive resistance training in adults 65+, femur/hip BMD rose by 2.77% (95% CI 0.44 to 5.10, p=0.02) but lumbar spine BMD did not change significantly (1.60%, 95% CI -1.44 to 4.63); BMD heterogeneity was very high (I2 98.6%), and combined resistance plus weight-bearing exercise, lower volumes and higher loads favoured BMD. In men, a 2022 meta-analysis of RCTs longer than 24 weeks found a small femoral neck benefit (g = 0.21, 95% CI 0.03 to 0.40) and no significant lumbar spine benefit (g = 0.10, 95% CI -0.07 to 0.26); the authors recommend ground- and joint-reaction force exercise for the femoral neck.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Adults 65+ (men and women) and adult men, in trials of about 6 months or more.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** An earlier meta-analysis in men pooled only 3 femoral neck and 5 spine effect sizes and judged the evidence insufficient; it has a corrigendum whose content could not be opened. In postmenopausal women, combination programmes did improve the spine (see "Expected bone gains are modest" in cardio-health.md).
**Sources:** [S792], [S793], [S794]

### Low bone mass: heavy loading only when screened and supervised
**Rule:** Progressive loading is reasonable for clients with low bone mass who have no vertebral or multiple low-trauma fractures. Start with lighter loads and technique, progress gradually, avoid loaded spinal flexion, and keep the heavy LIFTMOR-style protocol (over 85% 1RM plus impact) for screened clients under close supervision. Anyone with known osteoporotic fractures sees a clinician or physiotherapist before loading starts.
**Evidence:** In the LIFTMOR RCT (101 postmenopausal women aged 65 +/- 5, T-score below -1.0, screened), 8 months of supervised twice-weekly 30-minute training (5 sets of 5 at over 85% 1RM, plus impact) beat a home programme for lumbar spine (2.9 vs -1.2%) and femoral neck BMD (0.3 vs -1.9%), with one minor adverse event (lower back spasm). A follow-up found no new vertebral fractures and better standing thoracic kyphosis (-6.7 vs -1.6 degrees). The UK consensus finds little evidence of significant harm from physical activity, judges that benefits generally outweigh risks, and advises people with vertebral or multiple low-trauma fractures to keep impact to about brisk-walking level.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Screened postmenopausal women with low to very low bone mass under close supervision (one 8-month trial); not men and not people with prior vertebral fractures.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** LIFTMOR is heavier than the UK consensus dose (8-12 RM), which the consensus prefers as easier to deliver. PubMed lists an erratum for the 2018 trial report whose content could not be opened; the current abstract carries the numbers above.
**Sources:** [S790], [S791], [S784]

## Sex differences and female physiology

### Same relative hypertrophy for women and men
**Rule:** Do not cut hypertrophy expectations or change the programme because a client is a woman. Expect the same percentage gain from baseline for the same programme, and judge progress against her own baseline, not male absolute numbers.
**Evidence:** Refalo 2025 (29 studies, ages 18-45) found a relative difference of only +0.69% (95% HDI -1.50% to 2.88%), unaffected by experience. Roberts 2020 (10 studies) found no sex difference (ES 0.07 ± 0.06, p=0.31). In older adults, Jones 2021 (over 50, g=0.10, p=0.16) and Hawley 2023 (60+) found no sex difference in relative size.
**Grade:** strong
**Applies to:** Healthy adults 18-45, consistent in people over 50 or 60; mostly short studies in untrained or recreationally trained people; few data on highly trained women.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Absolute gains favour men somewhat (next rule). Type I fibre hypertrophy slightly favoured men in Refalo. Refalo and Roberts share an author and likely overlap. Between-person variation exceeds the average sex difference.
**Sources:** [S139], [S140], [S141], [S142]

### Absolute gains and expectations
**Rule:** Set goals for women as percentage change and give them the same programme. Tell female clients, especially for upper-body muscles, that absolute gains in cm or kg may be smaller than a man's because they start from a smaller baseline, not because they respond worse. Do not add volume just to close an absolute gap.
**Evidence:** Refalo 2025 found absolute gains favouring men (SMD 0.19, 95% HDI 0.11-0.28), driven by upper-body regions. Jones 2021 found larger absolute size gains in men over 50 (g=0.45). Hawley 2023 found more absolute whole-body fat-free mass gain in men (ES 0.18) but not more relative gain.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Healthy adults including older adults; concentrated in upper-body measures.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Hawley found no sex difference in absolute limb size, disagreeing with Jones and Refalo on that measure.
**Sources:** [S139], [S141], [S142]

### Relative strength gains by sex
**Rule:** Expect fast early percentage gains in upper-body strength in novice women, and in older women at least as good relative strength gains as men. Use these gains to motivate and progress load as soon as rep targets are met. Give older women and men the same progressive prescription and never lower intensity because a client is an older woman.
**Evidence:** Roberts 2020 found larger relative upper-body strength gains in women (ES -0.60 ± 0.16, p=0.002, I²=72%) and no significant lower-body difference (ES -0.21, p=0.20). In adults over 50, Jones 2021 (30 studies, n=1410) found relative lower-body strength favouring women (g=-0.21) and absolute strength favouring men; Hawley 2023 (60+, 36 studies, n=1305) found larger relative upper- (ES -0.46) and lower-body (ES -0.24) gains in women, larger absolute gains in men, and no sex difference in physical performance.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Young to middle-aged adults (mostly short-term, many untrained women) and healthy adults over 50 or 60 (moderate study quality).
**Individual variation / conflicts:** High heterogeneity; the young-adult effect may reflect neural learning or short untrained samples. Jones and Hawley disagree on whether the older women's relative upper-body advantage is significant.
**Sources:** [S140], [S141], [S142]

### One prescription for both sexes
**Rule:** Default to one evidence-based prescription for everyone (load, sets, frequency, proximity to failure, rest). Individualise by training status, goals, recovery, preferences and response, not by sex. When giving numbers such as sets per week, note the data come mostly from young men and monitor the individual's response. Treat claims that women "need" much higher volume or shorter rests as a hypothesis to test on the individual.
**Evidence:** ACSM 2026 gives one set of recommendations for healthy adults and does not split them by sex. Roberts 2020 found similar hypertrophy and lower-body strength in men and women on the same protocol. A women-only meta-analysis (Hagstrom 2020, 24 studies) found large strength effects (upper g=1.70, lower g=1.40; 1.07 and 0.52 after trim-and-fill) and a medium hypertrophy effect (g=0.52), with frequency and volume moderating strength. Pelland 2026 was 79.1% male; of 570 hypertrophy studies in young adults (Dias 2026), 72.2% recruited only men and 11.0% only women. In lifters with bench experience, women's soreness and estimated 1RM recovered like men's over 72 h despite roughly twice the reps; in an 8-week trial there were no significant between-group differences by velocity-loss protocol.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Healthy adults 18+; ACSM does not stratify by sex or mention athletes.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** ACSM is sex-agnostic rather than testing sex. The sex-neutral default rests mainly on Roberts 2020. Hagstrom detected publication bias. Faster between-set recovery in women and a velocity-loss signal (Rissanen 2022, not significant) suggest women might tolerate more volume, but this is untested; the volume and rest evidence for sex differences is emerging.
**Sources:** [S001], [S003], [S140], [S143], [S144], [S147], [S149]

### %1RM tables and repeated-set fatigue
**Rule:** Use the same %1RM-to-reps tables for women and men, chosen by exercise, and set working loads by RIR or rep targets rather than %1RM alone. Do not assume a woman will show the same rep drop-off as a man across several sets; prescribe multi-set work by a target RIR (or rep range plus RIR) and let her do the extra reps she can.
**Evidence:** A meta-regression of 952 reps-to-failure tests (7289 individuals, 269 studies) found little influence of sex, age or training status, with separate tables for bench press and leg press and between-individual SD shrinking as %1RM rises. Women are usually less fatigable than men at the same relative intensity in isometric and some dynamic tasks, with the difference narrowing as load rises toward 90% 1RM. In experienced lifters doing 5-rep bench sets at 75% 1RM with 90 s rest, women completed 58.3 ± 27.3 total reps versus 29.6 ± 10.6, attributed to faster between-set recovery; velocity loss across sets was 9% in women versus 15% in men.
**Grade:** emerging
**Applies to:** The tables: single sets to failure, groups 66% male, mostly healthy and under 59 (moderate evidence). The fatigue findings: young trained adults in small acute bench-press studies (n=24 and 42); uncertain for other exercises, heavier loads, beginners and older women.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** No meta-analysis for dynamic fatigue; the difference is task-specific. Between-person variability is large (SD about 27 reps in women).
**Sources:** [S145], [S146], [S147], [S148]

### RIR targets and accuracy in women
**Rule:** Use the same RIR targets for women and men: mostly 1-3 RIR, failure optional. Teach and check RIR estimation in beginners of both sexes.
**Evidence:** Trained men and women (12 each) predicted 1 and 3 RIR on bench at 75% 1RM with an absolute error of 0.65 ± 0.78 reps, unrelated to sex. In an 8-week within-participant leg-extension trial (11 men, 8 women), stopping 1-3 reps short gave hypertrophy and 1RM comparable to failure (thickness difference -0.019 cm, 1RM 0.198 kg). Fatigue and negative perceptions rose as sets approached failure in both sexes.
**Grade:** emerging
**Applies to:** Young trained adults, n=19-24; one exercise each for accuracy and adaptation.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Few women; a sex-specific effect cannot be ruled out.
**Sources:** [S148], [S150], [S151]

### Menstrual-cycle phase
**Rule:** Do not schedule, cancel or periodise sessions by cycle phase, and do not tell clients they build more muscle in one phase. Keep consistent progressive training throughout the cycle. If a client feels worse around menstruation, autoregulate on the day (lower load or volume, adjust RIR) based on her symptoms, and invite her to log symptoms if she wants.
**Evidence:** McNulty 2020 (78 studies, low quality) found at most a trivial reduction in the early follicular phase (ES -0.06, 95% CrI -0.16 to 0.04) and advised a personalised approach. Niering 2024 (22 studies, 3-7 per outcome) reported larger effects favouring the late follicular phase (isometric SMD 0.60, dynamic 0.14) and ovulation for isokinetic strength (0.39). Thompson 2020 found conflicting, small, limited training data. A 2023 umbrella review called it "premature" to say hormonal fluctuations appreciably affect performance or adaptation. A 2025 trial with rigorous phase verification (n=12) found no difference in myofibrillar protein synthesis between late follicular and mid-luteal phases (1.52 vs 1.46 %/day) and no phase effect on breakdown.
**Grade:** emerging
**Applies to:** Eumenorrheic women aged roughly 18-38; not users of hormonal contraception, pregnant, peri- or postmenopausal women.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Genuine disagreement: Kissow 2022 (narrative review) and a few small trials favour follicular-phase training. Responses and symptoms are highly individual.
**Sources:** [S152], [S153], [S154], [S155], [S156], [S157]

### Oral contraceptives
**Rule:** Do not change the programme because a client uses oral contraceptives. Never advise starting or stopping contraception for training purposes; that is a medical decision for the client and her doctor.
**Evidence:** Nolan 2024 (8 studies, 325 participants) found no significant effect of oral contraceptive use on hypertrophy (0.01, 95% CI -0.11 to 0.13), power (-0.04, -0.93 to 0.84) or strength (0.10, -0.08 to 0.28). An earlier review found mixed small studies on acute recovery, in both directions.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Premenopausal women using oral contraceptive pills; no data on other hormonal methods.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** The power estimate is very imprecise; possible effects of pill androgenicity come from small studies.
**Sources:** [S155], [S158]

## Screening

### Preparticipation screening
**Rule:** Before the first session, ask: (1) Have you been exercising regularly (30 min, 3 days per week, for 3 months)? (2) Do you have diagnosed heart, vascular, cerebrovascular or kidney disease, or diabetes? (3) Do you get any of the listed symptoms? Any symptom: stop and get medical clearance before exercise. Known disease and inactive: medical clearance before starting any programme, then start at light-to-moderate intensity. Known disease, active and no symptoms: continue at moderate intensity; get clearance before progressing to vigorous. Otherwise start light-to-moderate and progress gradually. If a symptom appears at any time, stop and get clearance. Healthy, asymptomatic clients do not need medical clearance. For resting blood-pressure bands, apply "Resting BP thresholds" in recovery-habits.md.
**Evidence:** The ACSM 2015 algorithm screens on current activity, known cardiovascular, metabolic or renal disease and/or signs or symptoms, and desired intensity; risk-factor counting is no longer part of it. Symptoms include possibly ischaemic chest, neck, jaw or arm pain; shortness of breath at rest or with mild exertion; dizziness or syncope; orthopnoea or paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnoea; ankle oedema; palpitations or tachycardia; intermittent claudication; known heart murmur; unusual fatigue or breathlessness with usual activities.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Adults of all ages; targets exercise-related cardiac events, not musculoskeletal or falls risk.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Not yet validated against cardiac events. Applied to US adults 40+ it would refer 54.2% before any exercise (70.9% of those 70+). The 11th-edition criteria cut referrals by 41% versus the 9th in a small 2025 sample. Uncontrolled hypertension and unstable conditions still warrant medical input.
**Sources:** [S119], [S120], [S121]

### Fall-risk screening
**Rule:** At intake for clients 60+, ask about falls in the last 12 months (optionally: do you feel unsteady, do you worry about falling), and time a 4 m walk and/or TUG. Gait speed below 0.8 m/s or TUG over 15 s makes gait and balance work the priority and the coach suggests a professional gait and balance assessment. Any high-risk criterion means referral to a doctor for a multifactorial falls assessment before progressing training. Re-ask yearly.
**Evidence:** The World Falls Guidelines recommend routinely asking older adults about falls (1A), at least yearly (expert recommendation). "Have you fallen in the last 12 months?" is highly specific but has low sensitivity (43% at 65-74, 67% over 85). Gait speed is the recommended stratification test (1A), cut-off <0.8 m/s; TUG >15 s is an alternative (1B). High risk is a fall with injury, 2 or more falls in 12 months, known frailty, inability to get up for at least an hour, or suspected transient loss of consciousness, and warrants a multifactorial assessment. Intermediate-risk adults are offered targeted strength and balance exercise.
**Grade:** strong
**Applies to:** Community-dwelling adults 65+ (60+ in low- and middle-income settings).
**Individual variation / conflicts:** The TUG cut-off evidence is less consistent than gait speed; the yearly interval is consensus.
**Sources:** [S117]

## Conflicts and open questions

- **Frequency and hypertrophy:** Schoenfeld 2016 favoured twice-weekly training; the larger 2019 analysis, ACSM 2026 and Pelland 2026 find no independent effect when volume is equated. For strength, Grgic 2018 found no volume-equated frequency effect while Pelland found a positive slope ([S001], [S003], [S011], [S012]).
- **Volume ceilings:** Baz-Valle 2022 suggests 12-20 weekly sets for trained men, ACSM 2026 cites a plateau around 18-20, the continuous models show no hard ceiling, and per-session cut-offs come only from a non-peer-reviewed preprint. Treat all caps as soft ([S001], [S003], [S007], [S020]).
- **Volume and strength:** Ralston 2017 finds a small graded strength benefit; Schoenfeld 2019 found none across 1, 3 and 5 sets in trained men ([S006], [S008]).
- **Set counting:** the 2019 expert review provisionally counted 1:1; Pelland 2026 favours 0.5, a weight that was assumed rather than estimated ([S003], [S072], [S073]).
- **Single- versus multi-joint literature:** much of it comes from one research group with retracted papers and an expression of concern; rows versus pulldowns for the biceps conflict directly ([S080], [S081], [S082], [S083], [S084], [S085], [S086], [S087], [S088]).
- **Lengthened partials:** popular claims of superiority outrun trials that show equivalence; formal equivalence in the largest trial was not established at its pre-set alpha ([S053], [S054], [S055], [S056], [S057], [S058]).
- **Training to failure:** a small hypertrophy edge in trained people (Grgic 2022, Refalo 2023) versus a roughly linear RIR benefit (Robinson 2024) versus ACSM 2026 "not necessary" ([S001], [S024], [S025], [S027]).
- **Rest intervals:** ACSM 2009 (1-2 min for hypertrophy, 3-5 min for heavy strength) versus newer data leaning to >60-90 s and ACSM 2026 judging the evidence insufficient or null ([S001], [S037], [S038], [S039], [S040]).
- **Periodisation for strength:** small benefits in Williams 2017 and Moesgaard 2022, none in Harries 2015, undeterminable per ACSM 2026 ([S001], [S044], [S046], [S047]).
- **Older adults' frequency:** Borde 2015 (2 per week best for strength, 3 for morphology) versus Kneffel 2021 (no frequency effect on hypertrophy) ([S017], [S018]).
- **Deloads and "muscle memory":** popular calendar deloads and myonuclear "muscle memory" outrun human evidence ([S068], [S069], [S134], [S136]).
- **Concurrent training:** whole-muscle analyses show no interference while Wilson 2012 and fibre-level analyses suggest small costs; training status, sex and modality moderators disagree between meta-analyses; ACSM 2026 rates the evidence insufficient ([S001], [S089], [S090], [S091], [S092], [S093], [S094], [S095], [S100]).
- **Power training and falls:** ICFSR 2021 says power plus slow-velocity training reduces falls; Cochrane is uncertain about resistance-only programmes ([S110], [S114], [S116]).
- **Power training safety:** Balachandran 2022 finds low, similar adverse-event rates; Tschopp 2011 says safety is undetermined ([S104], [S105]).
- **Menstrual cycle:** McNulty 2020 finds trivial effects, Niering 2024 larger ones from few studies; cycle-syncing programmes are unsupported by the umbrella review and mechanistic data ([S152], [S153], [S154], [S156], [S157]).
- **Women and volume:** popular advice that women need much more volume or shorter rests rests on acute fatigue data, not training trials ([S147], [S149]).
- **Screening over-referral:** the ACSM algorithm is unvalidated against cardiac events and would refer most adults over 70 ([S119], [S120], [S121]).

## When to refer out

- Any cardiovascular, metabolic or renal symptom at intake or during training: stop and refer to a doctor; known disease with inactivity needs medical clearance first ([S119]).
- Any high-risk falls criterion (injurious fall, 2 or more falls in 12 months, frailty, long lie, suspected blackout): refer for a multifactorial falls assessment; gait speed <0.8 m/s or TUG >15 s: suggest a professional gait and balance assessment ([S117]).
- Frail or multimorbid clients: program only with clinical supervision ([S070], [S110]).
- Shoulder, knee or hernia history before explosive training: screen, and refer when tears or active disease are suspected ([S110]).
- Contraception questions: refer to the client's doctor ([S158]).
