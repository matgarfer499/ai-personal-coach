# Cardio and general health

> Part of the coach's knowledge base. Loaded on demand by the coach commands (/plan, /review, /menu, /setup). Rules, not a literature review. Source IDs refer to knowledge/sources.md. Evidence checked on 2026-10-24.

## How to read this file

Each rule carries a grade. **Strong**: consistent evidence from guidelines, large meta-analyses or several good RCTs; apply by default. **Moderate**: supported by meta-analyses or trials with real limits (short duration, small samples, observational designs, indirect populations); apply, but adjust to the client. **Emerging**: single studies, small or uncontrolled samples, conflicting results or reasoning from mechanism; use as a reasonable default and do not promise outcomes. Most evidence comes from population cohorts, lab trials and clinical groups, not from ordinary gym-goers, so the "Applies to" line says how far each rule stretches. Where a rule contains a practical inference that the sources did not test, it is labelled as such.

## Key rules at a glance

- Weekly baseline for every client: 150-300 min moderate or 75-150 min vigorous aerobic activity (or a mix), plus strength training on at least 2 days. More than 300 min is optional extra. (strong) [S016], [S396]
- Some activity beats none: start a sedentary client at a level they can repeat (10-20 min walks, building toward 150 min/week), progress gradually, and screen with the ACSM 2015 algorithm: inactive with known cardiovascular, metabolic or renal disease, or anyone with symptoms, gets medical clearance before starting; healthy asymptomatic people need no clearance for light or moderate activity. (strong) [S016], [S396], [S119]
- Treat cardiorespiratory fitness as a core health outcome; the biggest gain is moving an unfit client out of the bottom fitness group. (strong) [S416], [S417], [S418], [S450]
- Aim for at least about 7,000 steps/day; benefit starts low, and extra longevity benefit above about 8,000-10,000 steps/day is unproven. (moderate) [S398], [S399], [S400], [S401]
- Desk-bound clients: break up sitting and push total moderate activity toward about 60 min/day if they sit 8 h or more. (moderate) [S403], [S404], [S016]
- Program resistance training for every client on at least 2 days/week (WHO strong recommendation); the observational finding that 30-60 min/week captures most of the mortality association is context, not a cap. (strong) [S016], [S415], [S419], [S396]
- HIIT and SIT raise VO2max about as well as or slightly better than moderate continuous training; the advantage is time, not a much larger effect. (strong) [S481], [S482], [S483], [S484]
- Exercise without dietary change moves the scale only about 1.5-3.5 kg; meaningful fat loss needs an energy deficit. (strong) [S407], [S408], [S409], [S563], [S564], [S538]
- Wearable and machine calorie figures are unreliable: do not let clients eat back more than about half of them; set intake from the 2-4 week weight trend. (strong) [S543], [S440], [S544], [S545]
- Use the 0-10 effort scale (moderate 5-6, vigorous 7-8; Borg 6-20 moderate 12-13, vigorous 14-17, the ACSM 2011 bands) and the talk test alongside any HR zone. (moderate) [S433], [S396], [S776]
- Use the ACSM 2011 bands as approximate HR and RPE zones (moderate 40-59% HRR, 64-76% HRmax, RPE 12-13; vigorous 60-89% HRR, 77-95% HRmax, RPE 14-17) and check them against RPE and the talk test; %HRR beats %HRmax when resting HR is known. (strong) [S396], [S776], [S777]
- For bone, combine progressive resistance training on 2-3 days a week with moderate impact on most days; walking alone does little for bone density, and gains are a few percent at most. (strong) [S784], [S785], [S786], [S789]
- Wrist HR is good enough for steady cardio (about ±7 bpm per reading). (strong) [S438], [S439], [S440]
- Do not trust wrist HR for intervals, cycling or resistance work; use a chest strap or RPE. (moderate) [S438], [S441], [S442]
- Age-predicted HRmax can be off by 20+ bpm; never use formula-based HR zones for clients on beta-blockers. (moderate) [S427], [S428], [S436], [S437], [S445], [S446], [S447]
- Moderate amounts of cardio do not meaningfully blunt muscle size or maximal strength gains; lift first if strength is the priority. (moderate) [S089], [S092], [S100], [S098]
- Before vigorous work, a previously inactive client builds up over several weeks; habitually active healthy clients can train vigorously without routine clearance. (strong) [S119], [S016], [S531]
- All-out sprints (SIT, REHIT) are not for sedentary beginners: they need a PAR-Q+ with all "no" answers and at least 4 weeks of regular moderate cardio without symptoms; anyone over 45 or with any PAR-Q+ "yes" gets a professional review before vigorous or maximal work. (emerging) [S641], [S119], [S432]

## Weekly activity targets and patterns

### Weekly baseline dose
**Rule:** Default weekly health baseline for every client: 150-300 min of moderate cardio, or 75-150 min vigorous, or a mix (1 vigorous minute counts as about 2 moderate minutes), plus at least 2 full-body strength sessions. Use 150 moderate or 75 vigorous min as the minimum and push toward 300/150 when the client has time. Gym programmes cover the strength part; make sure cardio minutes are also scheduled or tracked (brisk walking counts). Treat more than 300 min as optional extra benefit, not a requirement. Let the client choose modalities they enjoy, because adherence beats optimisation.
**Evidence:** WHO 2020: all adults should do 150-300 min/week moderate or 75-150 min/week vigorous aerobic activity, or an equivalent combination, plus muscle-strengthening activity at moderate or greater intensity for all major muscle groups on 2 or more days/week (strong recommendations). Going beyond 300 min moderate or 150 min vigorous gives additional benefit, rated conditional. ACSM 2011: at least 150 min/week moderate (at least 30 min/day on at least 5 days) or at least 75 min/week vigorous (at least 20 min/day on at least 3 days), for 500-1000 MET-min/week; resistance training for each major muscle group on 2-3 days/week, neuromotor (balance, agility, coordination) work on 2-3 days/week, and flexibility on at least 2 days/week (60 s total per exercise). ACSM states that gradual progression may reduce exercise risk and that enjoyable exercise and behaviour-change strategies improve adherence.
**Grade:** strong
**Applies to:** Apparently healthy adults 18-64; WHO also applies it to older adults and people with chronic conditions or disability, within their abilities (with medical clearance where indicated for clinical populations). The evidence is mostly from population cohorts, not from gym-goers in particular.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** The upper ranges and the extra benefit above 300 min/week are rated conditional by WHO, and the benefit curve flattens at higher volumes. Adjust plans to habitual activity, health status and goals, as ACSM states.
**Sources:** [S016], [S396]

### Start low, progress gradually, screen by symptoms
**Rule:** Never make the full guideline a prerequisite. A sedentary beginner starts with 10-20 min walks (or 1-2 short strength sessions) and builds toward 150 min/week, adding frequency or duration before intensity; 20-60 min sessions on 3-5 days/week is the target once the habit is established. Screen every client with the ACSM 2015 algorithm: (1) currently inactive with known cardiovascular, metabolic or renal disease, or any symptoms (chest pain, unusual breathlessness, fainting, palpitations): medical clearance before starting any programme, then start light to moderate; (2) currently active with known disease and no symptoms: continue moderate work, clearance before progressing to vigorous; (3) symptoms at any time: stop and get clearance. Healthy, asymptomatic people do not need medical clearance to start light or moderate activity; over-screening them is itself a barrier.
**Evidence:** WHO 2020: some physical activity is better than none; people not meeting the targets should start small and gradually increase frequency, intensity and duration. Pre-exercise medical clearance is generally unnecessary for people without contraindications starting light or moderate activity (up to the demands of brisk walking). ACSM 2015 (Riebe): preparticipation screening rests on current physical activity, signs or symptoms and/or known cardiovascular, metabolic or renal disease, and desired intensity; risk-factor profiling is no longer included. ACSM 2011: adults who cannot meet the targets still benefit from smaller amounts; gradual progression plus education about cardiac warning signs may reduce exercise risk; diagnostic exercise testing is not recommended as universal screening. The 2023 PAR-Q+ starts cleared people at 20-60 min of low-to-moderate exercise, 3-5 days/week, progressing toward 150 min/week or more.
**Grade:** strong
**Applies to:** All adults, including older adults. The no-clearance guidance applies only to people without symptoms or known disease. The 10-20 min walk starting dose is a practical choice for very sedentary clients, below the PAR-Q+ starting range.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Screening needs vary by individual. People with symptoms or known disease need clinical evaluation, which is outside the coach's scope. The ACSM algorithm's predictive ability is not yet validated.
**Sources:** [S016], [S396], [S119], [S641]

### One or two sessions a week still count
**Rule:** For a client who can only commit to 1-2 short sessions/week, start there and treat it as a real health win, then build volume toward the weekly range. Never let "I can't reach 150 minutes" become a reason to do nothing.
**Evidence:** In pooled English and Scottish cohorts (n=63,591, age 40 or older), "insufficiently active" people reporting only 1-2 sessions/week had all-cause mortality HR 0.66 (0.62-0.72) vs inactive, essentially the same as weekend warriors (0.70) and the regularly active (0.65); CVD mortality HR 0.60, 0.60 and 0.59. WHO 2020 states that some activity is better than none and that people should start small and increase gradually.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Adults aged 40 or older in UK surveillance cohorts with self-reported activity; the WHO guidance covers all adults and older adults.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** The near-equal HR for below-guideline activity likely reflects self-report misclassification and residual confounding. The dose-response literature (e.g. Arem 2015) shows more benefit with more volume, so this should not be read as "more does not help".
**Sources:** [S016], [S514]

### Bouts of any length count
**Rule:** Count every minute of genuine moderate-to-vigorous activity toward the weekly total, including short bouts such as brisk stair climbs, 3-5 min fast walks, walking to the gym or carrying loads uphill. Use them as an adherence tool for busy clients, but still schedule some planned sessions, because fitness gains come from structured sessions.
**Evidence:** WHO 2020 dropped the 2010 requirement for bouts of at least 10 min: "MVPA bouts of any duration now count towards these recommendations", because cohort evidence links total volume, regardless of bout length, to outcomes including all-cause mortality. The systematic review for the 2018 US Physical Activity Guidelines Advisory Committee (29 articles) found that bouts under 10 min are associated with health outcomes, but that evidence is cross-sectional and from cohorts; the RCTs tested only bouts of 10 min or more.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** All adults and older adults, for health outcomes. The evidence is not about fitness or performance adaptations.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** The WHO recommendation is firm (guideline-level consensus), but the evidence base is observational. No RCTs have tested bouts under 10 min, so the equivalence of short and long bouts for fitness gains has not been established (see Exercise snacks).
**Sources:** [S016], [S397]

### Weekend warrior pattern: mortality
**Rule:** Plan the weekly aerobic dose by total minutes first; frequency is a logistics choice. If a client can only train 1-2 days/week, fit the full weekly dose into those days rather than telling them it does not count. Prefer 3 or more days when the schedule allows (better for skill, recovery and adherence), but never present 1-2 days as "not worth it".
**Evidence:** Kim 2025 (18 publications): vs inactive, all-cause mortality RR 0.76 (0.66-0.87) for weekend warriors (WW, guideline volume in 1-2 sessions) vs 0.71 (0.63-0.80) for regular activity; CVD mortality 0.74 vs 0.69; cancer mortality 0.81 vs 0.86. Zhang 2025 (21 studies): all-cause HR 0.77 (0.68-0.87) WW vs 0.70 (0.63-0.78) regular. In NHIS (n=350,978, mean age 41), WW vs regularly active at equal total MVPA: HR 1.08 (0.97-1.20) all-cause, 1.14 (0.85-1.53) CVD, 1.07 (0.87-1.31) cancer mortality.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Adults in population cohorts with self-reported leisure-time activity (cohorts aged 40 or older, NHIS mean age 41, UK Biobank about 62). Observational only; no RCT compares patterns on hard outcomes. "Weekend warrior" here means meeting guideline volume in 1-2 sessions, not one all-out session after a sedentary week.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Point estimates consistently favour regular activity slightly (e.g. dos Santos WW vs inactive HR 0.92, 0.83-1.02; regular 0.85), so a small advantage for spreading cannot be excluded. The two 2025 meta-analyses pool largely the same cohorts. WW groups are small, giving wide CIs.
**Sources:** [S509], [S510], [S511]

### Weekend warrior pattern: incident cardiovascular and other disease
**Rule:** Reassure time-constrained clients that a concentrated pattern still delivers cardiometabolic protection; the priority is reaching the weekly volume. Do not use "you only train on weekends" as a reason to cut volume or skip intensity.
**Evidence:** In UK Biobank (n=89,573, mean age 62, 1 week of wrist accelerometry), meeting 150 min/week MVPA or more with at least 50% of it in 1-2 days was associated with similarly lower risk vs inactive of incident AF (HR 0.78 vs 0.81 regular), MI (0.73 vs 0.65), heart failure (0.62 vs 0.64) and stroke (0.79 vs 0.83). Across 678 conditions, both patterns were associated with lower risk of more than 200 diseases (e.g. diabetes HR 0.57 vs 0.54, hypertension 0.77 vs 0.72, obesity 0.55 vs 0.44), and no condition differed significantly between patterns in direct comparison.
**Grade:** emerging
**Applies to:** Middle-aged and older UK adults (mean 62, 56% women), largely White; activity measured for one week. 42% were classified WW, so most still moved on other days; this does not describe a fully sedentary weekday pattern. Observational.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Both papers come from the same group and cohort. The obesity HR was stronger for regular activity (0.44 vs 0.55), not significantly different in direct comparison, and open to reverse causation. At the median threshold (230 min/week or more), stroke associations were no longer significant (WW 0.89, 0.79-1.02; regular 0.87, 0.74-1.02). One-week measurement may misclassify habitual patterns.
**Sources:** [S512], [S513]

### Diminishing returns at high volume
**Rule:** For health and longevity, the big win is getting from inactive into the guideline range. Beyond roughly 2-4x the minimum (about 300-600 min moderate or 150-300 min vigorous per week), extra aerobic volume buys little additional mortality benefit. Above that, choose volume for performance, enjoyment or body-composition goals, and spend extra time on resistance training, recovery and nutrition rather than more cardio "for health".
**Evidence:** In 661,137 adults (6 pooled cohorts, median follow-up 14.2 years), mortality was 20% lower below the minimum (7.5 MET-h/week), 31% lower at 1-2x, 37% lower at 2-3x, and plateaued at 3-5x (HR 0.61), only modestly more than at the minimum (39% vs 31%). In 116,221 adults followed 30 years, near-maximal benefit came at about 150-300 min/week vigorous or 300-600 min/week moderate; 2-4x the minimum gave only 2-4% (vigorous) and 3-13% (moderate) further lower mortality, and among people already doing 300 min/week moderate or more, extra vigorous activity did not further lower mortality. WHO 2020 rates benefits beyond 300/150 min as conditional because "it is not possible to specify the physical activity levels where diminishing returns begin".
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Middle-aged and older adults in US and European cohorts (median age 62 in Arem; health professionals in Lee). Self-reported leisure-time activity.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Self-report overestimates volume, so thresholds are approximate. The Lee 2022 cohorts are less confounded but not representative. Fitness and body-composition goals may justify more volume even when mortality curves are flat.
**Sources:** [S521], [S522], [S016]

### No upper cap on mortality grounds
**Rule:** Do not impose an upper limit on weekly aerobic activity for ordinary adults on mortality grounds. Limit volume instead for recovery, injury risk, interference with strength goals and adherence. Clients who do very high endurance volumes (roughly more than 6-7 h/week of hard endurance for years) should know about the AF and coronary signals and report symptoms (see Safety).
**Evidence:** Arem 2015 found no evidence of harm even at 10x the recommended minimum or more (HR 0.69, 0.59-0.78 vs inactive). Lee 2022: 300 min/week vigorous or 600 min/week moderate or more "did not clearly show further lower... mortality or harm". In the Cooper Center cohort (n=26,724, 20.5 years), the 3000 MET-min/week or more group had the lowest all-cause mortality (HR 0.71, 0.60-0.83 vs under 500 MET-min/week), but its acute MI risk was not lower (HR 0.95, 0.72-1.25). WHO 2020 does not address athletes training for competition.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** General adult population, including high-volume recreational exercisers. Not designed for elite competitive athletes. The Cooper cohort is 72% men, mean age 54, self-reported activity.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** The point estimate at 10x (0.69) is slightly worse than the 3-5x plateau (0.61), which some read as a slight upturn, but the CIs overlap and it remains well below inactive. Few people sit at the extreme, so precision is low. Specific outcomes show signals at very high doses (no MI reduction in Cooper; AF risk in other data).
**Sources:** [S521], [S522], [S523], [S016]

### Muscle strength and strength training
**Rule:** Program resistance training for every client, including those whose main goal is health or fat loss, on at least 2 days/week, working all major muscle groups (WHO strong recommendation). As observational context, 30-60 min/week of strength work already captures most of the observed mortality association; more volume is fine for hypertrophy goals, but do not sell it as extra longevity benefit. Pair it with aerobic training rather than replacing aerobic training.
**Evidence:** Across 38 cohorts (1,907,580 apparently healthy people), higher handgrip strength was associated with lower all-cause mortality (HR 0.69, 0.64-0.74; women 0.60, men 0.69) and higher knee-extension strength with HR 0.86 (0.80-0.93). Independent of aerobic activity, muscle-strengthening activity was associated with 10-17% lower risk of all-cause mortality, CVD, total cancer, diabetes and lung cancer (Momma 2022, 16 cohorts). For all-cause mortality, CVD and total cancer the dose-response was J-shaped, with the largest reduction (about 10-20%) at about 30-60 min/week and an unclear effect of higher volumes; for diabetes it was L-shaped, with a large reduction at up to 60 min/week. Combining strength and aerobic activity was associated with lower all-cause, CVD and cancer mortality than neither. WHO 2020 recommends muscle-strengthening activity at moderate or greater intensity for all major muscle groups on 2 or more days/week as a strong recommendation (moderate-certainty evidence, no evidence of a dose-response with higher volumes); ACSM recommends each major muscle group on 2-3 days/week.
**Grade:** strong
**Applies to:** Adults 18 and over without severe health conditions (Momma) and apparently healthy adults including older adults (Garcia-Hermoso). Grip strength is partly a marker of overall health and frailty.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** The strong grade covers the 2 days/week recommendation (WHO). The mortality associations and the 30-60 min/week dose are observational only, so treat them as moderate-grade context. The J-shaped curve at high volumes is probably confounded or imprecise and should not be read as harm; do not tell lifters that more than 60 min/week is harmful. No association was found for some site-specific cancers (colon, kidney, bladder, pancreas). Strength partly reflects genetics and illness (reverse causation).
**Sources:** [S016], [S415], [S419], [S396]

### Older adults: multicomponent balance and strength
**Rule:** For clients aged 65 and over (and sensibly from about 60), program balance and functional strength work on at least 3 days/week. Examples: single-leg stance, tandem or backward walking, step-ups, sit-to-stand, carries. These can be built into strength sessions. Resistance training itself starts at 2 days/week and progresses to 3 if recovery allows; until then, meet the 3-day target with short balance and functional work on a non-lifting day. Progress balance difficulty safely, with support nearby.
**Evidence:** WHO 2020: on top of the adult aerobic and strength targets, adults 65 and over should do varied multicomponent activity emphasising functional balance and strength training, at moderate or greater intensity, on 3 or more days/week, to enhance functional capacity and prevent falls (strong recommendation). This now applies to all older adults, not only those with poor mobility. Moderate-certainty evidence indicates multicomponent activity may reduce the risk of fall-related injury.
**Grade:** strong
**Applies to:** Adults 65 and over, including those with chronic conditions. Extending it to people around 60 with low fitness is reasonable but outside what the guideline covers.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Type and dose depend on mobility, fall history and comorbidities. Frail clients or those with a history of falls may need physiotherapist input.
**Sources:** [S016]

## Steps, sitting and incidental activity

### Daily step targets
**Rule:** For general health, aim for at least about 7,000 steps/day (about 6,000-8,000 for clients aged 60 and over; 8,000-10,000 is a reasonable stretch goal for younger clients). Treat 10,000 as optional, not a threshold. Set targets relative to the client's measured baseline and add about 1,000 steps/day at a time.
**Evidence:** Paluch 2022 (15 cohorts, n=47,471, 3,013 deaths): vs the lowest quartile (median about 3,550 steps/day), mortality HR 0.60 at about 5,800, 0.55 at about 7,800 and 0.47 at about 10,900 steps/day; risk kept falling up to about 6,000-8,000 steps/day in adults 60 and over and about 8,000-10,000 under 60. Ding 2025 (57 studies, 35 cohorts; dose-response meta-analyses of 31 studies): 7,000 vs 2,000 steps/day was associated with 47% lower all-cause mortality (HR 0.53, 0.46-0.60; 14 studies), lower CVD incidence (HR 0.75), dementia (0.62; 2 studies) and depressive symptoms (0.78), with inflection points around 5,000-7,000 steps/day. GRADE certainty was moderate for most outcomes; the falls association (HR 0.72) was very low certainty.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Adults in general-population cohorts, many middle-aged and older. Observational, from prospective cohorts; no randomised evidence exists for step targets and hard outcomes.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Residual confounding and reverse causation are possible (sick people walk less). The two meta-analyses probably share cohorts. Ding had no age-specific analysis and most outcomes have few studies. Step counts differ by device (hip, wrist, pedometer).
**Sources:** [S398], [S399]

### Steps: benefit starts low, upper plateau disputed
**Rule:** Tell very inactive clients that going from about 2,000-3,000 to about 4,000-5,000 steps/day already matters. Increase by about 1,000/day at a time. Do not promise further mortality benefit above about 8,000-10,000 steps/day (about 6,000-8,000 for clients aged 60 or older). More steps are fine for energy expenditure or enjoyment, but evidence for extra longevity benefit is mixed.
**Evidence:** Stens 2023 (12 studies, n=111,309): significant mortality reduction from about 2,500 steps/day (2,517; aHR 0.92 vs 2,000); optimal dose about 8,800 steps/day for all-cause mortality (aHR 0.40) and about 7,100 for incident CVD (aHR 0.49). Paluch 2022: risk kept falling up to about 6,000-8,000 (60 and over) and 8,000-10,000 (under 60). Banach 2023 (17 cohorts, n=226,889): each extra 1,000 steps/day was associated with 15% lower all-cause mortality (HR 0.85, 0.81-0.91), with progressively lower risk up to 20,000 steps/day.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Adults in observational cohorts, mostly middle-aged and older; Banach also included patients at cardiovascular risk. Associations, not causation.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Paluch and Stens show a plateau (age-dependent in Paluch); Banach shows continued benefit to 20,000. All agree benefit starts at low counts. Stens found larger effects with hip-worn accelerometers than with pedometers or wrist devices.
**Sources:** [S400], [S398], [S401]

### Step cadence vs total steps
**Rule:** Prioritise total daily steps. Encourage some brisk walking (about 100 steps/min or faster, roughly moderate intensity) as a bonus, but do not tell clients that slow steps "don't count".
**Evidence:** In a US NHANES cohort (n=4,840), higher step intensity was not significantly associated with lower mortality after adjusting for total steps (peak-30 cadence, top vs bottom quartile, HR 0.90, 0.65-1.27). Paluch 2022 found peak-30 and peak-60 cadence still associated after step adjustment (HR 0.67), but not time at 100 steps/min or faster (HR 0.86, 0.58-1.28). Stens 2023 found moving from low to intermediate or high cadence independently associated with lower mortality.
**Grade:** emerging
**Applies to:** Middle-aged and older adults in observational cohorts.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** One large cohort found no independent cadence effect; two meta-analyses found partial or independent effects depending on the metric.
**Sources:** [S402], [S398], [S400]

### Sedentary time and how much activity offsets it
**Rule:** For desk-bound clients: (1) reduce and break up sitting, and (2) push total moderate activity toward about 60 min/day (walking, training, commuting) if they sit 8 h or more. Treat evening TV time as a separate habit to cut. Do not quote a "safe" number of sitting hours, because none has been established.
**Evidence:** In a harmonised meta-analysis of over 1 million adults (Ekelund 2016), sitting more than 8 h/day with low activity (under 2.5 MET-h/week) was associated with mortality HR 1.59. In the most active quartile (over 35.5 MET-h/week, about 60-75 min/day moderate), sitting more than 8 h/day was not associated with higher risk (HR 1.04, 0.99-1.10), but TV time of 5 h/day or more still was (HR 1.16). In accelerometer data (Ekelund 2019, n=36,383), the most sedentary quarter had HR 2.63 vs the least. WHO 2020 recommends limiting sedentary time and doing more than the recommended MVPA to offset high sitting, and found the evidence insufficient for a numeric sitting threshold.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Adults, mostly middle-aged and older, mainly from high-income countries. Observational.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Most sitting and TV data are self-reported and all data are observational, hence moderate despite the WHO recommendation. The 60-75 min/day offset comes from one harmonised analysis. The accelerometer data were from older adults (mean age 62.6).
**Sources:** [S403], [S404], [S016]

### Light activity counts
**Rule:** Count light movement (standing chores, slow walking, gardening) as valuable, especially for very sedentary, older or deconditioned clients. Use it as the first rung before adding moderate or vigorous work.
**Evidence:** In the 2019 accelerometer meta-analysis, light activity was strongly and non-linearly associated with lower mortality (quarters: HR 1.00, 0.60, 0.44, 0.38); total activity of any intensity also (top quarter HR 0.27). WHO 2020 states that replacing sedentary time with activity of any intensity, including light, provides benefits (strong recommendation, partly based on largely cross-sectional replacement studies).
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Middle-aged and older adults (mean age about 63 in the accelerometer data). Observational.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** WHO's recommendation is strong but the data are observational and partly cross-sectional. Light activity does not replace the fitness and strength benefits of MVPA and resistance training.
**Sources:** [S404], [S016]

### Breaking up sitting and sit-stand desks
**Rule:** Advise desk workers to break up sitting often. A practical default is about every 30 min with 1-5 min of walking, stairs or squats (a practical choice; the evidence does not fix the best break dose). Suggest an after-meal walk, especially for clients with higher BMI or insulin resistance. Recommend a sit-stand desk if available. Expect advice or reminders alone to have small or delayed effects.
**Evidence:** Across 37 acute trials (Loh 2020), interrupting sitting with activity breaks lowered postprandial glucose (SMD -0.54), insulin (SMD -0.56) and triglycerides (SMD -0.26) vs uninterrupted sitting, with larger glucose effects at higher BMI. At matched energy expenditure, breaks beat one continuous bout for glucose only (SMD -0.26). Cochrane (Shrestha 2018, 34 studies): sit-stand desks reduced workplace sitting by about 100 min/workday short term (low quality) and about 57 min/day at 3-12 months (2 studies); treadmill and cycle desks had unclear effects; information or counselling alone had no significant short-term effect but a modest medium-term one (-28 min/day, low quality).
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Adults, metabolically healthy or impaired. Glucose data are from acute lab trials; desk data from high-income-country workplaces.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** These are acute metabolic effects; long-term outcomes from breaks are not established. Possible publication bias for triglycerides. Desk evidence is low quality, shrinks over time and stops at 12 months. Optimal break frequency and length are not established (WHO 2020 also found insufficient evidence).
**Sources:** [S405], [S406]

### Exercise snacks
**Rule:** Use exercise snacks (e.g. 3 bouts of 1-5 min brisk stair climbing or vigorous bursts across the day) as an on-ramp for inactive or time-poor clients to raise VO2max and build the habit. Do not sell them as a fat-loss or recomposition tool; pair them with resistance training and nutrition work, and progress toward full sessions when possible. Screening still applies: an inactive client over 45 or with any PAR-Q+ "yes" gets a professional review before vigorous bursts; until then, use brisk rather than all-out snacks.
**Evidence:** Meta-analysis of 11 RCTs (n=414, 69% women, inactive adults and older adults): snacks (bouts of 5 min or less, at least 2 times/day, at least 3 days/week, 4-12 weeks) improved cardiorespiratory fitness in adults (g=1.37, 0.58-2.17; k=6; moderate certainty) and muscular endurance in older adults (g=0.40, 0.06-0.75; very low certainty), with no significant effect on lower-limb strength, body composition, blood pressure or lipids. Compliance 91%, adherence 83%.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Inactive adults and older adults (mean ages about 19-74); short trials, small samples, mostly women. Not tested in trained people.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** High heterogeneity for CRF (I2=71%) across 6 trials. Null findings come from short, small trials, so they show missing evidence rather than proof of no effect. No long-term or hard-outcome RCTs.
**Sources:** [S515], [S641]

### Vigorous intermittent lifestyle activity (VILPA)
**Rule:** Encourage clients who will not exercise formally to add a few short vigorous bursts to daily life (fast stair climbing, hurrying uphill, carrying heavy shopping briskly), aiming for several 1-2 min bursts per day. Nudge toward bursts that are genuinely hard (breathless, cannot talk comfortably) and, when possible, last a few minutes rather than seconds; very short bursts should at least be vigorous. Present this as a promising, low-cost habit, not a proven substitute for training or a guaranteed risk reduction. For men, keep structured exercise as the default recommendation. Screening still applies: a client over 45 or with any PAR-Q+ "yes" gets a professional review before genuinely hard bursts, and a previously inactive client builds up from brisk to hard bursts over several weeks (see Concentrated vigorous sessions).
**Evidence:** In UK Biobank non-exercisers (n=25,241, mean age 62, 852 deaths over 6.9 years), wrist-measured VILPA showed near-linear inverse associations: about 3 bouts/day of 1-2 min was associated with 38-40% lower all-cause and cancer mortality and 48-49% lower CVD mortality; the median 4.4 min/day with 26-30% lower all-cause and cancer and 32-34% lower CVD mortality. In an overlapping sample (n=22,398), the median 4.5 min/day was associated with HR 0.80 for total cancer and 0.69 for activity-related cancers (minimal dose about 3.4-3.7 min/day). In non-exercising women (n=13,018), the median 3.4 min/day was associated with MACE HR 0.55 and heart failure HR 0.33, and as little as 1.2-1.6 min/day with MACE HR 0.70; associations in men were less clear. For moderate-to-vigorous intermittent activity (n=25,241, 7.9 years), bouts of 1-<3, 3-<5 and 5-<10 min vs under 1 min were associated with progressively lower mortality (HR 0.66, 0.56, 0.48) and MACE (0.71, 0.62, 0.59); bouts under 1 min were associated with lower MACE only when at least 15% of the bout was vigorous.
**Grade:** emerging
**Applies to:** Self-reported non-exercising, mostly White UK adults aged about 40-69 (mean about 62), plus one independent US NHANES sample of non-exercisers (n=3,293, mean age 51). Not studied in trained young adults.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Most papers use the same or overlapping UK Biobank sample, with about 7 years of follow-up and few events. The independent NHANES 2026 replication found HR 0.56 at the median of 5.3 bouts/day, but after excluding prevalent CVD or cancer the CI crossed 1 (HR 0.68, 0.41-1.11), suggesting partial reverse causation. The sex difference is unexplained and unreplicated. The senior author declares consultancy and equity in a VILPA-related company (2025 and 2026 papers; the 2022 and 2023 papers declare none).
**Sources:** [S516], [S517], [S518], [S519], [S520], [S641]

## Cardiorespiratory fitness

### Cardiorespiratory fitness and mortality
**Rule:** Treat improving aerobic fitness as a core health goal, not only a performance or aesthetic one. The largest risk gap is between the least fit and everyone else, so for an unfit client (below about 8 METs, about 28 mL/kg/min, or in the lowest age/sex quartile) raising CRF is the top aerobic priority. Frame goals as "add about 1 MET" (about 3.5 mL/kg/min) over months, track a simple fitness proxy (timed walk or run, cycle test or step test), and do not promise specific risk reductions to an individual.
**Evidence:** Kodama 2009 (33 cohorts, 102,980 participants for all-cause mortality): each 1-MET higher CRF (about 3.5 mL/kg/min; about 1 km/h faster running speed) was associated with RR 0.87 (0.84-0.90) for all-cause mortality and 0.85 (0.82-0.88) for CHD/CVD events; low CRF (under 7.9 METs) carried RR 1.70 vs high (10.9 METs or more) and 1.40 vs intermediate. Lang 2024 umbrella review (199 cohorts, over 20.9 million observations): 11-17% lower all-cause mortality per MET and HR 0.47 (0.39-0.56) high vs low CRF, GRADE very low to moderate. Mandsager 2018 (122,007 patients referred for treadmill testing): low (under 25th percentile) vs elite (97.7th percentile or higher) CRF adjusted HR 5.04 (4.10-6.20); below- vs above-average fitness HR 1.41, comparable to smoking (1.41) and diabetes (1.40); elite vs high HR 0.77, with no observed upper limit. A 2016 AHA statement (Ross 2016) argues CRF should be assessed as a clinical vital sign.
**Grade:** strong
**Applies to:** Kodama: apparently healthy men and women. Lang: general and clinical adults. Mandsager: clinical patients at one US tertiary centre (mean age 53, 59% male), with the elite-vs-high benefit also seen at 70 or older. All observational.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Part of CRF is genetic and confounded by health status; reverse causation is possible. Whether training-induced gains carry the same risk reduction as baseline CRF is not shown. The "no upper limit" finding is from a single-centre retrospective cohort of referred patients, and HR 5.04 compares extreme groups; do not quote it as a typical client's risk.
**Sources:** [S416], [S417], [S418], [S450]

## Measuring and tracking fitness

### Estimated CRF is good enough for risk framing
**Rule:** Record a baseline CRF estimate at onboarding for every client, even if it is only a non-exercise estimate (age, sex, waist or BMI, activity level, resting HR). Use it for risk framing and to choose a starting dose, not as a precise individual number.
**Evidence:** In a 2025 meta-analysis (42 studies, 35 cohorts, 3.8 million observations, 81% male), mortality risk per 1-MET higher CRF was 0.86 all-cause and 0.84 CVD, with no significant difference between measured (RR 0.86-0.90), maximal-exercise-estimated (0.85-0.86), submaximal-estimated (0.91-0.94) and non-exercise-estimated CRF (0.81-0.85). The NTNU/HUNT non-exercise model, in 37,112 healthy Norwegians followed a mean 24 years, predicted 21% lower CVD mortality per MET in both sexes (men 95% CI 17-26%, women 12-29%) and 15% (12-17%) lower all-cause mortality per MET in men, in adults under 60 at baseline (AUC 0.70-0.77); the women's all-cause estimate is not quoted because its CI is misprinted in the abstract.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Population-level risk prediction in adults. Evidence in women is thinner. The HUNT associations held only under 60 at baseline, in a Norwegian population.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Estimates rank risk at group level; an individual estimate can be off by more than 1 MET. The Nes 2014 abstract prints the women's all-cause result as 8% lower per MET with a 95% CI of "3%-3%", which cannot be right; a 2020 corrigendum (doi 10.1249/MSS.0000000000002352) exists but its content could not be opened, so that figure is left out.
**Sources:** [S451], [S452]

### Non-exercise equations: individual accuracy
**Rule:** Present a non-exercise estimate as a range (about ±5 mL/kg/min, or ±1.5 METs), never as a single number or firm fitness category. Do not use repeated non-exercise estimates to track training progress; they mostly reflect changes in the inputs, not measured fitness. For clients with obesity, label the estimate low confidence.
**Evidence:** NTNU/HUNT (Nes 2011; 2,067 men, 2,193 women; age, waist, leisure activity, resting HR) explained 61% (men) and 56% (women) of variance, SEE 5.70 and 5.14 mL/kg/min. Comparing 28 equations with measured CPET in 2,529 healthy adults (Peterman 2021): R2 0.25-0.70, SEE 4.1-6.2 mL/kg/min, 27 of 28 significantly different from measured, and on average only 52% placed in the correct one of three categories. In 660 patients with obesity (Vecchiato 2024): R2 0.24-0.46, 19 of 21 equations biased, 50% classified correctly, worst in severe obesity and low CRF.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Apparently healthy adults (Peterman mean age 45 ± 13, 42% female; HUNT mean about 48). Accuracy is worse in obesity (Italian clinical cohort).
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Peterman cites a statement recommending at least non-exercise CRF assessment in clinical practice, yet its data show about half of individuals misclassified; clinical utility is unresolved.
**Sources:** [S453], [S454], [S455]

### Walk and run field tests
**Rule:** For clients who can safely run continuously (screened, no symptoms, already jogging), the 12-min run or 1.5-mile run is the best simple maximal field test. For beginners or older clients, use the Rockport 1-mile brisk walk (time, final HR, age, weight, sex); use it with extra caution in obesity, where it was not validated. Treat the converted VO2max as an estimate; the main value is comparing distance or time with the client's own previous result.
**Evidence:** Meta-analysis of 123 studies (Mayorga-Vega 2016): walk/run tests have low-to-moderate criterion validity (rp 0.42-0.79); best were the 1.5-mile run (0.79) and 12-min walk/run (0.78), unaffected by sex, age or VO2max level. The Rockport equation (Kline 1987, n=343, aged 30-69) had r=0.93 (SEE 0.325 L/min) and 0.92 on cross-validation (SEE 0.355 L/min) in absolute units; the mL/kg/min equations had similar SEE. In sedentary male Indian students (n=88), the Cooper equation overestimated VO2max (42.8 predicted vs 39.8 measured).
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Apparently healthy children and adults; Rockport in healthy adults 30-69. Maximal running tests are not appropriate for untrained, symptomatic or high-risk clients without screening.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Equations carry population-specific bias. Pacing, motivation and running economy plausibly affect scores independently of VO2max (reasoning, not a finding of these sources).
**Sources:** [S456], [S457], [S458]

### Submaximal step tests
**Rule:** For gym clients who should not do maximal running, use a standardised submaximal step test (Chester Step Test preferred) with a chest-strap HR monitor. Keep step height, cadence, time of day, caffeine and prior exercise the same at every test. Treat only a change larger than about 4 mL/kg/min, or a clearly lower HR at the same stage, as real.
**Evidence:** Bennett 2016 (11 studies, 8 protocols, healthy adults 18-65): correlations with measured VO2max ranged r=0.47-0.95. Test-retest reliability (2 studies) was good: mean difference -0.8 ± 3.7 mL/kg/min (about 7.7% of mean VO2max). The authors judged the Chester Step Test appropriate for tracking change.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Healthy adults 18-65. Because HR-based step tests usually extrapolate to an age-predicted HRmax, they are likely less valid on beta-blockers or with atypical HRmax (physiological reasoning, not tested).
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Validity varies widely between protocols, and reliability rests on 2 studies. Consistent individual deviation from predicted HRmax probably biases the absolute value, which makes within-person tracking more useful than absolute values (inference).
**Sources:** [S459]

### 6-minute walk test
**Rule:** Do not convert 6-minute walk distance (6MWD) into a VO2max number. In deconditioned or older clients, record 6MWD with HR and RPE as a functional capacity marker and track its change. For fitter clients, prefer a step test or walk/run test.
**Evidence:** Ross 2010 (1,083 cardiopulmonary patients, 11 studies): SEE 3.82 mL/kg/min, 26.7% of mean peak VO2, "unacceptably large" for individuals (1.1 mL/kg/min for group means). In 62 healthy young adults (Liu 2021), 6MWD alone explained R2=0.549 of VO2peak, SEE 4.08 mL/kg/min (12.9%).
**Grade:** emerging
**Applies to:** Cardiopulmonary patients and healthy young adults (mean age 21). No source validates it against VO2max in healthy community-dwelling older adults.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Gait, joint pain and pacing affect 6MWD independently of aerobic capacity. Adding HR and cardiac output improved prediction (R2 0.866) but needs lab equipment.
**Sources:** [S460], [S461]

### Smartwatch VO2max estimates
**Rule:** Treat watch VO2max as a trend indicator only. Do not quote it as a measured value, use it for zone cut-offs, or tell a client their fitness percentile or category from it. Prefer estimates from outdoor runs or walks with GPS and HR over resting-based estimates. Read a change as meaningful only if it persists over weeks and agrees with field-test or HR-at-pace data. Always compare readings from the same device, firmware and activity type.
**Evidence:** INTERLIVE meta-analysis (Molina-Garcia 2022, 14 studies): resting-based wearables overestimated VO2max (bias +2.17 mL/kg/min, LoA -13.07 to +17.41); exercise-based ones had almost no mean bias (-0.09) but wide individual error (LoA -9.92 to +9.74).
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Validation samples are typically small groups of healthy adults; applying them to older, clinical or very unfit clients is uncertain.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** A correction to this meta-analysis was published on 2026-10-13 (doi 10.1007/s40279-026-02547-9). Its text could not be opened; the PubMed abstract, which now links the correction, still shows the bias and limits of agreement quoted above (rechecked 2026-10-24), but whether the correction changes other figures is unverified. The rule uses no specific number from it. Algorithms change with firmware.
**Sources:** [S462]

### Apple Watch VO2max
**Rule:** Expect an Apple Watch VO2max to read about 4.5-6 mL/kg/min lower than a lab value on average, with larger individual error. Use it only to watch the direction of change over months, never for a percentile or category.
**Evidence:** Series 7 (Caserman 2024, n=19, age 18-63, cycle criterion): 41.4 vs 45.9 mL/kg/min, MAPE 15.8%, RMSE 8.85, ICC 0.47. Lambe 2025 (n=30, treadmill): mean underestimation 6.07 mL/kg/min, LoA -6.11 to +18.26, MAPE 13.3%. Series 10 (Lambe 2026, n=40): bias -6.25, LoA -18.23 to +5.73, MAPE 13.2%; only 14% placed in the correct FRIEND percentile band and 34% one band off. Caserman concludes smartwatches tend to overestimate in low-fit and underestimate in high-fit people.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Small samples (19-40) of mostly young healthy adults. Longitudinal tracking accuracy is not established.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Direction is consistent, but samples are small and two of three studies share authors. Caserman's Bland-Altman showed "good overall agreement" despite poor ICC. A low-fit client could be overestimated.
**Sources:** [S463], [S464], [S465]

### Garmin and Polar VO2max
**Rule:** A running-based Garmin VO2max is acceptable for following trends in runners, but may read a few mL/kg/min high. Treat resting-based estimates (Polar OwnIndex and similar) as the least reliable, especially in older adults.
**Evidence:** Forerunner 265 (Howard 2026, n=20): overestimated by about 2.6-2.9 mL/kg/min before and after 12 weeks of half-marathon training (43.95 vs 41.10; 45.00 vs 42.43), MAPE under 10%. fenix 6 (Carrier 2025, n=19, outdoor run): MAPE 7.05%, CCC 0.73. Polar OwnIndex substantially overestimated VO2max in 50 male masters basketball players aged 51-81 (Dakule 2025), despite the strongest correlation (r=0.75), consistent with INTERLIVE.
**Grade:** emerging
**Applies to:** Small samples of recreational runners and healthy adults; Polar data from older male masters athletes; Garmin estimates from outdoor runs with GPS and HR.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Results vary by model and firmware. Garmin seems to track direction of change, but the mean overestimate did not shrink after 12 weeks. Algorithms depend on correct HRmax settings.
**Sources:** [S466], [S467], [S468]

### Age and sex norms
**Rule:** Interpret any CRF value against age-, sex- and mode-specific FRIEND 2022 percentiles (treadmill vs cycle), and give a percentile range, not a single number, when the value is estimated. For clients over 50, frame holding steady over a year as real success, because the untrained trend is downward and speeds up with age.
**Evidence:** FRIEND 2015 (7,783 US treadmill tests): median VO2max fell from 48.0 (men) and 37.6 (women) mL/kg/min at 20-29 to 24.4 and 18.3 at 70-79, about 10% per decade. FRIEND 2022 (22,379 tests, ages 20-89): about 13.5% or 4.0 mL/kg/min per decade on the treadmill (16.4% on the cycle); updated treadmill norms are 1.5-4.6 mL/kg/min lower than 2015, and cycle values run lower. BLSA (810 adults, median 7.9 years; Fleg 2005): decline is 3-6% per decade in the 20s-30s, rising to more than 20% per decade in the 70s, at all activity levels.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** US adults referred for or volunteering for CPET, and a healthy community cohort. Use in European clients is reasonable but not validated.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Cross-sectional decline (10-13.5% per decade) differs from longitudinal decline, which accelerates. The 2015 and 2022 norms differ by up to 4.6 mL/kg/min, so state which version is used.
**Sources:** [S478], [S479], [S480]

### Realistic gains: younger and middle-aged adults
**Rule:** Set expectations for 8-24 weeks of guideline-level aerobic training at roughly +3 to +6 mL/kg/min (about +1 to 1.5 METs, or 10-20%), with lower-fit beginners at the upper end. Tell clients individual results vary widely, and that weight loss alone raises relative VO2max (mL/kg/min) without changing absolute VO2.
**Evidence:** Milanovic 2015 (28 controlled trials, n=723, healthy 18-45, mean age 25, baseline 40.8 mL/kg/min): endurance training +4.9 mL/kg/min (±1.4) and HIIT +5.5 (±1.2) vs no-exercise controls, larger in lower-fit people and longer programmes. HERITAGE (481 sedentary adults, 20 weeks; Bouchard 1999): mean gain about 400 mL/min, with individual responses from little or none to more than 1.0 L/min.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Healthy, mostly young sedentary to recreationally active adults; HERITAGE involved sedentary Caucasian two-generation families. Not covered for older or clinical groups (see next rule).
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Magnitude depends on baseline fitness, programme length, intensity and adherence. HERITAGE estimated heritability of the response at about 47%. Part of the responder range is likely measurement noise.
**Sources:** [S420], [S469]

### Realistic gains: older adults
**Rule:** For clients over 60, expect about +1 MET (about +3.5-4 mL/kg/min, about 15%) over roughly 5-9 months of moderate-to-vigorous aerobic training (about 60-75% HRR, 3-4 days/week, 40-50 min). Tell them progress is gradual but clinically meaningful.
**Evidence:** Huang 2005 (41 trials, 2,102 participants, group mean age 60+) and Huang 2016 (1,257 exercisers, 845 controls, mean age 67, apparently the same pooled sample): aerobic training raised VO2max by a net 3.78 mL/kg/min (3.24-4.33; about 16%, roughly 1 MET). Gains were significant from 35-50% HRR, largest at a mean of 66-73% HRR, and greater in programmes longer than 20 weeks. The authors judged 40-50 min sessions, 3-4 days/week for 30-40 weeks optimal. Between studies, intensities above about 75-80% HRR gave no additional benefit.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Healthy sedentary adults 60+ (mean about 67) in controlled trials. Not frail or clinical populations.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** The two meta-analyses share one dataset. Intensity explained only about 11% of between-study variance. The lower gains above 75-80% HRR come from between-study comparisons, are probably confounded, and are not evidence that higher intensity is harmful; HIIT trials in older adults are poorly represented.
**Sources:** [S470], [S471]

### Low response is dose-dependent
**Rule:** If CRF has not improved after about 8-12 weeks despite good adherence, first check adherence and the testing method. Then raise the dose: move intensity toward vigorous (about 75% VO2peak or HRR, or add intervals) and add 1-2 sessions or about 60-120 min per week. Do not tell clients they are "genetically unable to improve".
**Evidence:** Ross 2015 (24-week RCT, 121 sedentary abdominally obese adults, age about 53): CRF non-responders were 38.5% with low amount/low intensity (50% VO2peak), 17.6% with high amount/low intensity and 0% with high amount/high intensity (75% VO2peak). Montero and Lundby 2017 (78 healthy adults, 1-5 sessions of 60 min/week for 6 weeks): non-response on maximal power output (typical error ±3.96%) was 69%, 40%, 29%, 0% and 0%; when non-responders added 2 sessions/week for another 6 weeks, every one responded. The paper notes that about one in five guideline-following adults are described as not improving.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Middle-aged adults with abdominal obesity (62% women; Ross) and healthy adults over 6-week blocks (Montero).
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Montero defined response on Wmax, not VO2max, and groups were assigned rather than clearly randomised. Classifying responders against a threshold is statistically fragile.
**Sources:** [S473], [S474]

### Individual response: real but overstated
**Rule:** Never label a client a "non-responder" from a single before/after test. Re-test with the same protocol, look at several indicators (HR at a fixed pace or workload, field-test result, RPE), and apply the dose-escalation rule before drawing conclusions.
**Evidence:** In sedentary people on standardised programmes, VO2max gains ranged from almost none to about +100% (Bouchard and Rankinen 2001). HERITAGE (481 sedentary adults from 98 families, 20 weeks): mean gain about 400 mL/min, range from little or none to more than 1.0 L/min, 2.5 times more variance between than within families, maximal heritability 47%; age, sex and race had little influence. A consensus statement (Ross 2019) accepts a genetic component. Williamson 2017 notes HERITAGE had no non-exercising comparator; in the only earlier study with one, the SD of change was larger in controls (±5.6 mL/kg/min) than trainees (±3.7), so true individual differences cannot be separated from measurement error and day-to-day variation.
**Grade:** emerging
**Applies to:** Sedentary adults in supervised training studies; HERITAGE limited to white two-generation families in the 1999 analysis.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Direct disagreement between the Bouchard/HERITAGE and Ross 2019 consensus position and Williamson, Atkinson and Batterham. Both agree a single individual test cannot classify a person.
**Sources:** [S475], [S469], [S476], [S477]

### Re-test frequency and detecting real change
**Rule:** Baseline: a non-exercise estimate for everyone, plus one repeatable field test chosen by risk and ability (Chester step test, Rockport walk, or 12-min or 1.5-mile run for runners), recorded in raw units (distance, time, HR). Re-test every 8-12 weeks, no sooner than about 6, with the same test and conditions. Consider a change real only if it exceeds about 4 mL/kg/min or is confirmed by a second test and lower HR at a fixed workload. Use watch VO2max as a trend line only. If there is no change after a block, increase frequency or intensity before calling the client a non-responder. This is a derived rule, not a tested protocol.
**Evidence:** Average controlled-trial gains are about 3.8-5.5 mL/kg/min (4.9 endurance and 5.5 HIT at 18-45, Milanovic 2015; 3.78 at 60+, Huang 2005), about the size of step-test test-retest variability (-0.8 ± 3.7 mL/kg/min, 2 studies) and smaller than wearable limits of agreement (about -9.9 to +9.7 mL/kg/min). In Ross 2015 (tests at 4, 8, 16 and 24 weeks), non-responders fell between weeks 4 and 8 (low amount, low intensity) and by week 16 (high amount, low intensity), then plateaued; at high intensity they kept falling to 0% at 24 weeks. Montero 2017 used 6-week blocks; an additional 6-week block with 2 more sessions/week eliminated non-response.
**Grade:** emerging
**Applies to:** Ordinary adults training in commercial gyms, by extrapolation from healthy adults 18-45, sedentary adults 60+, abdominally obese adults (mean 53) and healthy adults 18-65.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** No opened guideline specifies a re-test interval. Step-test reliability rests on 2 studies. Montero used Wmax, not VO2max. Measurement error differs by test and person, and weight loss alone changes relative VO2max. Gains are larger in younger and less fit people and with longer interventions.
**Sources:** [S473], [S459], [S462], [S420], [S471], [S474]

## Setting and monitoring intensity

### 0-10 effort scale and talk-test anchors
**Rule:** Give every client a 0-10 effort target alongside the HR zone: moderate 5-6, vigorous 7-8. When the Borg 6-20 scale is used, use ACSM's bands: moderate 12-13, vigorous 14-17 (see "ACSM intensity bands"). A "light" anchor of about 3-4 is an extrapolation, not stated in the guideline. Add the talk test as a quick check (moderate = can talk but not sing; vigorous = only a few words without pausing for breath). When no HR data exist, these tools are a reasonable practical guide for general-health cardio; when HR data are available, pair them with an HR zone. Do not present the 0-10 lay scale as a validated Borg CR10 prescription.
**Evidence:** The US Physical Activity Guidelines (2nd ed., 2018) use a lay 0-10 scale (sitting 0, highest effort 10): moderate 5 or 6, vigorous "begins at a level of 7 or 8"; moderate means you "can talk, but not sing", vigorous that you "cannot say more than a few words without pausing for a breath". Absolute anchors: 3.0-5.9 METs moderate, 6.0 METs or more vigorous; relative intensity is the better guide for older adults. ACSM: RPE, OMNI scales and the talk test can "modulate or refine" intensity, but evidence is insufficient to use them "as a primary method of exercise prescription"; RPE and OMNI show "moderate to strong validity" against %VO2max, %HRmax and lactate; the talk test is "moderately associated" with intensity markers in some but not all studies.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** General adult population. Older or less fit adults need more effort for the same absolute activity, so "moderate" MET activities can feel vigorous to them.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Graded moderate because ACSM judges these tools insufficient as a primary method of prescription. The PAG scale does not exactly match Borg CR10. ACSM classes RPE and the talk test as adjuncts. The talk test's association with physiological markers is inconsistent.
**Sources:** [S433], [S396], [S776]

### Talk test validity
**Rule:** Use the talk test as the default intensity tool for steady cardio: "can speak full sentences comfortably" = easy/moderate (around the first threshold); "can speak only a few words" = vigorous. Teach it from session one, especially for clients without HR monitors or on HR-altering medication. For intervals, use RPE or pace instead.
**Evidence:** Comfortable speech is usually possible below the ventilatory or lactate threshold and not above it; the talk test yields moderate-to-vigorous intensities within ACSM and Canadian ranges and is consistent across walking, jogging, cycling, elliptical and stair-stepper, but may not be practical for HIIT (Reed & Pipe 2014). A systematic review of 10 studies, mostly in coronary patients, found the last comfortable-speech stage matches the first ventilatory threshold in workload, VO2 and HR; validity was supported and reliability satisfactory, though only one study analysed reliability adequately.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Healthy adults, athletes and cardiac patients; most review data come from coronary artery disease.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** No standard protocol, thin reliability data, and individuals differ in how "comfortable speech" feels. Less useful for intervals.
**Sources:** [S429], [S430]

### RPE validity
**Rule:** Use RPE (0-10 or 6-20) as a co-equal tool with HR and the talk test, not as a precise physiological measure. Anchor the scale for the client (for example 3-4/10 easy, 5-6 moderate, 7-8 hard, 9-10 near maximal) and recalibrate over weeks as they learn. Combine RPE with at least one other cue for key sessions.
**Evidence:** A meta-analysis of Borg RPE in healthy people found weighted mean correlations of r=0.62 with HR, 0.57 with blood lactate, 0.64 with %VO2max and 0.63 with VO2: real but lower than the 0.80-0.90 often assumed, and varying with fitness, exercise type, protocol, RPE mode and sex.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Healthy individuals across fitness levels and modes.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Correlations were highest for men exercising maximally, for unusual tasks such as swimming, and with the 15-point scale for lactate. That novices misjudge effort until experienced is a practical assumption, not a finding of this meta-analysis.
**Sources:** [S431]

### Interval intensity cues without lab testing
**Rule:** SIT and REHIT: "all-out, as hard as you can, from the first second", against enough resistance that cadence drops. Intervals of 1-4 min: RPE 7/10 ("hard; only a few words"), around 85-95% of estimated HRmax by the final third of the interval. Recovery: RPE 2-3/10. Steady moderate cardio: RPE 5-6/10 ("can talk, but not sing"), about 65-75% HRmax. All-out efforts are only for clients who meet the SIT prerequisites (see HIIT safety and pre-HIIT screening). If HR is used, prefer a measured peak HR from a hard effort over 220 - age.
**Evidence:** In 17 current and former PE students (12 men, mean age 24), Borg CR10 RPE was valid for 3 x 3 min running intervals: RPE 7 and 8 did not differ in time at or above 90% VO2max and 90% HRmax, but RPE 7 caused less ventilatory stress; RPE 6 was significantly lower on all measures. Helgerud's 4-min intervals were at 90-95% HRmax with 3 min recovery at 70%. Generation 100 compared HIIT at about 90% peak HR with MICT at about 70%. REHIT uses all-out 10-20 s sprints needing no HR target, rated at an average RPE of 13 ± 1 (6-20).
**Grade:** emerging
**Applies to:** RPE validation in young, fit PE students; HR anchors from moderately trained young men and adults aged 70-77; REHIT RPE from sedentary young adults. Not established for sedentary, obese or older people or for short intervals.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Single RPE validation study. HR targets depend on accurate HRmax, and HR lags in short intervals, so HR is unreliable for intervals of 30 s or less. Beginners differ in effort perception.
**Sources:** [S505], [S491], [S425], [S489], [S433]

### HRmax: formulas and setting the anchor
**Rule:** Default to Tanaka (208 - 0.7 x age), or 220 - age, only as a rough starting point, and tell the client it can be off by 20+ bpm (about ±11 bpm is 1 SEE, so about a third of people are further off than that). Measure resting HR yourself (seated, morning or after 5 min rest, average of several days) and use Karvonen. If a client reliably sees a higher HR during genuinely all-out efforts (on a well-fitted chest strap, or in a hard interval or race), use that observed peak, or lab testing. Cross-check zones against RPE and the talk test in the first 2-3 sessions. Never cap or push a client purely on a formula number.
**Evidence:** Tanaka 2001 (351 studies, 18,712 subjects; lab cross-validation in 514 adults gave 209 - 0.7 x age) derived 208 - 0.7 x age, unaffected by sex or activity level, and noted 220 - age underestimates HRmax in older adults. Gellish: 207 - 0.7 x age (132 people, 908 tests). Nes (HUNT, n=3,320): 211 - 0.64 x age, SEE 10.8 bpm, with older equations underestimating HRmax over 30. Shookster 2020 (99 adults aged 17-67, RER > 1.10): 220 - age and Tanaka were nearly unbiased at group level (-0.15 and 0.39 bpm), but RMSE for Fox, Gellish, Tanaka and Arena was about 10.7-11.7 bpm with 95% limits of agreement about ±21-23 bpm; others were worse (Gulati RMSE 14.8). All nine equations showed wide limits of agreement, all except Fox showed proportional bias, and Gulati, Astrand, Nes and Fairbarn (male) differed significantly from measured values.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Healthy adults across a broad age range (HUNT and Tanaka include older adults). Shookster's sample was small (two-thirds men, mostly 26-51, VO2max about 46.5). Not valid with beta-blockers or other rate-limiting drugs, or chronotropic incompetence.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Shookster favoured 220 - age (no proportional bias); Tanaka and Nes found 220 - age underestimates in older adults. All equations tend to underestimate HRmax in less fit or older people and overestimate it in fitter or younger people. Individual error matters more than the choice of equation.
**Sources:** [S427], [S428], [S436], [S437]

### Threshold-based vs %HRR prescription
**Rule:** Do not tell a client they "need" lab threshold testing. Use the closest field substitute: anchor "moderate" to where breathing first deepens and speech gets slightly harder (around VT1), and "vigorous" to where only short phrases are possible. If a client fails to improve after 8-12 weeks of %HRR-based cardio, progress intensity by RPE and talk test before concluding they are a non-responder.
**Evidence:** In a 12-week RCT in sedentary adults (n=36 completers, 30 min/day, 5 days/week), VT1/VT2-based prescription raised VO2max by 3.93 mL/kg/min vs 1.76 with %HRR; responders (over 5.9% gain) 12/12 vs 5/12. In a second 12-week trial (3 days/week, n=39, mean age 48, 30 women), all responded with threshold prescription vs 60% with %HRR (4.7% cut-off). Both are small, from one group (Dalleck), and needed lab-measured thresholds.
**Grade:** emerging
**Applies to:** Sedentary healthy adults, including middle-aged.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Groups may have differed in actual intensity; samples are small; in Weatherwax 2019 baseline VO2max differed (24.3 vs 29.2). Responder cut-offs are arbitrary and differ. The talk-test or RPE substitute is an inference, not tested in these trials.
**Sources:** [S434], [S435]

### ACSM intensity bands
**Rule:** When the coach shows heart-rate or effort zones, use the ACSM 2011 position-stand bands as the reference table: light = 30-39% HRR (or %VO2R), 57-63% HRmax, RPE 9-11 on the 6-20 scale; moderate = 40-59% HRR, 64-76% HRmax, RPE 12-13; vigorous = 60-89% HRR, 77-95% HRmax, RPE 14-17; near-maximal = 90% HRR or more, 96% HRmax or more, RPE 18 or more. Prefer %HRR (Karvonen) when resting HR is known and %HRmax otherwise, and do not treat the two as interchangeable. Present every zone as approximate and check it against RPE and the talk test (see "Fixed percentages miss individual thresholds"). For clients with known heart disease, use the zones their cardiac team sets.
**Evidence:** Garber 2011 (ACSM position stand, Table 5): very light <30% HRR or VO2R, <57% HRmax, RPE <9; light 30-39%, 57-63%, RPE 9-11, 2.0-2.9 METs; moderate 40-59%, 64-76%, RPE 12-13, 3.0-5.9 METs; vigorous 60-89%, 77-95%, RPE 14-17, 6.0-8.7 METs; near-maximal 90% or more, 96% or more, RPE 18 or more. Absolute moderate METs fall with age (4.8-7.1 at 20-39 y, 4.0-5.9 at 40-64 y, 3.2-4.7 at 65+). The authors warn that "it cannot be assumed that one method of determining exercise intensity is necessarily equivalent to that derived using another method". A 2020 paper reproducing the GETP 10th edition (2018) table gives the same light, moderate and vigorous %HRmax and RPE cut-offs but puts vigorous at 60-84% HRR and very hard to maximal at 85-100% HRR.
**Grade:** strong
**Applies to:** Apparently healthy adults. The 10th-edition numbers come from a secondary transcription; the 11th and 12th editions could not be opened.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** The vigorous/near-maximal boundary differs by edition (89/90% HRR in 2011, 84/85% in GETP 10); capping the vigorous HRR band at 84% in coach tools keeps it inside both (a convention). Every coach-convention aerobic Borg band in these files follows this table (moderate 12-13, vigorous 14-17). Bands quoted from a source stay as the source gives them (pregnancy RPE 13-14, ICFSR Borg 12-14 aerobic and 15-18 resistance), and the RIR-to-Borg mapping for lifting (2-3 RIR, about 13-15, in strength-hypertrophy.md and recovery-habits.md) is a separate convention for resistance sets, not an aerobic zone. The EAPC cardiac-rehabilitation scheme uses different bands (moderate 40-69% HRR, 55-74% HRmax), says peak-based indices "should be applied with great caution" and treats the talk test and RPE only as adjuncts, so do not mix the two schemes.
**Sources:** [S396], [S776], [S447]

### %HRR tracks %VO2R, not %VO2max
**Rule:** Translate effort targets into heart rate with heart-rate reserve (Karvonen) whenever resting HR is known: %HRR approximates %VO2R one to one. Do not convert %VO2max targets into %HRmax with the old 55/70/85/90 table; in young adults the true %HRmax equivalents run higher. Treat every conversion as an estimate.
**Evidence:** In 63 adults on a cycle ergometer, %HRR vs %VO2R had an intercept of -0.1 and slope of 1.00, indistinguishable from identity, while %HRR vs %VO2max had -11.6 and 1.12, with a larger gap in less fit people. On a treadmill (n=50), %HRR vs %VO2R (1.5 and 1.03) was significantly closer to identity than %HRR vs %VO2max (-6.1 and 1.10). In 162 adults aged 18-34, 40, 60, 80 and 85% VO2max matched 63, 76, 89 and 92% HRmax, all higher than the older ACSM equivalents of 55, 70, 85 and 90%. ACSM 2011 says the HRR or VO2R methods "may be preferable" but that their advantage "has not been supported by all studies".
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Healthy adults in laboratory incremental tests; the %HRmax data come from adults aged 18-34.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Three small laboratory studies from one group; both treadmill regressions still differed statistically from identity, and ACSM notes inconsistent support.
**Sources:** [S777], [S778], [S779], [S396]

### Fixed percentages miss individual thresholds
**Rule:** Present %HRmax and %HRR zones as population averages with wide individual error: at the same percentage one client can be below and another above their metabolic threshold. Use the talk test and RPE as the day-to-day check, and if a zone feels much harder or easier than its label, trust the client's response over the number.
**Evidence:** In 100 adults (46 women), the lactate threshold fell anywhere from 60-90% HRmax (45-74% VO2max) and maximal lactate steady state from 75-97% HRmax (69-96% VO2max); fixed percentages "conform poorly to exercise intensity domains". In 2,554 patients with cardiometabolic disease, HR at the first ventilatory threshold sat close to 40% HRR (mean absolute error 6.4 bpm), but HR at the second threshold was well above 59% HRR (bias -11.4 bpm, mean absolute error 12.0 bpm). A narrative review notes that at the same %VO2max or %HRmax some people are above and others below their thresholds, with similar variation at the same %VO2R or %HRR, and that threshold-based prescription needs lab testing, varies more from day to day and has not yet shown its theoretical advantages directly.
**Grade:** emerging
**Applies to:** Adults tested in the lab (training status not stated); the HRR data come from cardiac-rehabilitation patients only.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** One cross-sectional study, one retrospective cohort and one narrative review.
**Sources:** [S780], [S781], [S782]

### Wrist optical HR: steady-state cardio
**Rule:** For steady walking, running or elliptical work, a current wrist watch from a well-validated brand is good enough to track HR zones. Treat any single reading as roughly ±7 bpm (Apple Watch limits; other brands may be wider), about one-third to one-half of a 20%-HRR-wide zone for most adults. Coach from the session average or several minutes of stable data, not momentary spikes.
**Evidence:** Meta-analysis of 44 studies (738 effect sizes, 15 brands): mean wrist-vs-reference difference -0.01 bpm at rest and -0.51 bpm (-1.60 to 0.58) on the treadmill. Living meta-analysis of Apple Watch (82 studies, mean age 41): HR bias -0.27 bpm, limits of agreement -7.19 to +6.64 bpm. A 158-study review found Apple Watch and Garmin most accurate for HR in the lab, with Fitbit tending to underestimate.
**Grade:** strong
**Applies to:** Mostly healthy, young to middle-aged lab samples. The ±7 bpm figure is Apple Watch-specific and pooled across conditions. Device models change quickly.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Mean bias near zero does not mean small individual error. Accuracy varies by brand and model, and HR accuracy is "more variable" than step counts (Fuller).
**Sources:** [S438], [S439], [S440]

### Wrist optical HR: intervals, cycling and resistance work
**Rule:** For HIIT, short intervals, cycling and circuit or resistance work, do not trust wrist HR to place the client in a zone or confirm an interval target. Use a chest strap or upper-arm sensor, or prescribe by RPE or power and pace. Ignore wrist HR zones in strength sessions. Wrist HR can trail rapid changes in effort, so judge each work bout by its later part or by RPE.
**Evidence:** Wrist HR underestimated by -7.26 bpm (-10.46 to -4.07) in resistance training and -4.55 bpm (-7.24 to -1.87) in cycling; in resistance training the error grew about 3 bpm per 10 bpm rise in HR. Absolute error during activity averaged 30% higher than at rest, and devices differed in how they "respond to changes in activity". Skin tone made no significant difference. In a 2026 test of 10 devices (rest, cognitive stress, steady and intermittent walking), mean absolute error ranged from 4.5 bpm to 9-14 bpm (MAPE 5.5-16%), and intermittent walking increased errors for several devices.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Healthy adults in lab protocols (e.g. 21-68, mean 34, n=45 in Gielen). The 2026 test used walking, not HIIT or resistance work. Model- and firmware-specific.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Results vary by brand, model, fit and wrist position. Zhang still judged wrist devices acceptable for most common activities. Evidence that wrist HR lags at interval onset is indirect.
**Sources:** [S438], [S441], [S442]

### Watch auto zones and threshold features
**Rule:** Do not accept a watch's auto-detected lactate-threshold HR or auto zones as precise; treat them as about ±10 bpm and check against RPE and the talk test. If the watch zones are based on 220 - age or a default HRmax, overwrite them with Karvonen zones built from measured resting HR. Watch VO2max is for trends, not zone cut-offs.
**Evidence:** A 2025 review (13 studies, mostly Garmin with chest strap and Firstbeat) found VO2max estimates valid or acceptable in 7 studies but lactate-threshold estimates validated in only 3. In recreational runners vs lab Dmax-mod (Huawei n=100, Garmin n=23, Coros n=17), watch threshold HR had MAE 8.9-11.4 bpm, r 0.13-0.67, with non-significant mean differences; single-test success rates were 47-78%. Neither source evaluated default auto HR zones against ventilatory thresholds.
**Grade:** emerging
**Applies to:** Recreational runners and healthy adults, largely with chest straps; small Garmin and Coros samples. Wrist-only use and gym modalities untested.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** No group-level bias but weak individual correlation (r as low as 0.13), and threshold pace was overestimated by all devices. Algorithms change with firmware.
**Sources:** [S443], [S444]

### Beta-blockers: no formula-based HR zones
**Rule:** If a client takes a beta-blocker, never use 220 - age, Tanaka or any formula-based HR zone. Use an HRmax from a medically supervised exercise test on the current medication if available; otherwise prescribe by RPE (12-13 moderate on 6-20; no sustained vigorous work, RPE 14 or more, without medical clearance, see next rule) and the talk test. After any dose change, reset targets by re-anchoring to recent session workloads or a new test. Ask every new client about cardiovascular or rate-limiting medication and refer for medical clearance when appropriate.
**Evidence:** In heart-failure patients on beta-blockers, 220 - age had SEE 43 bpm (R2 -2.93); a specific equation (119 + 0.5 x HRrest - 0.5 x age, minus 5 on a bike) still had SEE 18 bpm, which "may make it impractical". In cardiac-rehab patients on beta-blockers, measured HRmax averaged 133 ± 21 vs predicted 161 ± 11; 0-61% would have trained within 50-80% HRR from measured HRmax, and "resting HR + 20 bpm" left 100% (+30 bpm, 48%) below 50% HRR. The EAPC says beta-blockers distort the HR-VO2 relationship and that target HR should be reset after a dose change.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Heart-failure and cardiac-rehab patients. People on beta-blockers for hypertension, migraine or anxiety are likely affected similarly, but this was not studied.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Blunting depends on drug, dose and timing. Other rate-limiting drugs (e.g. non-dihydropyridine calcium-channel blockers, ivabradine) were not covered.
**Sources:** [S445], [S446], [S447], [S396]

### RPE as the anchor under beta-blockade
**Rule:** For clients on beta-blockers, use RPE as the main intensity anchor: Borg 12-13 (CR10 about 5-6) for moderate work, the ACSM 2011 moderate band (see "ACSM intensity bands"), starting at the lower end (the post-bypass trial below used CR10 4-5), with the talk test as a check. Set a ceiling: no sustained vigorous work (RPE 14 or more, ACSM's vigorous band) without medical clearance. Respect any objective cap from a supervised test (e.g. 50% HRR or 65% of peak workload). Re-teach RPE anchoring in the first sessions, because some people under-rate effort and drift harder.
**Evidence:** In a double-blind crossover in 16 healthy adults (mean age 25), 100 mg metoprolol lowered HR and VO2 at VT, RCP and peak, but values relative to each condition's peak did not differ; RPE at VT was slightly higher (12.9 vs 12.3) and at max slightly lower (19.1 vs 19.4), RPE rose more steeply relative to HR, and the RPE-VO2 slope was unchanged with a higher origin; the authors call RPE "useful and reliable". In 71 post-bypass patients on beta-blockers, training at CR10 4-5 improved peak capacity as much as training at the HR of VT1, but the RPE group trained at higher workloads and HRs and six patients ended close to the anaerobic threshold; the authors advised objective limits (about 50% HRR or 65% peak workload). The EAPC says beta-blockers may make RPE harder to interpret and RPE should be combined with objective measures.
**Grade:** emerging
**Applies to:** Healthy young adults after one acute dose (Mitchell) and post-CABG rehab patients (Zanettini). Application to chronically medicated middle-aged gym clients is inferred.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** RPE-HR and RPE-VO2 relationships shift under beta-blockade, and self-regulation can drift toward overtraining. Anchoring skill, education and training familiarity matter.
**Sources:** [S448], [S449], [S447], [S396]

## HIIT, SIT and moderate continuous training

### HIIT and SIT raise VO2max; the edge over MICT is small
**Rule:** For a time-poor, already-active client whose main goal is fitness or health, offer SIT or low-volume HIIT as an evidence-based alternative to longer moderate cardio, once they meet the prerequisites in HIIT safety and pre-HIIT screening. Expect roughly 1-2 MET (3-6 mL/kg/min) of VO2max gain over 6-12 weeks. Tell the client HIIT beats MICT only slightly; the advantage is time, not a much greater effect.
**Evidence:** Umbrella review of 24 meta-analyses (429 studies, 12,967 participants): HIIT including SIT increased CRF vs no exercise (WMD 3.25-5.5 mL/kg/min) and vs MICT (WMD 0.52-3.76). Cochrane 2026 (58 RCTs, sedentary healthy adults 18-64): HIIT +5.98 mL/kg/min vs no exercise (4.66-7.30, moderate certainty) and only +1.39 vs MICT (0.44-2.34, low certainty). Gist 2014: SIT about 3.6 mL/kg/min (about 8%), d=0.69 vs no exercise and d=0.04 (not significant) vs endurance training. Hutchinson 2026 (93 trials, n=1,188): +8.3% (7.0-9.5%).
**Grade:** strong
**Applies to:** Sedentary or recreationally active healthy adults 18-64. SIT data mostly from young (Gist mean 23.5), untrained people on cycle ergometers. All Cochrane trials were supervised, mostly short and small.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** All agree HIIT/SIT beats no exercise; the size of the advantage over MICT varies, Cochrane rates it low certainty, and Gist found none. Most reviews in the umbrella scored moderate to critically low on AMSTAR-2. Unsupervised HIIT is untested. Individual responses vary widely.
**Sources:** [S481], [S482], [S483], [S484]

### Using HIIT on top of a continuous base
**Rule:** Use moderate continuous training as the base. When the main goal is aerobic fitness and the client tolerates hard efforts, add 1-2 HIIT sessions/week (for example 4 x 4 min hard with 3 min easy, longer intervals of 3-5 min at about 1:1 work:rest, or 30-60 s efforts), especially when progress has stalled. If the client dislikes intervals, has orthopaedic limits or is a complete beginner, MICT still produces most of the gain. A lead-in of at least 4 weeks of regular moderate cardio before intervals is sensible practice but untested (it is required before all-out sprints; see HIIT safety and pre-HIIT screening). Do not promise HIIT is dramatically better: the edge is about 1 mL/kg/min in healthy people.
**Evidence:** Milanovic 2015 (28 studies, n=723, healthy 18-45, mean age 25, baseline about 41): +5.5 HIIT and +4.9 MICT vs controls, extra HIIT benefit +1.2 mL/kg/min (±0.9), larger with longer repetitions (+2.2) and longer programmes (+1.7). In lifestyle-related cardiometabolic disease (10 studies, n=273), HIIT improved VO2peak 3.03 mL/kg/min (2.00-4.07) more than MICT (about 9%). A 2026 network meta-analysis (18 RCTs, 8-16 weeks, overweight or obesity) ranked HIIT of 75 min/week or more highest (+5.94 vs control; moderate-to-low certainty). Bacon 2013 (37 studies, 334 subjects under 45, 6-13 weeks, no continuous comparator): mean +0.51 L/min; a longer-interval subset (9 studies, 72 subjects) showed about 0.8-0.9 L/min with a response in all subjects.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Healthy data mainly from young adults and short trials; clinical data from CAD, heart failure, hypertension, metabolic syndrome and obesity; the network meta-analysis covers overweight or obesity. Evidence in older adults is thinner.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** The advantage is small and imprecise in healthy people ("possibly small"; Milanovic used contested magnitude-based inference) and larger in less fit and clinical groups. The Bacon subset is small, selected and uncontrolled. Individual response, adherence and injury risk vary, and enjoyment drives adherence more than a 1 mL/kg/min difference.
**Sources:** [S420], [S421], [S422], [S472]

### Fewer sprints, more weeks (and skip classic Wingate)
**Rule:** In SIT, use 2-3 sprints per session, not the classic 4-6 x 30 s Wingate. Make the plan last longer (at least 6-12 weeks) rather than adding sprints, and push intensity, not repetitions. If a client asks for classic sprint sessions, explain that 2-3 all-out sprints of about 20 s give similar VO2max gains with less discomfort and time.
**Evidence:** Vollaard 2017 (38 trials): possibly small negative effect of -1.2% per 2 extra sprints (±0.8%, 90% CL). Hutchinson 2026 (93 trials, 78 studies, n=1,188): SIT raised VO2max about 8.3%; -0.04% per extra sprint (-0.32 to 0.24), trivial, while each extra week added 0.7% (0.3-1.0%); fewer sprints improve time-efficiency and lessen the drop in affect. Yin 2024 (21 studies, n=849): more sprint repetitions (beta -0.52) and longer high-intensity time (beta -0.21) did not increase CRF gains with low-volume HIIT, while higher intensity did. Vollaard and Metcalfe (2017) judge classic Wingate SIT (up to about 30 min per session, high perceived exertion) unsuitable as an alternative to standard recommendations, and report fewer and shorter sprints give health benefits that are no worse.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Untrained or recreationally active, mostly young adults doing all-out cycle sprints (Vollaard 2017 excluded patients, athletes and baseline VO2max above 55). Not running sprints or trained athletes; classic SIT may still suit athletes, which is outside this scope.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** All these analyses share an author (Vollaard), who developed REHIT; Vollaard 2017 used magnitude-based inference. Wen 2019 found the opposite for HIIT generally: long-interval (2 min or more), high-volume (15 min or more) protocols gave larger VO2max effects and were the only variants that beat MICT. So the conclusion is "fewer sprints is enough for SIT", not "less is always better". The meta-analysis tested sprint number, not sprint length.
**Sources:** [S484], [S485], [S486], [S487], [S508]

### Minimum effective dose: REHIT and 3 x 20 s
**Rule:** REHIT and 3 x 20 s SIT are options for already-active clients who lack time, not for sedentary beginners. Prerequisites: PAR-Q+ with all "no" answers and at least 4 weeks of regular moderate cardio without symptoms; anyone over 45 or with any PAR-Q+ "yes" gets a professional review first (see HIIT safety and pre-HIIT screening). For a client who qualifies, the minimum dose is 3 sessions a week of 10 minutes on a bike: 2-3 min easy, then 2-3 all-out 20 s sprints with about 2-3 min easy pedalling between, then a 3-min cool-down. Do not shorten sprints below about 20 s to make them "easier"; it costs adaptation without feeling better. Build in over 2-3 weeks: 1 sprint of 10 s, then 15 s, then 20 s, as in the REHIT build-up.
**Evidence:** REHIT (10-min sessions, 3 times/week for 6 weeks, 1-2 all-out sprints building from 10 to 20 s) raised VO2max 15% in men and 12% in women at an average RPE of 13 ± 1; insulin sensitivity rose 28% in men only. Gillen 2016 (3 x 20 s all-out with 2 min easy, 10 min including warm-up and cool-down, 3 times/week for 12 weeks) raised VO2peak 19% and improved insulin sensitivity similarly to 45 min of MICT at about 70% HRmax (50-min sessions), a fivefold lower time commitment. Cutting REHIT sprints from 20 to 10 s reduced the VO2max gain from +10% to +4% (d=1.06) without improving affect or perceived exertion.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Healthy sedentary young adults (Gillen: men 27 ± 8, BMI 26, n=9 SIT; Metcalfe: n=15 REHIT; Nalcakan: n=36, age 22 ± 3). Small, short, lab-based cycle trials; not established in older or obese people or unsupervised settings.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Small single RCTs, supported by the sprint-number meta-analyses, largely from the same research group. The insulin-sensitivity gain was in men, not women. Individual responses differ. The trials enrolled sedentary young adults under lab supervision; restricting all-out work to already-active, screened clients is a safety policy for unsupervised coaching, based on the screening sources, not a finding of these trials.
**Sources:** [S488], [S489], [S490], [S641]

### Longer submaximal intervals: 10 x 1 min and 4 x 4 min
**Rule:** For clients who cannot or will not go all-out, or who want the largest fitness gain, use submaximal HIIT. Start with 10 x 1 min hard / 1 min easy (about 90% HRmax, RPE about 7-8/10). Progress to 4 x 4 min at 90-95% HRmax (RPE about 7/10) with 3 min easy, about 25 min plus warm-up, 2-3 times a week. Pair HR targets with RPE.
**Evidence:** 10 x 60 s cycling at about 90% HRmax with 60 s rest, 6 sessions over 2 weeks, lowered average 24-h glucose in 8 people with type 2 diabetes (age 63 ± 8, BMI 32), no control group. The Norwegian 4 x 4 at 90-95% HRmax with 3 min at about 70%, 3 times/week for 8 weeks, raised VO2max from 55.5 to 60.4 mL/kg/min, significantly more than work-matched training at 70% or 85% HRmax. Wen 2019: long intervals (2 min or more), higher volume (15 min or more of work) and 4-12+ weeks gave the largest effects and were the only HIIT variants that beat MICT.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** 4 x 4: 40 moderately trained young men (baseline about 55-60). 10 x 1: a tiny 2-week uncontrolled pilot in older adults with T2D. Extrapolated to ordinary gym-goers.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Single small trials, supported by Wen 2019. Age-predicted HRmax has large individual error. The Helgerud abstract reports the 4 x 4 gain as 7.2%, which does not match its own 55.5 to 60.4 mL/kg/min (about 8.8%); only the absolute values are quoted here.
**Sources:** [S491], [S492], [S487]

### Zone 2: popular claims outrun evidence
**Rule:** Do not prescribe Zone 2 as a uniquely superior or required intensity. Easy, conversational cardio is a perfectly good way to build volume, recover and meet guideline minutes. For a time-limited client (2-3 hours/week total), spend part of the aerobic time at higher intensity rather than all of it in Zone 2. Do not obsess over lab-precise zone boundaries.
**Evidence:** Zone 2 (low intensity below the lactate threshold) is promoted as optimal for mitochondrial and fat-oxidation capacity and cardiometabolic health, largely on observational data from high-volume elite endurance athletes. A 2025 critical narrative review (Storoschuk, Moran-MacDonald, Gibala, Gurd) concluded evidence does not support Zone 2 as optimal for mitochondrial or fatty-acid oxidative capacity, and that prioritising higher intensities is critical for cardiometabolic benefit, especially at lower volumes. This fits Milanovic 2015: both HIIT and continuous training produced large VO2max gains, with a possibly small extra +1.2 mL/kg/min for HIIT.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** General-population adults. The VO2max meta-analysis covered healthy adults 18-45 (mean 25), so extrapolation to older or clinical clients is indirect. High-volume endurance athletes are a different context.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** The main source is a narrative review; one author (Gibala) is a high-intensity researcher with an equity disclosure (Longevity League). Few RCTs test "Zone 2" directly, and definitions vary (lactate, ventilatory threshold, %HRmax). Zone 2 work is still valid exercise; only the "optimal" claim lacks support.
**Sources:** [S426], [S420]

### HIIT for broader cardiometabolic health
**Rule:** Present HIIT as an effective, time-efficient option for cardiometabolic health, not as obligatory or magic. For older adults, regular activity at guideline levels is the main goal; some vigorous work is reasonable if tolerated, but do not claim HIIT has been proven to extend life.
**Evidence:** An umbrella review of 133 systematic reviews (Viderman 2025) concluded HIIT improves body composition, cardiovascular and metabolic parameters and mental health, comparable or superior to MICT in conditions such as diabetes and CVD, with caution over high heterogeneity. In the 5-year Generation 100 RCT (n=1,567, age 70-77, 2 sessions/week), supervised exercise (HIIT about 90% peak HR or MICT about 70%) did not reduce all-cause mortality vs following national guidelines; HIIT showed a non-significant trend (vs control HR 0.63, 0.33-1.20, absolute risk reduction 1.7 percentage points; vs MICT HR 0.51, 0.25-1.02).
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Umbrella review: mixed, largely clinical adult populations. Generation 100: Norwegian adults 70-77, 87.5% in good health and 80% with medium or high activity at baseline.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** High heterogeneity across reviews. In Generation 100 the control group did more of its activity as HIIT than the MICT group, diluting the contrast; mortality differences were not significant and the HIIT vs MICT comparison was exploratory.
**Sources:** [S424], [S425]

### Insulin sensitivity and glycaemic markers
**Rule:** Present HIIT and SIT as one valid way to improve insulin sensitivity, not as better than moderate cardio for HbA1c. For clients with prediabetes or diabetes, HIIT or MICT are both acceptable and consistency matters more than modality. Diabetes counts as known metabolic disease under the ACSM 2015 algorithm: a currently inactive client with diabetes needs medical clearance before starting any programme, and an active one before progressing to vigorous work (see Start low, progress gradually).
**Evidence:** Jelleyman 2015 (50 studies): HIIT lowered insulin resistance vs control (SMD -0.49) and vs continuous training (SMD -0.35), HbA1c 0.19% vs control, and fasting glucose 0.92 mmol/L in people at risk of or with type 2 diabetes. Li 2026 (40 RCTs, n=1,460): SIT improved glycaemic, insulin and lipid outcomes vs no exercise; only HOMA-IR differed vs MICT (low to very low certainty). Zeng 2026 (21 RCTs, type 2 diabetes): no HbA1c difference between HIIT and MICT.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Adults, including those with or at risk of type 2 diabetes. SIT data are heterogeneous and mostly in young or healthy samples.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Jelleyman found HIIT better for insulin resistance; Zeng found no HIIT-MICT difference for HbA1c or HOMA-IR in T2D. Glycaemic response varies widely.
**Sources:** [S493], [S494], [S495], [S119]

### Enjoyment and affect
**Rule:** Do not promise that HIIT is more fun. Protect pleasure by keeping the number of hard efforts low, preferring very short sprints (about 5-6 s) or few repetitions. Ask about in-session feeling (Feeling Scale -5 to +5) and post-session enjoyment. If a client reports clearly negative feelings or dreads sessions, switch to MICT or gentler intervals; their own response outweighs group averages.
**Evidence:** Oliveira 2018 (8 acute studies): small HIIT advantages in enjoyment (PACES SMD 0.49, I2 69%; EES 0.48) and a trivial one for in-task pleasure (Feeling Scale SMD 0.19, I2 79%). Ekkekakis, Hartman and Ladwig (2023) argue greater-enjoyment claims conflict with "voluminous evidence" that high intensity usually feels less pleasant, citing problems in how conditions are defined and when affect is measured. An individual-participant meta-analysis of sprint exercise (Metcalfe 2022, 13 studies, n=316) found end-of-sprint affect fell per additional sprint by 0.84 units (30 s sprints), 1.02 (15-20 s) and 0.20 (5-6 s); recovery duration had no effect.
**Grade:** emerging
**Applies to:** Healthy adults in acute lab sessions. Long-term affect and unsupervised adherence are largely unstudied.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Open methodological disagreement between HIIT proponents (e.g. Metcalfe, Vollaard) and affect researchers (Ekkekakis). Affective responses differ strongly between individuals.
**Sources:** [S497], [S498], [S499]

### Real-world adherence to unsupervised HIIT
**Rule:** Treat HIIT as one option and let the client choose. Assume adherence will fade without structure: schedule sessions, have the client log them, review every 2-4 weeks, and keep a moderate-cardio fallback ready. Judge success by sessions completed over months, not the protocol on paper.
**Evidence:** Roy 2018: 250 overweight or obese New Zealand adults chose between HIIT and 30 min/day moderate exercise (42% chose HIIT). After one training session, HIIT was unsupervised; adherence to 2 or more sessions/week fell from 60.8% to 19.6% at 12 months. Weight (-0.44 kg, -2.5 to 1.6) and visceral fat did not differ between groups, though HIIT participants reported more enjoyment. The 23% who stayed adherent (67% men) lost more weight (-2.7 kg, -5.2 to 0.2) and visceral fat. All Cochrane 2026 trials were supervised. Biddle and Batterham (2015) debate population-level HIIT: Biddle cites aversive affect, low perceived competence and poor maintenance; Batterham argues scalable protocols exist and displeasure is overstated.
**Grade:** emerging
**Applies to:** Overweight or obese New Zealand adults and sedentary healthy adults in supervised trials. Unsupervised commercial-gym data are scarce.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** One non-randomised 12-month study; the adherer comparison is post hoc and its CI crosses zero. Expert opinion is split. Adherence likely depends on sex, experience and preference.
**Sources:** [S500], [S501], [S482]

### Dropout tracks time, not intensity
**Rule:** When a client first starts HIIT (after the moderate-cardio lead-in), keep sessions short (10-20 min including warm-up) and weekly time modest. Prefer a bike or other low-impact mode when starting intervals, especially for heavier clients or those with joint pain; otherwise let preference decide. Long, grinding sessions, not intensity itself, are the dropout risk.
**Evidence:** Across 67 HIIT interventions in sedentary healthy adults (n=1,318), pooled dropout was 17.6% (14.2-21.5%). Longer sessions, more weekly time and more total time predicted dropout; intensity did not. Cycling-based HIIT had lower dropout than running or walking.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Sedentary healthy adults in supervised research interventions of 4 weeks or more; gym dropout may be higher.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** One meta-analysis of motivated volunteers. Wewege 2017 suggests running gives more fat loss than cycling, so mode choice trades adherence against outcome.
**Sources:** [S502]

### Stair climbing and home-based HIIT
**Rule:** For already-active clients without time for a gym session who meet the SIT prerequisites (see HIIT safety and pre-HIIT screening), 3 x 20 s of fast continuous stair climbing (as fast as safely possible, handrail if needed), 3 times a week, with easy recovery between efforts, is a validated option. The one-flight 3 x 60 s up-and-down version works where only one flight is available, but feels about as hard and gave smaller gains. For clients with knee or balance problems, use a bike. Make no glucose-control promises from stair sprints.
**Evidence:** Allison 2017 (31 sedentary women, age 24 ± 10): 3 x 20 s all-out stair climbs, 3 times/week for 6 weeks, raised VO2peak 12% (about 1 MET; n=12); acutely (n=8), HR, lactate and RPE matched 3 x 20 s cycle sprints. The 3 x 60 s one-flight protocol gave similar acute HR and RPE and raised VO2peak 7% (n=11). The Cederholm glucose index did not change significantly (p=0.056). Home-based HIIT (Tsuji 2023, 15 RCTs) improved CRF vs no exercise (SMD 0.61, 0.21-1.02) and did not differ significantly from lab-based HIIT (SMD -0.35) or MICT (0.34).
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Stairs: sedentary young women in small single-arm phases without controls. Home HIIT: mixed healthy and patient adults. Older adults, men and people with knee or balance problems not studied on stairs.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** The stair evidence is small and uncontrolled; broader HIIT evidence supports the idea, not the specific protocol. Fall risk on stairs depends on balance, footwear and fatigue.
**Sources:** [S503], [S504]

### Older adults and interval training
**Rule:** Older adults can do interval training, but use submaximal intervals (for example 4 x 4 or 1-min intervals at RPE 6-7/10) on a bike or by uphill walking, not all-out sprints, at least at first. Always combine with resistance and balance training. Supervise the first sessions. Clients over 45 new to vigorous work get a professional review before intervals; anyone with symptoms, or with known cardiovascular, metabolic or renal disease, follows the ACSM clearance rules (see Start low, progress gradually).
**Evidence:** In middle-aged and older women (19 RCTs), HIIT improved VO2max (SMD 1.20, 0.86-1.54, high certainty), though the VO2peak estimate was not significant (SMD 0.23), and did not significantly improve strength, flexibility or sit-to-stand. A 2026 review of adults 60+ (25 reports, about 2,818 people) found the most consistent gains in CRF and generally favourable feasibility and adherence, with inconsistent adverse-event reporting and no established superiority over MICT, comparative safety or optimal prescription. Generation 100 (n=1,567, age 70-77, 5 years): combined MICT plus HIIT did not change all-cause mortality vs control; HIIT vs control HR 0.63 (0.33-1.20); exploratory HIIT vs MICT HR 0.51 (0.25-1.02).
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Adults 60-77+, mostly healthy and supervised. Cai covers women only. Limited evidence for unsupervised use or frail adults.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Generation 100's primary outcome was null and its control group did much of its activity as HIIT. VO2max and VO2peak results disagree in Cai. Review quality is limited. Suitability depends on comorbidities, joints and fall risk.
**Sources:** [S506], [S425], [S507], [S641], [S119]

### Choosing HIIT vs MICT vs SIT
**Rule:** (0) Sedentary beginner: MICT first (walking as the base, building from 10-20 min toward 150 min/week); no all-out sprints. (1) Very time-poor client who is already active (PAR-Q+ all "no", at least 4 weeks of regular moderate cardio without symptoms, professional review first if over 45 or any PAR-Q+ "yes"): SIT/REHIT 3 times a week (2-3 x 20 s all-out on a bike), presented as a minimum dose, plus extra daily walking toward WHO minutes. (2) Wants the largest fitness gain and has 25-30 min: 4 x 4 or 10 x 1 min HIIT 2-3 times a week. (3) Deconditioned, obese with joint pain, older, anxious about hard effort, has disliked HIIT, or has an unscreened medical issue: MICT (brisk walking, cycling) first, adding submaximal intervals later. (4) In every case, prioritise resistance training 2 or more times a week and let preference decide, because adherence drives outcomes.
**Evidence:** WHO 2020 recommends 150-300 min moderate or 75-150 min vigorous plus muscle strengthening, and says some activity is better than none. Cochrane 2026 notes WHO gives no specific HIIT advice because efficacy and acceptability in sedentary people were unclear, and concludes HIIT may be an effective alternative to MICT. Yin 2024 (low-volume HIIT: 5 min or less of hard work in sessions of 15 min or less; 21 studies, n=849) improved CRF vs untrained controls (SMD 1.19), blood pressure and waist, with no significant difference from MICT or high-volume HIIT, in 14-47% of MICT's time. A typical REHIT week (3 x 10 min, 2 x 20 s sprints, about 2 min of hard work, 30 min total) falls well below the WHO vigorous-minute target.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Ordinary adults 18-65, plus older adults with the caveats above. Low-volume evidence comes mostly from short supervised trials; the REHIT example from sedentary young adults.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Whether 1-3 min/week of all-out work can stand in for guideline volume for long-term outcomes such as mortality is unknown, and WHO does not endorse it. Cochrane rates HIIT's CRF advantage low certainty and found no trial reporting mortality. Yin's authors include low-volume proponents.
**Sources:** [S016], [S486], [S482], [S489], [S641]

### Jump rope as conditioning
**Rule:** Jump rope can serve as a cardio option. In trials, mostly in young people, it gave small to moderate gains over active or passive controls in resting heart rate, BMI, fat mass, cardiorespiratory endurance, jumping and sprinting, but did not change blood pressure, waist-hip circumference or lean mass. Treat continuous skipping as very strenuous, vigorous work: about 11.7 to 12.5 METs, with heart rate about 176 to 177 bpm in one lab study, and women reached about 92% of VO2max. Between about 125 and 145 skips per minute, skipping faster barely changed energy cost, but very high rates cost more. Practical inference, not tested in trials: beginners should use short bouts with rests rather than long continuous sets.
**Evidence:** Singh 2022 (21 moderate to high quality studies, 1,021 participants, programmes of 6 to 40 weeks): improvements (ES = 0.23 to 1.19) in resting HR, BMI, fat mass, cardiorespiratory endurance, maximal strength, jumping, range of motion and sprinting; no significant effect on systolic or diastolic blood pressure, waist-hip circumference, bone or lean mass, or muscle endurance; 18 of 21 studies had a mean age under 18. Town 1980 (30 subjects): skipping for 5 min at 125, 135 and 145 skips/min gave HR 176, 177 and 177 bpm and mean METs 11.7 to 12.5, with no difference by skipping rate. Quirk 1982 (12 subjects): skipping reached 92% of VO2max in women and 76 to 88% in men; in men VO2 did not rise with rate over a wide range, but extremely high rates required more energy.
**Grade:** emerging
**Applies to:** Training effects come mainly from children and adolescents, so applying them to adults is an extrapolation. Intensity data come from two small 1980s lab samples whose ages the abstracts do not give.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Adult training trials are few. Zhao 2023 (preadolescents aged 10 to 12, 15 studies, 1,048 subjects) found no advantage over regular physical education for body morphology, which partly conflicts with the BMI and fat mass findings (Singh compared jump rope with active or passive controls). No review in adults was found. No source gave injury rates or a volume progression for beginners.
**Sources:** [S860], [S861], [S862], [S863]

## Bone health

### Exercise for bone: resistance plus impact
**Rule:** For bone health, combine progressive resistance training of the major muscle groups (including the back and legs) on 2-3 days a week, building over time toward about 3 sets of a load that can be lifted 8-12 times, with some moderate impact work (stamping, jogging, low hops or jumps) on most days, about 50 impacts per session (for example 5 sets of 10). Start impact low and build it once the client is conditioned and has no joint or fracture problem that rules it out. Avoid loaded spinal flexion in anyone with known low bone mass. The doses are consensus numbers, not trial-derived thresholds.
**Evidence:** The 2022 UK consensus (Royal Osteoporosis Society and partners) recommends resistance and impact exercise to maximise bone strength: muscle strengthening on 2-3 days a week, progressing to 3 sets of the maximum load that can be lifted 8-12 times (labelled evidence-based), and moderate impact on most days with at least 50 impacts per session (labelled expert consensus); combined impact and progressive resistance best promotes bone strength. ESSA (2017) agrees that bone responds to impact and high-intensity progressive resistance training and advises against loaded spine flexion. The UK consensus reports Australian guidance of 50 jumps in 3-5 sets on 4-7 days at more than 4 times body weight for people without osteoporosis.
**Grade:** strong
**Applies to:** The UK consensus is written for people with osteoporosis (mostly postmenopausal women and older adults); ESSA covers prevention as well as management. Applying the doses to healthy adults and recreational lifters is an extrapolation.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** UK guidance chose moderate impact because higher-intensity impact showed no clear extra benefit, while Australian guidance uses higher-intensity jumps for people without osteoporosis. People with vertebral fractures or several low-trauma fractures should usually keep impact to brisk-walking level and get clinical advice first (see "Low bone mass: heavy loading only when screened and supervised" in strength-hypertrophy.md).
**Sources:** [S784], [S785]

### Expected bone gains are modest
**Rule:** Tell clients that exercise slows bone loss or adds a little bone, typically a few percent of BMD over months to a year, and that a bone programme needs to run for a year or more. Prefer combined resistance plus impact or weight-bearing work over resistance or walking alone. Do not promise fewer fractures from these BMD changes.
**Evidence:** Cochrane (43 RCTs, 4,320 postmenopausal women): lower-limb progressive resistance training gave the best femoral neck result (mean difference 1.03, 95% CI 0.24 to 1.82) and combination programmes the best spine result (3.22, 95% CI 1.80 to 4.64), both in percentage change versus control; fractures were not significantly reduced (OR 0.61, 95% CI 0.23 to 1.64). Zhao 2015 (24 studies): resistance training raised femoral neck (SMD 0.303) and lumbar spine BMD (SMD 0.311), combined resistance plus high-impact or weight-bearing programmes did better (SMD 0.411 and 0.431), and resistance alone was not significant. A 2026 network meta-analysis (74 RCTs, 5,331 women, at least 24 weeks) found site- and modality-specific effects, more consistent gains with programmes of 52 weeks or more, and similar gains with normal and low BMD.
**Grade:** strong
**Applies to:** Postmenopausal women (the network meta-analysis also included healthy women 60+). For men and adults 65+, see "Resistance training and bone in men and adults 65+" in strength-hypertrophy.md.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Reporting quality was low in the Cochrane trials and heterogeneity was notable. The 2026 network meta-analysis ranks mind-body exercise plus resistance training highly at several sites, which differs from the emphasis on impact.
**Sources:** [S786], [S787], [S788]

### Walking alone does little for bone density
**Rule:** Keep walking as the base for general health, but do not present it as the bone-health plan: add resistance training and some impact. A client who only walks can expect little or no spine benefit and at most a small hip benefit after 6 months or more.
**Evidence:** A meta-analysis of 10 trials in perimenopausal and postmenopausal women found walking alone had no significant effect on lumbar spine BMD (WMD 0.01 g/cm2, 95% CI -0.00 to 0.02), radius or whole body, and no femoral neck effect overall (WMD 0.01, 95% CI -0.00 to 0.01); a positive femoral neck effect appeared only in interventions longer than 6 months.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Perimenopausal and postmenopausal women; small, mixed randomised and non-randomised trials.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** For people with vertebral fractures, the UK consensus sets brisk walking as the impact ceiling, so walking is the right level for them; that is a safety limit, not a bone-building claim.
**Sources:** [S789], [S784]

## Safety

### Concentrated vigorous sessions: ramp up, screen by symptoms
**Rule:** A previously inactive client should not start with one long or all-out weekend session; build intensity and session length over several weeks before using a concentrated weekend-warrior pattern or hard VILPA bursts. Screen at intake for symptoms (chest pain or pressure, unusual breathlessness, palpitations, fainting) and known cardiovascular, metabolic or renal disease, and apply the ACSM 2015 algorithm: inactive with known disease, or anyone with symptoms, gets medical clearance before starting any programme; active with known disease and no symptoms gets clearance before progressing to vigorous work. Habitually active healthy clients can train vigorously without routine clearance.
**Evidence:** Vigorous exertion transiently raises the relative risk of sudden cardiac death (RR 16.9 during and up to 30 min after an episode in 21,481 male physicians), but absolute risk is extremely low (1 per 1.51 million episodes), and habitual vigorous exercise attenuates the transient risk (Albert). For myocardial infarction, heavy exertion (6 METs or more) raised the relative risk in the following hour to 5.9 (4.6-7.7), ranging from 107 in people exercising less than once a week to 2.4 in those exercising 5 or more times a week (Mittleman, n=1,228 MI patients). ACSM's screening model is based on current activity, signs or symptoms or known cardiovascular, metabolic or renal disease, and desired intensity; events are often preceded by warning symptoms, and risk falls as people become active and fit. WHO 2020: potential harms are managed by gradually increasing amount and intensity.
**Grade:** strong
**Applies to:** All adults; the trigger data come from US male physicians (Albert, 2000) and MI patients (Mittleman, case-crossover), so absolute numbers may differ for women and other groups. Especially relevant for middle-aged and older adults starting vigorous exercise.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Risk depends on underlying, often undiagnosed, heart disease. A family history of sudden death or inherited cardiac conditions warrants medical input regardless of fitness.
**Sources:** [S119], [S016], [S531], [S644]

### HIIT safety and pre-HIIT screening
**Rule:** HIIT is acceptable for healthy adults, but build up to it. All-out sprints (SIT, REHIT, stair sprints) are not for sedentary beginners. Prerequisites for any all-out work: PAR-Q+ with all "no" answers and at least 4 weeks of regular moderate cardio without symptoms. Anyone over 45, or with any PAR-Q+ "yes", gets a qualified exercise professional or clinician review before vigorous or maximal work. Then progress the number and length of intervals, and always warm up (3-5 min) and cool down (3 min easy). Before HIIT or SIT, screen for symptoms (chest pain, unusual breathlessness, fainting, dizziness, palpitations), known cardiac, metabolic or renal disease, and family history, and teach these as stop-and-seek-care signs. Anyone with symptoms or known disease follows the ACSM clearance rules and should start with supervised, submaximal intervals rather than all-out sprints. The 4-week lead-in is practical judgement, not a tested protocol.
**Evidence:** Among 4,846 coronary heart disease patients in Norwegian cardiac rehabilitation (175,820 exercise hours), there was 1 fatal cardiac arrest in 129,456 h of moderate exercise and 2 non-fatal arrests in 46,364 h of HIIT (about 1 per 23,182 h), with no myocardial infarctions; the authors judged risk low for both and recommended considering HIIT for these patients. ACSM: benefits far outweigh risks in most adults; education about and screening for CHD symptoms, with gradual progression, may reduce risk; diagnostic exercise testing is not recommended for universal screening. The 2023 PAR-Q+ clears people who answer "no" to every question to start slowly and build up gradually, sends any "yes" to further questions, the ePARmed-X+ or a qualified exercise professional, and advises people over 45 not used to regular vigorous-to-maximal effort to consult a qualified exercise professional before such exercise. Cochrane 2026 (58 RCTs, healthy sedentary adults) found no trials reporting adverse events, all supervised. In cardiometabolic disease (10 studies, n=273), HIIT raised VO2peak 3.03 mL/kg/min more than MICT; in healthy sedentary adults the advantage was 1.39 (low certainty).
**Grade:** emerging
**Applies to:** Event data from supervised cardiac-rehab patients; absolute risk in healthy gym-goers is presumably lower but not quantified. ACSM guidance targets apparently healthy adults. Safety of unsupervised HIIT is untested.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Only 3 events in total, so the HIIT vs moderate rate comparison is very imprecise and comes from one observational cohort. Healthy-population trials rarely track adverse events. Individual risk depends on underlying disease.
**Sources:** [S432], [S396], [S421], [S482], [S641], [S119]

### Atrial fibrillation at very high endurance volumes
**Rule:** At guideline and moderately above-guideline volumes (up to about 25-30 MET-h/week, e.g. about 4-5 h/week of mixed moderate-vigorous cardio), treat exercise as AF-protective. Clients who accumulate more than about 6-7 h/week of hard endurance training for years should hear about the modest AF signal and report palpitations, an irregular pulse, unexplained performance drop or dizziness to a doctor. Do not discourage ordinary clients from vigorous training because of AF.
**Evidence:** Physical activity is associated with lower AF risk in the general population (22 studies, OR 0.78, 0.68-0.89), but athlete status with higher odds (15 studies, OR 2.74, 1.72-4.39; an earlier meta-analysis OR 2.46, 1.73-3.51). In a dose-response analysis of 26 studies (n about 1.02 million), AF odds were lowest at about 26 MET-h/week and rose beyond about 78 MET-h/week (about 6-7 h/week of endurance training). The 2021 meta-analysis found higher risk in mixed-sport than endurance athletes, and in athletes under 55 than 55 or older. The guideline minimum is about 7.5 MET-h/week, so the signal begins at roughly 10x the minimum.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Athlete data come predominantly from male, mostly White athletes; for coaching purposes this concerns high-volume recreational endurance exercisers. The protective effect applies to ordinary adults.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** The 2026 dose-response meta-analysis is a single ahead-of-print study standardising mostly self-reported exposures, so thresholds are approximate. Mixed sports carrying more risk than endurance sports conflicts with the common framing. Sex-specific thresholds are unknown and individual clinical significance is uncertain.
**Sources:** [S524], [S525], [S526]

### Coronary calcium in very high-volume exercisers
**Rule:** Ordinary clients at guideline volumes (about 7.5-20 MET-h/week) are well below the levels where a coronary artery calcium (CAC) signal appears; do not cite CAC as a reason to avoid cardio. For middle-aged men doing more than about 5-8 h/week of mostly very hard endurance work for years, mention that CAC can be higher and that standard cardiovascular risk assessment by a doctor still applies. Varying intensity (more vigorous, less maximal) is a reasonable choice.
**Evidence:** A review of 33 studies found 9 of 12 comparative studies with participants exercising over 450 min/week reported higher CAC in the highest-volume group (15 of 33 overall; 8 found no or an inverse association). A meta-analysis of 9 observational studies (n=61,150) found higher CAC in male athletes doing over 3000 MET-min/week (about 50 MET-h/week; mean difference 31.6, 10.7-52.6), but no difference in men at 1500-3000 or women at 1500 or more. In 284 middle-aged male athletes, lifelong volume over 2000 MET-min/week was associated with higher CAC prevalence (68%, adjusted OR 3.2 vs under 1000), with plaques more often purely calcified. In MARC-2 (n=289 men, 6 years), volume was not associated with CAC progression; very vigorous intensity (9 MET or more) was associated with more progression (beta +0.05 per 10% of training time) and vigorous intensity (6-9 MET) with less (beta -0.05).
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Almost entirely middle-aged and older male endurance or mixed-sport athletes; women showed no difference. The signal appears at roughly 2000-3000+ MET-min/week, about 1.7-2.5x the upper WHO-equivalent volume (about 1200 MET-min/week).
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Mostly observational and cross-sectional, with self-reported lifelong exercise. MARC-2 implicates intensity, cross-sectional studies implicate volume. Genetics, lipids, smoking and blood pressure dominate CAC risk.
**Sources:** [S527], [S528], [S529], [S530]

### Clinical meaning of exercise-related coronary calcium
**Rule:** Never tell a client that training volume cancels out a known positive CAC score or other cardiovascular risk. Anyone with a CAC finding, chest pain, exertional breathlessness or syncope is managed with their doctor; the coach adjusts training only within that medical advice.
**Evidence:** Lifelong athletes over 2000 MET-min/week with plaque had fewer mixed plaques (48% vs 69%) and more purely calcified plaques (38% vs 16%), and most of 9 outcome studies in a systematic review showed no excess mortality or CV events, suggesting benign plaque. Against that, in the Cooper Center cohort (n=26,724, 20.5 years), 3000 MET-min/week or more was associated with lower all-cause mortality (HR 0.71) but no MI reduction (HR 0.95, 0.72-1.25) vs under 500, while intermediate volumes (500-1499 and 1500-2499) were associated with about 22-23% lower MI risk (HR 0.77, 0.78); CAC predicted coronary events equally in high-volume exercisers (HR 1.29 per log unit; P interaction 0.969).
**Grade:** emerging
**Applies to:** Middle-aged, mostly male US and Dutch exercisers with self-reported volume (Cooper 28% women; Dutch plaque data men only).
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Direct conflict: Ellingson 2026 and Aengevaeren 2017 support benign calcified plaque; Berry 2025 shows CAC keeps its prognostic value regardless of activity and no MI benefit at the highest volume. The mortality benefit is retained either way.
**Sources:** [S523], [S529], [S527]

## Cardio, weight and energy balance

### Exercise alone gives modest weight loss
**Rule:** Tell clients plainly that cardio without dietary change typically moves the scale only about 1.5-3.5 kg (at most about 2 kg at guideline volumes, often less). Meaningful fat loss needs an energy deficit: combine a moderate (not severe) deficit with cardio and resistance training. Still prescribe exercise for visceral fat, fitness, cardiometabolic health, weight-gain prevention, maintenance and muscle retention. If exercise is a major part of a weight-loss plan, aim for more than 250 min/week. Never prescribe large cardio volumes as a substitute for nutrition changes.
**Evidence:** Meta-analysis of 14 RCTs (n=1,847, overweight or obesity): isolated moderate aerobic programmes gave about -1.6 kg at 6 months and -1.7 kg at 12 months, about -2 cm waist; the authors called it "not an effective weight loss therapy" alone. Umbrella review of 12 SR-MAs (149 studies): weight -1.5 to -3.5 kg, fat -1.3 to -2.6 kg, meaningful visceral fat loss (SMD -0.33 to -0.56), no significant effect on maintenance (1 SR-MA). ACSM 2009: 150-250 min/week prevents weight gain but gives only modest loss; more than 250 min/week is associated with clinically significant loss; 150-250 min/week improves loss when combined with moderate diet restriction. Swift 2014: public-health volumes give "up to modest weight loss (~2 kg)". The 2026 AHA statement: activity alone is unlikely to produce 5% or more loss unless aerobic volume is exceptionally high, but it adds to diet-, drug- or surgery-induced loss and improves blood pressure, insulin resistance and lipids independently of weight. In STRRIDE, non-exercising overweight controls gained 1.0 kg over 8 months; about 12 miles/week of walking reversed that (about -0.9 kg).
**Grade:** strong
**Applies to:** Adults with overweight or obesity in controlled trials (mostly moderate aerobic programmes of 3-12 months), including patients on anti-obesity medication or after bariatric surgery (AHA). STRRIDE: sedentary non-dieting 40-65-year-olds with mild-to-moderate dyslipidaemia. Lean recreational lifters would likely see even smaller scale changes.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Responses vary widely through compensation in intake and other expenditure. The over-250 min/week figure is an association; ACSM notes no well-designed RCTs on activity for preventing regain. The umbrella review's null maintenance result rests on one SR-MA.
**Sources:** [S407], [S408], [S409], [S563], [S564], [S538]

### Resistance training during weight loss
**Rule:** Pair a calorie deficit with regular resistance training to protect lean mass. Choose between HIIT and steady cardio by preference, time and joint tolerance, since they are equivalent for fat loss at equal energy cost. Present weight-loss success as a body-composition outcome, not just a scale number.
**Evidence:** During weight loss in adults with overweight or obesity, resistance training reduced lean mass loss by about 0.8 kg (0.4-1.3; one meta-analysis within the Bellicha umbrella review). Aerobic training and HIIT produced no difference in weight, fat or visceral fat loss at equal energy expenditure. ACSM 2009: resistance training does not enhance weight loss but may increase fat-free mass and fat loss and is associated with reduced health risk. Momma 2022 cohort data: maximal mortality, CVD and cancer reduction (about 10-20%) at about 30-60 min/week of muscle strengthening.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Adults with overweight or obesity (weight-loss trials) and general adult cohorts (mortality data). Not from trained lifters.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** The lean-mass estimate comes from one meta-analysis and ACSM words the benefit as "may". The Momma J-curve above about 60 min/week is uncertain and should not be read as harm.
**Sources:** [S407], [S409], [S415]

### HIIT vs MICT for body composition
**Rule:** For fat loss, choose cardio modality on time budget and preference, not "fat-burning zones". Do not sell HIIT or SIT as a better fat-loss method; HIIT saves time but does not burn fat faster per se. Expect modest fat-loss effects from cardio alone; energy balance and resistance training should carry the plan (a practical inference, not tested in these trials).
**Evidence:** Wewege 2017 (13 trials, overweight or obese adults 18-45, about 10 weeks x 3 sessions/week): HIIT and MICT both reduced fat mass and waist with no difference between them, without body-weight change; HIIT took about 40% less time; running produced large fat-mass effects (SMD -0.82 HIIT, -0.85 MICT) while cycling did not. Sultana 2019 (47 studies; low-volume HIIT of 500 MET-min/week or less, at least 4 weeks): no difference in fat mass, body-fat % or lean mass vs no exercise or vs MICT, though CRF improved more. A 2026 network meta-analysis (18 RCTs, 8-16 weeks) found HIIT of 75 min/week or more reduced body-fat by about 3 points (MD -3.05) and waist by about 4.8 cm (MD -4.82) vs control, ranked first, with MICT of 150 min/week or more as an alternative for low tolerance (moderate to low certainty). Cochrane 2026 (58 RCTs, sedentary healthy 18-64): HIIT reduced waist 3.56 cm vs no exercise (-6.14 to -0.98; high certainty) with little to no difference vs MICT (0.06 cm; moderate certainty).
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Normal-weight, overweight and obese adults in short (8-16 weeks), mostly supervised trials. Not tested in lean older adults or over long periods.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** The network meta-analysis ranks HIIT first and reports greater BMI and weight reductions vs control, while Wewege finds no HIIT-MICT difference and no weight change; Sultana found no fat-mass effect vs no exercise, while Cochrane found a small waist reduction. All agree there is no body-composition advantage of HIIT over MICT. The running vs cycling difference is a subgroup finding.
**Sources:** [S423], [S422], [S496], [S482]

### No special "fat-burning zone"
**Rule:** Do not restrict clients to a "fat-burning zone". For fat loss, prescribe the weekly cardio volume (minutes or kcal) the client can sustain, at whatever intensity they enjoy and recover from. Low intensity is a valid choice for comfort, joints, beginners and recovery; higher intensity mainly saves time.
**Evidence:** STRRIDE (8 months, overweight adults 40-65, not dieting): low-amount vigorous jogging (65-80% VO2peak) and low-amount moderate walking (40-55%), equal at about 19 km/week, did not differ (fat mass -2.5 vs -2.0 kg); the high-amount group (about 32 km/week) lost more (-4.8 kg fat), so amount, not intensity, drove results. In 8 obese men doing equicaloric high- vs low-intensity cycling, substrate differences during exercise were compensated afterwards, giving similar 24-h substrate oxidation and energy expenditure. Wewege 2017: no HIIT-MICT difference in body composition, HIIT about 40% less time.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Sedentary to recreationally active adults with overweight; chamber evidence from 8 obese men. HIIT suitability depends on screening and joint tolerance.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** FATmax meta-analyses compare against non-exercising controls, not against higher intensities at matched energy, so they do not show the zone is superior. Popular advice goes beyond the evidence here.
**Sources:** [S538], [S539], [S423]

### FATmax intensity as a sustainable default
**Rule:** For clients with obesity, use about 57-66% of peak HR (easy-to-moderate, conversational) as a sustainable default. For normal-weight beginners a similar easy intensity is a reasonable start, but these HR figures were not derived in that group. Present it as a comfortable intensity for accumulating volume, not a special fat-burning zone; results come from accumulated volume (weeks x minutes).
**Evidence:** Jiang 2026 (19 RCTs, n=644, overweight or obesity): FATmax training vs non-exercising controls -3.66 kg weight, -2.86 kg fat, -4.21 cm waist, optimal total volume about 2,446 min, GRADE mostly very low to moderate. Zhao 2026 (18 RCTs): -4.43 kg weight, -3.68 kg fat. Chavez-Guevara 2023 (64 studies, obesity): FATmax at 61-66% HRpeak for body fat above 35% and 57-64% below 35%; relative HR varied less within individuals than relative VO2 (CV 8.8% vs 17.2%).
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Adults with overweight or obesity, heterogeneous protocols; age, BMI and ethnicity moderated some outcomes. HR references from FATmax tests in mostly middle-aged (40-60) adults with obesity and poor fitness; not directly applicable to lean or fit adults.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** No evidence FATmax beats other intensities at equal energy expenditure. FATmax varies between individuals and by ergometer (higher maximal fat oxidation on the treadmill than the bike). Certainty is low because of heterogeneity.
**Sources:** [S540], [S541], [S542]

### Modality choice for fat loss
**Rule:** Walking is the base for every client. For beginners, heavier clients or anyone with joint pain, use cycling or another low-impact option alongside walking. Weight-bearing options (incline walking, then jogging) may give somewhat more fat loss per session in the data below, and for bone, impact plus resistance training helps while walking alone does little (see Bone health), but beyond that the client's preference wins, because adherence decides: the best modality is the one done consistently. If a non-weight-bearing mode burns less per minute, compensate with more minutes.
**Evidence:** Wewege 2017 (13 trials, overweight or obese 18-45, about 10 weeks): running produced large fat-mass effects for HIIT (SMD -0.82) and MICT (SMD -0.85), while cycling did not produce fat loss. In people with obesity (Chavez-Guevara 2023), maximal fat oxidation was higher on treadmill walking than stationary cycling, and the authors suggested treadmill exercise needs less volume to reach significant fat oxidation.
**Grade:** emerging
**Applies to:** Adults with overweight or obesity: 18-45 in short trials for fat mass; mostly middle-aged low-fitness adults for fat oxidation. A subgroup analysis.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** The running vs cycling contrast is across different trials, not head-to-head, and the cycling null may reflect lower energy expenditure rather than modality. Higher fat oxidation does not by itself mean more fat loss. Older adults and people with knee or hip osteoarthritis may need non-impact options. Cycling HIIT has lower dropout (see Dropout). For bone, walking alone did not change spine BMD in a meta-analysis of 10 trials in women, and consensus guidance recommends impact plus resistance work. The same modality message (walking base, low-impact for beginners, heavier clients or joint pain, otherwise preference) applies across the knowledge files.
**Sources:** [S423], [S542], [S784], [S789]

### Fasted vs fed cardio
**Rule:** Do not prescribe fasted cardio as a fat-loss tool. Let the client train fasted or fed, whichever they prefer and tolerate (e.g. GI discomfort, dizziness, low energy). Focus on the weekly energy deficit, total activity volume and protein, not meal timing around cardio. Clients on glucose-lowering drugs such as insulin or sulfonylureas should get medical guidance before training fasted (a general clinical caution, not tested in these sources).
**Evidence:** Hackett & Hagstrom 2017 (5 studies, 96 healthy adults): trivial fasted vs fed differences for body mass, % fat and lean mass, with limited data. Schoenfeld 2014 (20 healthy young women, hypocaloric diet, 1 h steady cardio 3 times/week for 4 weeks): both groups lost weight and fat, no significant difference. Podesta 2024 (12 active young adults, 4 days of fasted vs fed 75-min runs): no difference in energy intake (15.0 vs 15.0 MJ/day), activity, total expenditure or appetite.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Healthy, mostly young, normal-weight adults doing moderate steady cardio; small, short trials. Little data in older adults or obesity.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Only 5 studies in the meta-analysis, so a small effect cannot be ruled out. Acute studies show more fat burned during the session (next rule). Tolerance varies.
**Sources:** [S532], [S533], [S534]

### Acute fat oxidation is not fat loss
**Rule:** When a client says fasted cardio "burns more fat", explain that a few extra grams of fat oxidised in a session do not add up to more fat lost over weeks; total energy balance decides. Do not promise faster fat loss from training on an empty stomach.
**Evidence:** Vieira 2016 (27 studies, 273 participants, sessions of 120 min or less): fat oxidation about 3 g higher (WMD 3.08 g, 0.79-5.38) during fasted vs fed aerobic exercise. Iwayama 2015 (10 young non-obese men, energy balance): 24-h fat oxidation rose only with 60 min at 50% VO2max before breakfast (717 vs 456 kcal/day sedentary control). Longer trials found no extra fat-mass loss.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Adults in lab trials of aerobic sessions of 120 min or less; the 24-h chamber evidence comes from 10 young non-obese men.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Iwayama points toward a possible benefit, but no adequately powered long-term trial shows it becoming more fat loss. Moderate heterogeneity (I2 39%).
**Sources:** [S535], [S536]

### Eating before longer sessions
**Rule:** For shorter easy-to-moderate cardio, fasted or fed is the client's choice. For long rides or runs, recommend a carbohydrate-containing meal or snack beforehand and count it in the day's intake rather than skipping it to "save calories". Treating sessions over about an hour as "prolonged" is a working assumption; the meta-analysis gives no cutoff. For intervals or other intermittent work, decide by tolerance and preference.
**Evidence:** Aird 2018 (46 studies): eating before exercise improved prolonged aerobic performance (P=.012) but not shorter aerobic performance (P=.687). Fasted exercise raised post-exercise free fatty acids (P=.023). Pre-exercise feeding blunted some muscle and adipose signalling linked to mitochondrial adaptation and substrate use, but the authors say more research is needed.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Healthy adults in lab performance tests. Generalising to beginners is reasonable for comfort and long-session performance; the molecular findings are preliminary.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** "Train-low" adaptation claims come from signalling data and remain unproven for recreational outcomes. GI tolerance varies widely.
**Sources:** [S537]

### Time of day
**Rule:** Schedule cardio whenever the client can do it most consistently. For fat loss, time of day does not matter. Clients with type 2 diabetes can discuss timing with their clinician, but should not let it override adherence.
**Evidence:** Wang 2026 (14 long-term and 5 acute studies, n=765): no consistent difference between morning and afternoon/evening exercise for weight, BMI, fat mass or lipids. Small glycaemic differences: HbA1c fell 0.11% more with morning training (-0.19 to -0.03), mainly in type 2 diabetes, and fasting glucose about 0.13 mmol/L more with afternoon training. Certainty moderate to very low; glycaemic findings preliminary.
**Grade:** emerging
**Applies to:** Adults, including type 2 diabetes, where the glycaemic differences were mainly seen. Heterogeneous trials.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** One 24-h chamber study (Iwayama 2015; 9 young male endurance athletes) found higher 24-h fat oxidation with pre-breakfast exercise (1,142 vs 608 kcal/24 h after lunch); this has not translated into body-composition differences and does not generalise to ordinary or female clients. Chronotype and work schedules make this individual.
**Sources:** [S565], [S566]

### Energy compensation: burned calories under-deliver
**Rule:** On average only part of the calories burned in exercise show up as weight loss, because the body compensates, and the amount varies a lot between people. Plan the deficit mainly through intake. When loss is smaller than the calorie maths predicts, check intake, daily steps and adherence before blaming appetite. Do not let clients eat back more than about half of estimated exercise calories. The weight trend over 2-4 weeks (with waist) is the real calibration.
**Evidence:** In 1,754 adults measured with doubly labelled water (IAEA database), energy compensation averaged 28% through reduced basal expenditure: only about 72% of calories burned by extra activity appeared as extra total daily expenditure, with more compensation at higher body fat. A systematic review of 61 exercise-intervention studies (928 subjects) found weight loss usually smaller than expected: mean energy compensation 18% +/- 93%, about 84% in interventions of about 80 weeks, with initial fat mass, age and duration explaining 48% of the variance; candidate reasons were eating more, spending less energy elsewhere, a small exercise dose and poor compliance. Pontzer 2016 (n=332, five populations): total energy expenditure rose with activity at lower levels but plateaued at the upper range (the constrained model).
**Grade:** emerging
**Applies to:** Free-living adults across populations. Cross-sectional and ecological analyses, not training trials.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Correlational; causality and direction are unclear (adiposity may cause compensation, or the reverse). Compensation varies widely between individuals. The "about half" eat-back limit is a pragmatic heuristic, not derived from these sources. The review lists candidate mechanisms but does not show which one dominates.
**Sources:** [S410], [S411], [S807]

### NEAT: a real but variable lever
**Rule:** Track daily steps alongside workouts. During a deficit, give a daily step target (individualised from baseline) as a NEAT floor so non-exercise movement does not quietly drop, especially on training days. Check step trends if weight loss stalls. Present NEAT as a useful individual lever, not a guaranteed source of large calorie burn.
**Evidence:** Levine 1999 (16 non-obese adults, +1,000 kcal/day for 8 weeks): two-thirds of the rise in daily energy expenditure came from NEAT; NEAT changes accounted for the 10-fold differences in fat storage and predicted resistance to fat gain (r=0.77). Silva 2018 (36 studies, 70 arms, 1,561 adults): compensatory drops in NEAT or non-exercise activity in 26 of 70 arms: 63% of diet-only, 27% of combined and 23% of exercise-only arms. Weight loss was about double in participants whose NEAT fell, suggesting larger deficits provoke more energy conservation, but the review did not support consistent compensation from prescribed exercise and judged the evidence underpowered.
**Grade:** emerging
**Applies to:** Healthy adults. The overfeeding data are from one small uncontrolled study of non-obese volunteers, about overfeeding, not dieting. The review covered free-living adults in trials of at least 7 days, many non-randomised.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Individual differences are large (10-fold in Levine). The review was inconclusive about whether dieting reliably lowers NEAT; compensation seems more likely with aggressive deficits. Popular claims (e.g. NEAT adding up to about 2,000 kcal/day, from a narrative review) go well beyond trial data for typical clients.
**Sources:** [S412], [S413], [S414]

### Appetite after a single session
**Rule:** Reassure clients that a workout does not automatically make them overeat later that day. Use exercise as a genuine short-term deficit, but watch the weekly trend, because compensation can build over weeks in some people.
**Evidence:** Schubert 2013 (29 studies, 51 trials; 30-120 min at 36-81% VO2max; 2-14 h windows): trivial effect on absolute energy intake (ES 0.14, -0.005 to 0.29) and a large effect on relative intake, i.e. a real deficit (ES -1.35). Launbo 2026 (randomised crossover, 58 adults with overweight or obesity, with or without type 2 diabetes): energy intake about 361 kJ lower after HIIE than rest, with no compensation over 24 h; time of day did not matter overall, though those without diabetes ate less after morning sessions.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Adults in lab settings over hours to 24 h; the 2026 trial tested HIIE only.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Short-term lab results do not rule out compensation over weeks or months. The 2026 trial was funded by Novo Nordisk, and some authors are or were employees.
**Sources:** [S553], [S554]

### Compensators vs non-compensators
**Rule:** Expect large differences between clients. If weight loss lags the prediction after 4-8 weeks of consistent training, check for higher intake, more hunger and snacking, and lower daily activity before changing the exercise plan. Address food structure (protein, fibre, planned meals) rather than adding more cardio. Always report non-scale gains too.
**Evidence:** King 2008 (12 weeks supervised, 35 sedentary overweight or obese adults, 5 days/week): mean loss 3.7 kg matched prediction, but individual results ranged from -14.7 to +1.7 kg. Compensators lost 1.5 kg vs 6.3 kg for non-compensators; compensators' intake rose about 268 kcal/day with more hunger, while non-compensators' intake fell about 130 kcal/day with no appetite change. King 2012 adds that exercise improves fitness and health even when mean loss is below 1 kg.
**Grade:** emerging
**Applies to:** Sedentary overweight or obese adults in supervised aerobic training; a single study of 35 people.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Classification may partly reflect measurement noise and regression to the mean. Data on whether compensation is a stable trait are limited.
**Sources:** [S555], [S556]

### Wearable calorie estimates are unreliable
**Rule:** Treat device calorie numbers as rough trend indicators, not numbers to eat back. Do not add wearable "active calories" to the intake target one-for-one; set intake from the 2-4 week weight trend. If a client insists on eating back exercise calories, suggest no more than about half of the device figure, and say plainly that this is a pragmatic heuristic, not an evidence-derived number.
**Evidence:** Shcherbina 2017 (7 wrist devices vs indirect calorimetry, 60 adults): no device had energy-expenditure error below 20%; error was highest during walking and for males, higher BMI and darker skin. Fuller 2020 (158 publications): "for energy expenditure, no brand was accurate". Choe 2025 (Apple Watch, 56 studies): MAPE above the 10% threshold in every energy-expenditure subgroup, limits of agreement about -2.1 to +2.7 kcal/min. O'Driscoll 2020 (60 studies): accuracy varied by activity and improved when combining HR with accelerometry.
**Grade:** strong
**Applies to:** Healthy adults using commercial wrist or arm devices, mostly in lab protocols; new models outpace validation.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Error direction is not consistent (next rule). Error is larger for some users (higher BMI, darker skin, walking), so a device may be systematically off for one person. Heterogeneity was large (O'Driscoll I2 above 75% for many devices).
**Sources:** [S543], [S440], [S544], [S545]

### Direction of calorie error: wearables vary, machines inflate
**Rule:** Treat cardio-machine calorie figures with suspicion. On ellipticals, discount them by about 100 kcal per 30 minutes of moderate work, or ignore them. For other machines, assume they are probably inflated but apply no fixed correction, because none was verified. Never assume a wearable errs in a known direction for a given person. If an exercise calorie estimate is needed, use a distance-based estimate for walking and running. Entering the client's body weight, age and sex into the machine may reduce error.
**Evidence:** Chevance 2022 (combined-sensing Fitbits, 52 studies): average underestimation 2.77 kcal/min (k=29, after excluding high risk-of-bias), population limits of agreement -12.75 to +7.41 kcal/min. Choe 2025: Apple Watch small positive bias (+0.30 kcal/min). Glave 2018 (Precor EFX 883 elliptical vs indirect calorimetry, 34 adults, mean age 25): significant overestimation growing with duration (about 19 kcal at 5 min, about 120 kcal at 25 min); the authors advise expecting about 100 kcal overestimation per 30 min of moderate elliptical exercise and cite similar deviations for other ellipticals, rowers and equipment.
**Grade:** emerging
**Applies to:** Healthy adults; machine evidence from one elliptical model in young adults. Other brands and machine types not verified.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** The belief that "wearables overestimate" is not consistently supported: Fitbit underestimates, Apple Watch slightly overestimates. Machine-specific percentages circulating online (e.g. treadmills 13%, bikes 7%) could not be traced to a primary source.
**Sources:** [S546], [S544], [S547]

### Gross vs net exercise calories
**Rule:** Explain that calorie figures built from METs are gross: they include the roughly 1 kcal/kg/h the body burns at rest anyway. The extra cost of a session is about (METs minus 1) x body weight in kg x hours. Use this when a client asks how much a workout really adds, label it an approximation, and do not claim to know how a specific device reports calories.
**Evidence:** The 2024 Adult Compendium of Physical Activities defines METs as multiples of a standard resting rate of 3.5 mL/kg/min, approximately 1.0 kcal/kg/h. In 6 adults walking on a treadmill, resting (1.08 W/kg) and standing (1.25 W/kg) rates made up large fractions (16-58%) of the gross walking cost, and the authors suggest subtracting resting metabolic rate to obtain net cost.
**Grade:** emerging
**Applies to:** Healthy adults; the partition data come from 6 people walking. That wearables report gross values is an inference, not tested in these sources.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Real resting rate varies between people. Subtracting standing instead of resting rate changes net walking cost by up to 16.0% at slow speeds.
**Sources:** [S808], [S809]

### Walking vs running energy cost
**Rule:** Estimate running at about 1 kcal per kg per km (about 70 kcal/km for a 70 kg person) regardless of pace. Walking at ordinary speeds costs less per km, but the gap shrinks for heavier people and at faster or slower walking speeds. Gross figures include resting energy that would be spent anyway, so the extra burn is smaller than the total, especially for slow walking. Recommend walking for injury-free volume and running for more calories per km and per minute in clients who tolerate it.
**Evidence:** Hall 2004 (n=24; walking 1.41 m/s, running 2.82 m/s): 1,600 m cost 481 kJ running vs 340 kJ walking on the treadmill and 480 vs 334 kJ on the track (gross, about 40% more for running); ACSM equations predicted running closely, ACSM and Pandolf walking. Wilkin 2012 (n=30; walking at 86 vs running at 160 m/min): 373 vs 471 kJ over 1,600 m, 463 vs 664 kJ including 30 min recovery. Mayhew 1977 (9 trained male runners): about 0.97 kcal/kg/km, independent of speed. Loftin 2010 gross per-mile estimate: kcal = 0.789 x mass (kg) - 7.634 x sex (men=1, women=2) + 51.1 (R=0.795, SEE 10.9 kcal).
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Healthy adults (trained male runners in Mayhew; young adults of average fitness, mean age 22, in Wilkin; normal-weight and overweight walkers and marathon runners in Loftin). Body mass is the main driver of absolute cost.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Loftin found similar energy per mile for normal-weight walkers (93.9 kcal), overweight walkers (98.4) and marathon runners (99.3) at preferred speeds, a between-group comparison showing body mass and speed can narrow the gap. The popular "0.5 vs 1 kcal/kg/km net" rule could not be confirmed for walking. Walking cost per km is U-shaped with speed, and subtracting standing cost changes results most at slow speeds (Abe 2015, 11 trained male athletes).
**Sources:** [S548], [S549], [S550], [S551], [S552]

### Activity volume to prevent weight regain
**Rule:** Once a client reaches goal weight, shift the activity target upward, not downward: aim toward more than 250 min/week of at least moderate activity (or a high daily step count plus structured sessions). Present it as the main maintenance lever alongside diet monitoring and self-weighing. Flag any sustained drop in activity as an early regain risk.
**Evidence:** ACSM 2009: cross-sectional and prospective studies show better maintenance with more than 250 min/week after weight loss, but no well-designed RCTs exist for activity preventing regain. National Weight Control Registry (NWCR) entrants (n=3,683) report about 2,621 ± 2,252 kcal/week of activity (25% below 1,000, 35% above 3,000). In 26 NWCR members, maintainers spent 41.5 min/day in MVPA bouts of 10 min or more vs 19.2 in overweight controls. Doubly labelled water: activity energy expenditure 812 kcal/day in maintainers vs 621 (normal-weight) and 637 (overweight or obese) controls. Over 10 years (n=2,886), drops in leisure activity were associated with greater regain.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Adults after meaningful weight loss (NWCR: at least 13.6 kg lost and kept off at least 1 year). NWCR data are self-selected, about 76-78% female and largely self-reported; objective studies are small (n=25-26 maintainers). Older adults may meet the volume with more light activity and walking.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Largely observational. The NWCR authors stress activity is too variable for a single recommendation. RCT meta-analyses of combined diet and activity maintenance show small effects: Dombrowski 2014 -1.56 kg less regain at 12 months (-2.27 to -0.86); Ghoreishy 2025 -0.3 kg (-4.13 to 3.52, crossing zero).
**Sources:** [S409], [S557], [S558], [S559], [S560], [S561], [S562]

## Concurrent training (cardio plus lifting)

### Cardio does not kill gains at moderate doses
**Rule:** Tell clients that moderate amounts of cardio will not meaningfully blunt whole-muscle size or maximal strength gains, and that the health benefits justify including it. A practical default of 2-3 sessions/week of 20-40 min is an extrapolation, not a tested threshold. For clients chasing power or jumping performance, or doing very high cardio volumes, manage interference actively (see scheduling and modality rules).
**Evidence:** Schumann 2022 (43 studies, healthy adults, supervised training of at least 4 weeks): adding aerobic training to an identical strength program did not compromise whole-muscle hypertrophy (SMD -0.01, p=0.919) or maximal strength (SMD -0.06, -0.20 to 0.09); explosive strength was attenuated (SMD -0.28, -0.48 to -0.08); no difference by type of aerobic training (cycling vs running), frequency of concurrent training (more vs fewer than 5 weekly sessions; in the methods, a low frequency of 4.1 ± 0.3 vs a high frequency of 6.1 ± 1.6 weekly sessions, against 2.0 ± 0.3 vs 3.1 ± 0.6 weekly sessions in the strength-only comparison groups), training status or age (under vs over 40). Wilson 2012 (21 studies, 422 effect sizes): numerically smaller effects with concurrent vs strength-only training for hypertrophy (0.85 vs 1.23), strength (1.44 vs 1.76) and power (0.55 vs 0.91), with only the power difference reported as significant. Lundberg 2022 (15 studies): small negative effect on muscle-fibre hypertrophy (SMD -0.23, -0.46 to -0.00, p=0.050).
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Healthy adults in supervised trials of at least 4 weeks, mostly untrained or recreationally active, with matched strength programs and moderate aerobic doses. Limited data for highly trained lifters and high endurance volumes.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Wilson found a significant power deficit and linked interference to running, frequency and duration; Schumann found no effect on hypertrophy or maximal strength; Lundberg shows a small fibre-level effect. Interference probably grows with aerobic volume and training age.
**Sources:** [S089], [S092], [S100]

### Modality, frequency and duration of cardio
**Rule:** For a client prioritising leg size or strength, keep total cardio volume moderate and do not stack long or frequent sessions on top of heavy leg training. Walking stays the base; choose any additional modality by preference, joint tolerance and adherence (see Modality choice for fat loss): steady-state cycling has some supporting data (Wilson, Lundberg), and for HIIT the Sabag data lean slightly towards running. Do not tell clients one modality is proven "safe" and the other "harmful".
**Evidence:** Wilson 2012: running, not cycling, combined with resistance training significantly reduced hypertrophy and strength; outcomes correlated negatively with endurance frequency (r -0.26 to -0.35) and duration (r -0.29 to -0.75). Lundberg 2022: running reduced type I fibre hypertrophy (SMD -0.81, -1.26 to -0.36), cycling did not. Schumann 2022: no modality effect on whole-muscle outcomes. Sabag 2018: concurrent HIIT plus resistance training gave similar hypertrophy and upper-body strength to resistance alone but slightly less lower-body strength (ES -0.25, p=0.049), trending worse with cycling HIIT (ES -0.38, p=0.074) than running HIIT (ES -0.18, p=0.261); the authors suggest running HIIT and longer rest between modes.
**Grade:** emerging
**Applies to:** Healthy adults in mostly short trials (about 4-16 weeks); mixed trained and untrained subjects.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Modality findings contradict each other: running looked worse in Wilson and Lundberg (mostly continuous), cycling HIIT trended worse in Sabag, and Schumann found no modality effect. Dose correlations are across studies.
**Sources:** [S092], [S095], [S100]

### Scheduling and order
**Rule:** If strength is the priority and both are done in one visit, lift first and do cardio after. When practical, separate hard cardio from leg training by at least 3 hours or put it on different days, especially for clients who care about power or jumping. For pure health or fat-loss clients, choose whatever order and timing they will stick to.
**Evidence:** Eddens 2018 (10 studies): resistance before endurance in the same session gave greater lower-body dynamic strength gains than the reverse (+6.91%, 1.96-11.87); order made no difference to hypertrophy, static strength, VO2max or body fat. Schumann 2022: explosive strength attenuation was significant when both were done in the same session, but not when separated by at least 3 hours.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Healthy adults in mostly short trials; the order effect applies to lower-body dynamic strength.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** The order effect is small and limited to one outcome. Hypertrophy is unaffected by order, so convenience can win when hypertrophy or health is the goal.
**Sources:** [S098], [S089]

## Conflicts and open questions

- **Steps above about 8,000-10,000/day.** Paluch and Stens report a plateau (age-dependent), Banach reports continued benefit to 20,000 steps/day. All agree benefit starts low. [S398], [S400], [S401]
- **Walking cadence.** One NHANES cohort found no cadence effect after adjusting for total steps; two meta-analyses found partial or independent effects depending on the metric. [S402], [S398], [S400]
- **Bouts under 10 minutes.** WHO counts them firmly, but the evidence is observational and no RCT has tested bouts under 10 min; WHO's strength of recommendation outruns the data, so this file grades it moderate. [S016], [S397]
- **Weekend warrior vs regular.** Point estimates slightly favour spreading activity; a small advantage cannot be excluded. [S509], [S510], [S511]
- **Upper end of volume.** Total mortality shows no harm at 10x the minimum, but no MI reduction at the highest volume (Cooper), an AF signal beyond about 78 MET-h/week, and higher CAC in very high-volume men. Whether that CAC is benign (Aengevaeren, Ellingson) or keeps its usual prognostic meaning (Berry) is disputed; MARC-2 implicates very high intensity rather than volume. [S521], [S523], [S524], [S526], [S527], [S529], [S530]
- **Size of HIIT's advantage over MICT for VO2max.** Ranges from none (Gist) to about 1.2-1.4 mL/kg/min in healthy adults (Milanovic, Cochrane, low certainty) to about 3 mL/kg/min in cardiometabolic disease (Weston). [S420], [S421], [S482], [S483]
- **Fewer sprints vs longer intervals.** For SIT, more sprints add nothing (Vollaard group analyses); for HIIT generally, long intervals and higher volume gave the largest effects (Wen). [S484], [S485], [S486], [S487]
- **Zone 2.** Popular claims that Zone 2 is optimal rest mainly on elite-athlete observational data; a critical narrative review disagrees, with an author equity disclosure noted. [S426], [S420]
- **HIIT and fat.** Sultana found no fat-mass effect from low-volume HIIT vs no exercise; Cochrane found a small, high-certainty waist reduction; the network meta-analysis ranks HIIT first vs control. None shows HIIT beating MICT. Running vs cycling for fat loss rests on a subgroup analysis. [S496], [S482], [S422], [S423]
- **HIIT and insulin.** Jelleyman found HIIT better than continuous training for insulin resistance; Zeng found no HIIT-MICT difference for HbA1c or HOMA-IR in type 2 diabetes. [S493], [S495]
- **HIIT enjoyment.** Oliveira and Metcalfe-group data show small enjoyment advantages or manageable affect with few short sprints; Ekkekakis argues high intensity is usually less pleasant and the methods are flawed. [S497], [S498], [S499]
- **HIIT in older adults and longevity.** Generation 100 found no significant mortality effect; the HIIT trend was exploratory and the control group's own HIIT diluted the contrast. [S425]
- **Best HRmax formula.** Shookster favoured 220 - age (no proportional bias); Tanaka and Nes found it underestimates in older adults. Individual error dominates either way. [S427], [S428], [S436], [S437]
- **RPE as a primary method.** The PAG endorses the 0-10 scale and talk test for the public; ACSM classes them as adjuncts. Under beta-blockade, RPE is "useful and reliable" (Mitchell) but can drift harder (Zanettini) and is harder to interpret (EAPC). [S433], [S396], [S448], [S449], [S447]
- **Trainability.** Bouchard/HERITAGE and the Ross 2019 consensus hold that genetic differences in response are real; Williamson, Atkinson and Batterham argue most apparent variability is measurement error. Dose escalation converts most non-responders. [S469], [S475], [S476], [S477], [S473], [S474]
- **Non-exercise CRF in practice.** Recommended for clinical use, yet about half of individuals are misclassified. [S453], [S454]
- **Concurrent training modality.** Running looked worse for continuous cardio (Wilson, Lundberg), cycling HIIT trended worse for leg strength (Sabag), and Schumann found no modality effect. [S092], [S095], [S100], [S089]
- **Wearable calorie error direction.** "Wearables overestimate" is not consistently true: Fitbit underestimates, Apple Watch slightly overestimates; machine-specific percentages circulating online are untraced. [S546], [S544], [S547]
- **Popular claims that outrun evidence.** Fasted cardio for fat loss (more fat oxidised acutely, no extra fat lost) [S532], [S535], [S536]; the "fat-burning zone" (no advantage at matched energy) [S538], [S539]; NEAT adding up to about 2,000 kcal/day [S414]; the "0.5 vs 1 kcal/kg/km net" walking vs running rule [S549], [S552]; 10,000 steps as a threshold [S398], [S399].

## When to refer out

- Chest pain or pressure, unusual breathlessness, fainting, dizziness or palpitations, at intake or at any time during training: stop and get medical clearance before continuing. [S119], [S396], [S531]
- Known cardiovascular, metabolic or renal disease (ACSM 2015): currently inactive, medical clearance before starting any programme, then start light to moderate; currently active without symptoms, continue moderate work and get clearance before progressing to vigorous work or HIIT. [S119], [S432]
- Over 45 and new to vigorous effort, or any PAR-Q+ "yes": qualified exercise professional or clinician review before vigorous or maximal work (HIIT, SIT, REHIT, all-out sprints). [S641]
- High or unknown resting blood pressure: follow the BP bands stated once in recovery-habits.md. [S661]
- Family history of sudden death or inherited cardiac conditions: medical input regardless of fitness. [S119]
- Beta-blockers or other rate-limiting medication: no formula-based HR zones; clearance and, ideally, a supervised exercise test on current medication. [S445], [S447]
- Diabetes is known metabolic disease: clearance per the ACSM algorithm above; clients on glucose-lowering drugs such as insulin or sulfonylureas also get clinician input before fasted training (a general clinical caution, not tested in this file's sources). [S119]
- Any known positive CAC score: training adjusted only within medical advice. [S523], [S529]
- Long-term high-volume endurance clients with palpitations, irregular pulse, unexplained performance drop or dizziness: refer for AF assessment. [S524], [S526]
- Frail older clients or those with a history of falls: consider physiotherapist input for balance and strength work. [S016]
