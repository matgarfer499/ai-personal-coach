# Recovery, habits and safety

> Part of the coach's knowledge base. Loaded on demand by the coach commands (/plan, /review, /menu, /setup). Rules, not a literature review. Source IDs refer to knowledge/sources.md. Evidence checked on 2026-10-24.

## How to read this file

Each rule carries a grade. **Strong**: official guidelines or several consistent meta-analyses or RCTs agree, so apply the rule by default. **Moderate**: at least one good meta-analysis, consistent trials or a consensus statement with clear limitations, so apply it and adjust to the person. **Emerging**: small trials, observational data, single studies or expert opinion, so present it as reasonable practice, not proven fact. Much of this evidence comes from young men, athletes, laboratory protocols or clinical populations; the "Applies to" line says who was actually studied, and the coach serves ordinary adults, so extrapolations are flagged. The coach screens and refers; it never diagnoses or prescribes medication.

## Key rules at a glance

- Aim for 7 h or more of actual sleep on most nights (7-8 h from age 65); treat short sleep as a modifiable barrier, not discipline. (strong) [S567], [S568]
- Expect more hunger after short nights (roughly 250-400 kcal/day in lab studies) and plan the food environment for it. (moderate) [S571], [S572], [S573]
- Sleep hygiene tips are general advice only; persistent insomnia means referral for CBT-I. (strong) [S584], [S585], [S586]
- Caffeine curfew: no coffee within about 9 h of bed, no high-dose pre-workout within about 13 h. (moderate) [S592]
- After a bad night, train anyway: move the session earlier if possible and auto-regulate load; no 1RM tests. (moderate) [S576]
- Deloads are optional and reactive: when needed, one week with volume cut by about 30-50% and loads kept. (emerging) [S134], [S136], [S137]
- A short break does not erase progress: under about 2 weeks off, resume at about 90% of previous loads; after longer breaks, start 10-20% below and rebuild over 1-3 weeks. (moderate) [S064], [S138]
- Strength training, not stretching, is the main injury-prevention tool; lifting through full ROM is mobility work for most people. (moderate) [S600], [S601], [S604], [S605]
- Keep people with chronic non-specific low back pain training; do not prescribe rest. (strong) [S613], [S614], [S615]
- Keep people with tendinopathy or other assessed chronic pain training with tolerable, settling pain and progressive loading; do not prescribe rest. (moderate) [S608], [S609], [S610], [S611], [S612]
- Daytime sleepiness plus two of loud snoring, witnessed apnoeas or high blood pressure: suggest a doctor (sleep-study trigger); never diagnose. (strong) [S810], [S811]
- Clients 65+ with grip under 27 kg (men) or 16 kg (women), five chair rises over 15 s, falls or weakness: suggest a sarcopenia assessment and keep training. (strong) [S814]
- Turn intention into a when/where/how plan with if-then backups, plus tracking tied to a goal and a regular review. (moderate) [S617], [S618], [S263], [S622], [S623], [S624], [S625], [S626]
- Expect about 2 months or more, not 21 days, for a habit to feel automatic; one missed session does not undo it. (emerging) [S627], [S628], [S629]
- Screen with the full PAR-Q+ and the ACSM logic; do not send every user to a doctor first, but stop and refer on any cardiac warning symptom. (moderate) [S641], [S119], [S120]
- Build intensity gradually in sedentary or detrained users; acute cardiac risk is concentrated in the unaccustomed, so no all-out sprints (SIT, REHIT) for sedentary beginners. (strong) [S643], [S644], [S531]
- Fever, systemic illness or chest symptoms mean no training; return after illness in graded, symptom-led stages. (moderate) [S758], [S759], [S747], [S760]
- Dark urine or muscle pain far beyond normal soreness after training means same-day medical care (possible rhabdomyolysis). (moderate) [S761], [S648], [S651]
- Eating-disorder red flags and pregnancy warning signs trigger referral; the coach never makes the medical call. (strong) [S671], [S672], [S673], [S707], [S708]
- Diabetes: the diabetes team owns medication and glucose targets; the coach applies the hypoglycaemia 15 g rule mid-session and refers. (strong) [S658], [S733]
- Resting BP bands decide whether a client trains, trains lightly or waits for a doctor: no exercise at 200/110 or higher, no training until a GP review at 180 systolic or higher. (emerging) [S734], [S641], [S735], [S746]

## Sleep

### Sleep duration target
**Rule:** Set a default goal of 7 h or more of actual sleep (so about 7.5-8.5 h in bed) on most nights; for clients aged 65 or over aim for 7-8 h. Ask about habitual sleep at intake and treat less than 7 h as a modifiable barrier to fat loss and recovery, not a badge of discipline. Do not push people who naturally sleep 9 h to cut back.
**Evidence:** The AASM/SRS joint consensus states adults should sleep 7 h or more per night on a regular basis; regularly sleeping less than 7 h is associated with weight gain, diabetes, heart disease, depression, impaired immunity, accidents and higher mortality. More than 9 h may be appropriate for young adults, people recovering from sleep debt or ill people; for others it is uncertain whether more than 9 h carries risk. The National Sleep Foundation panel recommends 7-9 h for young adults and adults and 7-8 h for older adults (65 and over), noting needs vary between individuals.
**Grade:** strong
**Applies to:** Healthy adults without a sleep disorder. Consensus recommendations (RAND/UCLA appropriateness method), mostly based on observational associations with health outcomes rather than RCTs.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Sleep need varies individually; the numbers are population-level. Associations of short sleep with disease are largely observational. AASM/SRS gives a floor (7 h or more) while NSF gives ranges (7-9 h; 7-8 h for 65 and over).
**Sources:** [S567], [S568]

### Protect sleep during a calorie deficit
**Rule:** During a fat-loss phase, treat sleep as part of the plan: ask clients to protect 7 h or more, and if they are chronically short-sleeping, fix sleep before making the deficit more aggressive. Do not rely on weekend catch-up sleep to offset weekday restriction. Track body composition or waist, not just scale weight.
**Evidence:** In a 14-day crossover RCT (n=10 overweight adults, 7 men, mean age 41) under moderate caloric restriction, 5.5 h vs 8.5 h sleep opportunity reduced fat lost from 1.4 to 0.6 kg (a 55% lower proportion of weight lost as fat) and increased fat-free mass loss from 1.5 to 2.4 kg (+60%), with more hunger. In an 8-week RCT (n=36 overweight/obese adults on caloric restriction), cutting about 1 h of time in bed on 5 nights/week (net -169 min/week despite weekend catch-up sleep) produced similar weight, lean and fat mass loss, but a significantly smaller proportion of the total loss as fat (p=0.016).
**Grade:** emerging
**Applies to:** Overweight/obese adults on a calorie-restricted diet without structured resistance training; 10 participants over 2 weeks in a laboratory and 36 participants over 8 weeks. Whether resistance training plus high protein offsets the effect is untested.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** The two small RCTs agree on direction but differ in magnitude: the lab study found large fat-free mass losses, while the 8-week trial found similar absolute lean and fat losses with only the proportion of loss as fat differing. The effect size in people who lift and eat enough protein is unknown.
**Sources:** [S569], [S570]

### Short sleep raises intake
**Rule:** When a client reports a run of short nights, expect more hunger and overeating (on average roughly 250-400 kcal/day in lab studies). Plan for it: keep protein and high-volume foods easy to reach, keep tempting snacks out of reach on those days, and do not read one hungry, off-plan day after poor sleep as a failure of willpower.
**Evidence:** Meta-analyses of experimental studies find that partial sleep restriction increases energy intake. One found +385 kcal/day (95% CI 252-517; 11 studies, n=172), with higher fat and lower protein intake and no change in total energy expenditure or RMR. A meta-analysis of 41 RCTs in healthy adults found +252.8 kcal/day, subjective hunger +13.4 mm on a 100 mm scale, 0.34 kg weight gain over the study periods, lower insulin sensitivity (SMD -0.70) and no strong evidence of an effect on energy expenditure. An earlier systematic review of RCTs (Capers 2015) also found increased food intake, but noted that total energy expenditure rose too and that effects on weight change were inconsistent.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Healthy adults in short (days to weeks), often laboratory studies with free access to food and small pooled samples. Long-term free-living effects, and effects in people with obesity or older adults, are less certain.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** The meta-analyses agree on direction but the size varies (about 250 to 385 kcal/day). Capers 2015 found sleep restriction also increased total energy expenditure and had inconsistent effects on weight, while Al Khatib found no change in expenditure. Individual responses vary.
**Sources:** [S571], [S572], [S573]

### Do not blame "hormone imbalance" for post-short-sleep hunger
**Rule:** Explain post-short-sleep hunger as real and expected, but do not tell clients it is caused by a "hormone imbalance" that needs fixing with supplements or special foods. The practical fix is more sleep and a planned food environment.
**Evidence:** A meta-analysis of 41 RCTs found no strong evidence that sleep restriction changes mean leptin or ghrelin, despite clear increases in hunger and intake, and reported changes in brain activity in reward and cognitive-control regions in response to food stimuli. Another meta-analysis (21 studies, n=2250) found small effects: ghrelin higher with short sleep (SMD 0.14) and, against the popular story, leptin also higher in experimental studies (SMD 0.19) and with sleep deprivation (SMD 0.24).
**Grade:** emerging
**Applies to:** Healthy adults in short-term sleep restriction RCTs (Zhu). Lin 2020 pooled experimental and cross-sectional studies and does not specify age range in its abstract (it may include non-adult samples).
**Individual variation / conflicts:** The two meta-analyses disagree on whether hormone levels change, and the direction for leptin goes against the popular story. Brain reward and food-cue responses may matter more than circulating hormones.
**Sources:** [S572], [S574]

### Sleep extension as a fat-loss lever for short sleepers
**Rule:** For clients who habitually sleep less than 6.5 h and want to lose fat, offer extending time in bed (an earlier, consistent bedtime aiming for about 8-8.5 h in bed) as an early, low-cost intervention alongside nutrition work.
**Evidence:** In an RCT of 80 adults aged 21-40 with overweight (BMI 25-29.9) who habitually slept less than 6.5 h, a single individualised sleep-hygiene counselling session targeting 8.5 h in bed increased sleep by about 1.2 h/night. Over the 2-week intervention, objectively measured energy intake fell by 270 kcal/day (95% CI -393 to -147) vs controls, with no change in total energy expenditure, and weight fell by 0.87 kg relative to controls (95% CI -1.39 to -0.35), with no diet or activity prescription.
**Grade:** emerging
**Applies to:** Young adults (21-40) with overweight and habitual short sleep; one single-centre RCT with a 2-week intervention. Unknown for normal sleepers, people with obesity, older adults or the long term.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** A single short RCT. An earlier systematic review (Capers 2015) found few controlled trials of increased sleep and called for more. The benefit is likely limited to genuine short sleepers.
**Sources:** [S575], [S573]

### Sleep hygiene is advice, CBT-I is treatment
**Rule:** Give sleep hygiene tips (regular schedule, caffeine and alcohol limits, dark, quiet bedroom) as general advice only. If a client reports trouble falling or staying asleep 3 or more nights/week for months, or daytime impairment, say that a hygiene checklist is not a treatment and refer them to a GP or sleep clinician for CBT-I (including digital CBT-I). Do not diagnose insomnia or recommend sleep medication.
**Evidence:** For chronic insomnia, the AASM (2021, GRADE) strongly recommends multicomponent CBT-I and conditionally recommends against sleep hygiene as a stand-alone treatment. The ACP (2016) recommends CBT-I as initial treatment for all adults with chronic insomnia (strong recommendation, moderate-quality evidence). A review found individual sleep hygiene components are linked to sleep, but their direct effects in the general population remain "largely untested".
**Grade:** strong
**Applies to:** Adults with chronic insomnia disorder (guidelines); sleep hygiene evidence for the general non-clinical population is limited.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Sleep hygiene may still help people without insomnia whose short sleep comes from habits or scheduling, but this is mostly untested. The AASM recommendation against stand-alone hygiene is conditional.
**Sources:** [S584], [S585], [S586]

### Possible sleep apnoea: suggest a doctor
**Rule:** Do not screen for or diagnose sleep apnoea. If a client reports excessive daytime sleepiness together with at least two of loud habitual snoring, witnessed breathing pauses or gasping or choking at night, or diagnosed high blood pressure, suggest they see a doctor and mention these symptoms, because that combination is the guideline trigger for a sleep study. Snoring or sleepiness alone is still worth mentioning to a GP (a coaching convention). Do not treat an online questionnaire score as a verdict.
**Evidence:** The AASM (2017) recommends polysomnography or home sleep apnoea testing for uncomplicated adults with signs and symptoms of increased risk of moderate to severe OSA, defined as excessive daytime sleepiness plus at least two of habitual loud snoring, witnessed apnoea or gasping or choking, or diagnosed hypertension; clinical questionnaires and prediction tools should not be used alone to diagnose OSA. The USPSTF (2022) found the evidence insufficient to assess screening in the general adult population (I statement).
**Grade:** strong
**Applies to:** Adults; clinical diagnostic and screening guidelines, not specific to gym-goers.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** The USPSTF statement covers screening adults without recognised symptoms, while the AASM covers testing people who already have symptoms; the two do not conflict.
**Sources:** [S810], [S811]

### Training improves sleep
**Rule:** Present regular training (resistance training, ideally with some aerobic work) as an effective, evidence-based way to improve sleep quality, especially for poor sleepers. For poor sleepers aged 50 and over, prioritise combined aerobic and resistance training, done frequently (benefit was largest with daily delivery).
**Evidence:** A meta-analysis of 66 studies found regular exercise has small benefits on total sleep time and sleep efficiency, small-to-medium benefits on time to fall asleep, and moderate benefits on sleep quality. A systematic review of 13 RCTs found chronic resistance training improves all aspects of sleep, most of all quality, while acute effects are poorly studied and inconsistent. A 2026 network meta-analysis (132 RCTs, n=10,872 adults aged 50 and over with poor sleep, PSQI 5 or more) found combined aerobic plus resistance training had the largest effect on sleep quality (SMD -1.56, 95% credible interval -2.08 to -1.03); only combined training, acupressure and aerobic exercise alone clearly exceeded the minimum clinically important difference (SMD -0.89).
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Adults generally, with the strongest effects in poor sleepers; the 2026 NMA included only adults aged 50 and over with PSQI 5 or more, with maximal benefit around PSQI 16. Effects depend on sex, age, baseline activity, exercise type, time of day, duration and adherence.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Effects are small in good sleepers. Acute effects of a single resistance session are inconsistent. Kovacevic found the benefit of resistance training alone was attenuated compared with aerobic or combined training. NMA effect sizes may be inflated by small trials.
**Sources:** [S587], [S588], [S589]

### Evening training is fine for most people
**Rule:** Do not tell clients to avoid evening training; adherence matters more. If someone trains late and sleeps poorly, finish hard sessions at least about 1 h before bed (ideally earlier) or lower the intensity of late sessions, and check whether caffeine from pre-workout is the real cause.
**Evidence:** A meta-analysis of 23 studies in healthy adults found a single evening exercise session did not impair sleep and slightly increased slow-wave sleep (+1.3 percentage points); sleep onset, total sleep time and efficiency might be impaired only after vigorous exercise ending 1 h or less before bedtime. A 2026 meta-analysis of 16 RCTs comparing morning and evening exercise found no clear overall advantage for morning exercise, but evening/night exercise was associated with more time awake after sleep onset (SMD -0.51, 95% CI -0.89 to -0.12, reported as higher WASO in the evening condition). Timing differences were more apparent at higher intensities, and possible small-study effects were identified.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Healthy adults; the Stutz meta-analysis covers single sessions only. Chronotype and sensitivity vary.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Partial conflict: Stutz 2019 found no harm or a slight benefit, while the 2026 meta-analysis found modestly worse sleep continuity with evening moderate-to-vigorous exercise. In Stutz, all significant moderator effects disappeared after removing one study. Use the client's own sleep feedback.
**Sources:** [S590], [S591]

### Caffeine curfew
**Rule:** Make a caffeine curfew the default: no coffee within about 9 h of bedtime and no high-dose pre-workout within about 13 h (for an 11 pm bedtime, coffee before about 2 pm and no pre-workout for evening sessions). Ask about pre-workout and energy drinks whenever a client reports poor sleep.
**Evidence:** A meta-analysis of 24 studies found caffeine reduced total sleep time by 45 min and sleep efficiency by 7%, increased time to fall asleep by 9 min and time awake after sleep onset by 12 min, and reduced deep sleep by 11.4 min. To avoid losing total sleep time, a coffee (107 mg) should be drunk at least 8.8 h before bed and a standard pre-workout serving (217.5 mg) at least 13.2 h before bed.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Healthy adults; cut-off times are model-derived averages.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Caffeine metabolism and sensitivity vary widely (genetics, habitual use, oral contraceptives, smoking); some people tolerate later intake.
**Sources:** [S592]

### Alcohol is not a sleep aid
**Rule:** Correct the "nightcap helps me sleep" belief: even a low dose (0.50 g/kg or less; about 3.5 Spanish standard drinks, UBE of 10 g, at 70 kg, fewer for lighter people) disrupts REM sleep. If clients drink, suggest smaller amounts, drinking earlier in the evening and not using alcohol to get to sleep, especially in hard training or fat-loss phases.
**Evidence:** A meta-analysis of 27 studies found alcohol before bed delays and reduces REM sleep in a dose-dependent way, starting at low doses (0.50 g/kg or less; the authors call this about two standard drinks, which at 70 kg is 35 g, about 3.5 UBE). Falling asleep faster was seen only after high doses (0.85 g/kg or more; the authors' "about five drinks", which at 70 kg is about 60 g, about 6 UBE), which further worsen REM disruption. Effects on total sleep time, efficiency and time awake after sleep onset were too uncertain to estimate.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Healthy adults in laboratory studies.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Large uncertainty for total sleep time and efficiency; sex differences and individual alcohol metabolism are unstudied.
**Sources:** [S593]

## Sleep loss and training

### Train after a short night, but auto-regulate
**Rule:** After a short night, do not cancel training. If possible, move the session earlier in the day, keep the planned exercises and auto-regulate load (use RPE/RIR and accept lower top sets). Do not test 1RMs or chase PRs after a bad night.
**Evidence:** Across 69 studies (227 outcomes; 89% male participants), sleep loss (6 h or less in 24 h) reduced exercise performance by an average of 7.56% (95% CI -11.9 to -3.13; I2 = 98.1%), with significant effects in every exercise category (anaerobic power, speed/power endurance, HIIT, strength, endurance, strength-endurance, skill). Consistent harm was seen only with total deprivation and late restriction (waking earlier than usual), not with delayed bedtime; within those protocols performance fell about 0.4% for every extra hour awake before exercise. Afternoon/evening sessions were consistently affected, morning sessions largely not.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Mostly male participants of mixed training status in acute (one to a few nights) laboratory protocols. Generalisation to women, older adults and chronic restriction is limited.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Very high heterogeneity (I2=98%). A systematic review of resistance-exercise studies found total deprivation had little effect on strength while consecutive restricted nights reduced compound-lift strength (next rule), so the task and the pattern of sleep loss matter.
**Sources:** [S576]

### Several short nights: lighten lower-body compound lifts
**Rule:** When a client has had several short nights, expect squats and deadlifts to feel heavier and move slower even if they can still complete the volume. Lower load targets on lower-body compound lifts, use RPE/RIR rather than fixed percentages, and keep accessory work as planned.
**Evidence:** A systematic review of 17 studies (all moderate or weak quality; no meta-analysis) found total sleep deprivation had little effect on strength during resistance exercise, but consecutive nights of restriction could reduce force in multi-joint lifts, not single-joint lifts; motivation aids (group training, caffeine) or training before long periods awake may offset this. In a randomised crossover study of 10 resistance-trained women, 9 nights of 5 h in bed reduced volume load only trivially (less than 1%) but slowed mean concentric velocity of lower-body compound lifts by up to 15% (upper-body lifts unaffected), increased within-set velocity loss in back squats by up to 7%, and raised salivary cortisol (AUC) by 42%, training distress by 84% and session RPE by 11%.
**Grade:** emerging
**Applies to:** Young trained adults; the female data come from one small crossover study (n=10, aged 18-35, eumenorrheic). Applies to repeated restriction rather than one bad night.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Partly conflicts with the Craven 2022 meta-analysis, which found strength reduced overall after acute sleep loss. Studies are small and of weak/moderate quality, and hormonal findings conflicted. Motivation, caffeine and time of day modify the effect.
**Sources:** [S577], [S578]

### Poor sleep blunts the muscle-building response
**Rule:** Tell clients that poor sleep blunts the muscle-building response, as one reason to protect sleep in a muscle-gain or fat-loss phase. During unavoidable short-sleep periods, keep training (exercise appears to protect protein synthesis) rather than skipping it.
**Evidence:** In 24 healthy young men, 5 nights of 4 h in bed lowered myofibrillar protein synthesis vs normal sleep (1.24 vs 1.53 %/day, about 19% lower); 3 sessions of high-intensity interval exercise during the restriction kept it at control levels (1.61 %/day). In 13 young adults (7 men, 6 women), one night of total sleep deprivation reduced post-meal muscle protein synthesis by 18%, raised cortisol by 21% and lowered testosterone by 24%.
**Grade:** emerging
**Applies to:** Healthy young adults in short laboratory studies; the protective exercise was HIIT, not resistance training. Mechanistic markers, not measured muscle mass or strength.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Two small studies; translation to long-term hypertrophy is unproven, and whether resistance training gives the same protection is untested. The Saner study allocated participants to matched groups and its abstract does not state randomisation.
**Sources:** [S579], [S580]

### Extra sleep before a test: low-risk, unproven
**Rule:** You can suggest a bit more sleep (30-60 min) before important sessions or tests as low-risk advice, but do not promise performance gains or sell "sleep banking" as proven.
**Evidence:** A systematic review found only 2 eligible studies of sleep extension in athletes (26-106 min more sleep). Of 15 performance measures, 6 showed large effects and the rest ranged from trivial to medium. Evidence quality ranged from very low to moderate, with high risk of bias in the RCT.
**Grade:** emerging
**Applies to:** Athletes only; no data for recreational lifters.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Very few studies and high risk of bias; magnitude depends on the measure.
**Sources:** [S594]

## Stress, fatigue and load management

### Life stress slows recovery
**Rule:** Ask about life stress (work, family, exams, money) during regular check-ins. In high-stress weeks, hold or reduce volume and intensity, allow more time between hard sessions for the same muscles, and do not increase load. Treat training as stress relief rather than another demand.
**Evidence:** In 135 undergraduates doing 12 weeks of resistance training twice weekly, those reporting fewer stressful life events (median split) gained significantly more in bench press and squat 1RM than high-stress peers; social support was unrelated to gains. In 31 resistance-trained students (apparently one cohort reported in two papers), higher life-event stress and perceived stress were linked to slower recovery of maximal isometric force after a hard leg-press session, both in the first hour and over 96 h. Over 96 h, life-event stress was also linked to worse energy, fatigue and soreness, and perceived stress to worse energy only.
**Grade:** emerging
**Applies to:** University students around 20 years old; observational designs. No systematic review or meta-analysis was found.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Small observational studies from one group, and the two recovery papers appear to share one sample. No effect sizes for strength gains in the abstract. Stress also lowers adherence, which confounds the findings.
**Sources:** [S581], [S582], [S583]

### Overreaching vs overtraining
**Rule:** Treat planned hard weeks (functional overreaching) as normal only if performance and mood rebound within about 2 weeks of reduced load. If performance stays flat or declines for more than about 2 weeks despite lighter training, flag possible non-functional overreaching: cut volume and intensity substantially and recommend a GP visit to rule out illness, anaemia or under-fuelling. Never label someone "overtrained" yourself; it is a diagnosis of exclusion. The 2-week threshold is a pragmatic reading of the consensus, not a validated cut-off.
**Evidence:** The ECSS/ACSM joint consensus (Meeusen 2013) describes a continuum. Functional overreaching is a short-term performance drop after intensified training, restored within several days to several weeks and possibly followed by supercompensation (evidence for which "is not abundant"); recovery within about 2 weeks may be argued to be a relatively normal stage of training. Non-functional overreaching is stagnation or decline lasting several weeks or months with psychological or endocrine disturbance. Overtraining syndrome is a "prolonged maladaptation" in which recovery may take months or possibly years. NFOR and OTS often show the same signs, so OTS can often only be diagnosed in retrospect, by clinical outcome and exclusion of other causes.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Consensus written mostly for endurance athletes. For recreational gym-goers true OTS is rare; FOR and NFOR, often driven by life stress, poor sleep or dieting rather than training volume alone, are the relevant states.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** The consensus says there is no evidence that OTS symptoms are more severe than NFOR symptoms. Bellinger (2020) notes some studies found dampened adaptations in overreached athletes and others substantial supercompensation, so planned overreaching is not clearly necessary.
**Sources:** [S747], [S748]

### Overreaching warning signs and the response
**Rule:** Trigger a deload when 2 or more of these persist for more than 1 week: performance falling at the same RPE, worsening mood or irritability, disturbed sleep, unusual persistent fatigue, or a resting HR clearly above the person's own baseline. The response is the standard reactive deload (one week with volume cut by about 30-50%, loads kept) plus 1-3 extra full rest days (the size and triggers are pragmatic coaching choices, not taken from the sources). Always check calorie intake and recent life stress, especially in people who are dieting. If symptoms persist after 2 weeks of reduced load, refer to a GP.
**Evidence:** No single marker (hormones, performance tests, psychological, biochemical or immune markers) meets all criteria for diagnosing NFOR or OTS; a decline in performance is the only certain sign. In resistance training, a systematic review of 22 studies found no reliable indicator other than a sustained performance decrease. Mood disturbance (POMS) shows a dose-response relationship with training load. Short-term (under 2 weeks) overload raised resting HR by about 4.5 bpm on average, but not in interventions longer than 2 weeks. Recommended responses: adjust daily intensity or volume or allow a full rest day when performance declines or fatigue is excessive, keep at least 1 passive rest day per week, avoid excessive monotony, support energy, carbohydrate, protein and sleep, and treat OTS with rest. In 18 of 21 overload/OTS studies that measured intake (86%), energy or carbohydrate availability decreased.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Mostly trained athletes. In ordinary adults the same signs more often come from life stress, sleep loss or aggressive dieting. Thresholds such as resting HR about 5 bpm above baseline are pragmatic, not validated.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Resting HR and HRV changes are inconsistent. Signs are nonspecific and individual. Stellingwerff (2021) argues many apparent overload or OTS cases may be misdiagnosed under-fuelling (RED-S); Grandou (2020) notes poor methodological standardisation in resistance-training studies.
**Sources:** [S747], [S749], [S750]

### Deloads are optional and reactive
**Rule:** Treat deloads as an optional fatigue-management tool, not a requirement for growth. For beginners and returning lifters on moderate volume, do not schedule fixed deloads. Use a reactive deload of about one week when performance stalls across sessions, joint aches or soreness build up, or life stress spikes: keep the same exercises, frequency and loads, cut volume (sets) by about 30-50%, and stop sets well short of failure. Overreaching signs get the same deload plus 1-3 extra rest days (see Overreaching warning signs). The 30-50% figure is a coaching convention consistent with the survey data below, not a tested dose. Do not promise that a deload will "re-sensitise" muscle or speed up gains.
**Evidence:** In the only RCT found (Coleman 2024; 39 resistance-trained young adults, 29 men and 10 women), a 1-week full training cessation at the midpoint of a 9-week high-volume programme gave the same lower-body hypertrophy, local muscular endurance and power as continuous training; the continuous group gained more isometric and dynamic lower-body strength. An expert Delphi consensus (Bell 2023) defines a deload as "a period of reduced training stress designed to mitigate physiological and psychological fatigue, promote recovery, and enhance preparedness for subsequent training". Competitive strength and physique athletes report deloading about every 5.6 +/- 2.3 weeks for 6.4 +/- 1.7 days, cutting volume and load, raising reps in reserve and keeping frequency and exercise selection the same.
**Grade:** emerging
**Applies to:** Resistance-trained young adults (mostly men) and competitive strength/physique athletes. Frequency and structure come from self-report surveys and expert opinion, and may not fit novices.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** One RCT, which tested full cessation, not a reduced-load deload. The "every 4-6 weeks" practice comes from surveys and consensus. Need depends on volume, proximity to failure, age, sleep and life stress.
**Sources:** [S134], [S136], [S137]

### Short breaks do not erase progress
**Rule:** Reassure clients that a 1-3 week break (holiday, illness, busy period) will not erase their progress. When they return after under about 2 weeks off, resume at about 90% of previous loads; after longer breaks, start 10-20% below previous loads and rebuild over 1-3 weeks (these restart figures are coaching conventions, not tested in the sources below). For older adults, minimise full stops: suggest a short home or bodyweight routine during breaks, because detraining hits them harder. A long break or a pattern of repeated stops should trigger a plan to get back to consistency.
**Evidence:** Stopping resistance training lowers all parts of muscular performance (meta-analysis of 103 studies): pooled SMDs -0.62 for submaximal strength, -0.46 for maximal force and -0.20 for maximal power. Losses grow with break length and are larger in people over 65; inactive people lose more maximal force and power than recreational athletes. In a 24-week RCT in 14 young men doing bench press, three cycles of 6 weeks training plus 3 weeks off produced similar gains in muscle cross-sectional area and 1RM as continuous training.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** The meta-analysis covers mixed ages and training statuses. The periodic-training RCT is small (n=14 young men) and tested only bench press.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Rate of loss depends on age, training status and break length. The single small RCT should not be read as "breaks are as good as training" for trained people or lower-body muscles.
**Sources:** [S064], [S138]

### Tapering only for a max test
**Rule:** A taper only matters if a recreational client wants to test a 1RM or compete. Then cut volume by about 40% in the final week while keeping some heavy singles or doubles, leave about 2-4 days between the last hard session and the test, and do the heaviest lower-body pulls earliest. Present this as reasonable practice, not a proven protocol. General body-composition clients do not need a taper.
**Evidence:** A 2020 review says there is no established evidence-based tapering protocol for maximal strength. A 2026 systematic review of self-reported strategies (7 studies) found weight-lifting-sport athletes taper for about 5-9 days, mostly with step tapers, cutting volume by about 40-45%; they last deadlift about 6-8 days before competition, squat about 5-6.5 days and bench about 3-5 days, with complete cessation lasting 1.5-4 days.
**Grade:** emerging
**Applies to:** Competitive powerlifters, weightlifters, strongman, Highland Games and CrossFit athletes (self-report). Generalising to recreational lifters is an extrapolation.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** The data describe what athletes do, not what works; outcome trials are scarce.
**Sources:** [S595], [S596]

### Simple readiness monitoring
**Rule:** Default monitoring for every client: (1) session-RPE (0-10) times duration, logged weekly (collecting it about 30 min after the session is a common convention not found in these sources); (2) a 4-5 item pre-session wellness check scored 1-5 (sleep quality, fatigue, soreness, stress, mood). Compare each person against their own rolling baseline. A drop on 2 or more items for 3 or more days, or a large weekly load jump, means hold or reduce load. These thresholds are pragmatic, not validated.
**Evidence:** A systematic review of 56 studies found subjective measures (mood, perceived stress, wellness) reflected acute and chronic training load with better sensitivity and consistency than objective measures (blood markers, HR), and the two generally did not correlate; wellness worsened with acute load increases and chronic training and improved with acute decreases. A review of session-RPE found 36 validation studies supporting its validity and reliability across sports, ages and levels. Meeusen 2013 describes session-RPE, weekly load, monotony and strain, reports that spikes in monotony and strain corresponded with later illness or injury, and recommends regular psychological questionnaires.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Athletes of many sports, ages and levels; plausible but unproven in recreational gym-goers. Depends on honest reporting.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Most sRPE validation is in team and endurance sports, not hypertrophy training. Within-person trends matter more than absolute thresholds. Response distortion ("faking good or bad") is a limitation.
**Sources:** [S751], [S752], [S747]

### RIR/RPE accuracy
**Rule:** Use RIR or RPE to regulate effort, but expect people to be off by about 1 rep or more. Use RIR targets mainly on sets of 12 reps or fewer. Prescribe beginners about 2-3 RIR so a 1-2 rep misjudgement still leaves a margin before failure, and calibrate occasionally with a safe set to failure on a machine.
**Evidence:** In an exploratory meta-analysis of 12 studies (414 participants), people underestimated reps to failure by an average of 0.95 reps (95% CI 0.17-1.73; I2 97.9%). Accuracy was better on sets of 12 reps or fewer (beta=0.06 vs 0.47 above 12 reps), so with heavier loads. Accuracy improved trivially in later sets and slightly, but not clearly, closer to failure. Training status and upper vs lower body did not influence accuracy.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Healthy trained and untrained adults; few data on older adults.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Extreme heterogeneity. Between-participant SD was about 1.45 reps. The authors say whether this inaccuracy is "acceptable" is unresolved, and definitions of failure varied.
**Sources:** [S031]

### HRV is optional
**Rule:** Do not require HRV. If a client already tracks nocturnal HRV or resting HR with a wearable, use only their own 7-day rolling trend, never single readings or comparisons with others. Treat a sustained drop below their normal range together with poor subjective wellness as a reason to keep the session easy, not as a diagnosis. Subjective wellness and performance take priority.
**Evidence:** HRV-guided endurance training gave at most small, non-significant gains vs predefined training (Duking 2021: 8 studies, 198 participants; performance g=0.079; VO2peak g=0.171), with fewer non-responders. Manresa-Rocamora 2021 found small non-significant advantages (SMD 0.20 for VO2max and performance) and unresolved questions about index, posture and baseline. Consumer wearable accuracy varies: in 13 adults over 536 nights, nocturnal HRV agreement with ECG ranged from CCC 0.99 (Oura Gen 4) to 0.82 (Polar, error about 16%).
**Grade:** emerging
**Applies to:** Recreationally trained to trained endurance exercisers; essentially no evidence for HRV-guided resistance training or sedentary beginners. Device validation is one small study.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Meeusen 2013 reports few, inconsistent HRV studies in overreached athletes. HRV is highly individual and affected by alcohol, illness, stress and posture; device firmware changes over time.
**Sources:** [S753], [S754], [S755]

## Soreness and recovery modalities

### DOMS and the repeated-bout effect
**Rule:** Warn every new or returning client that soreness peaking 1-3 days after the first sessions is expected and fades with repetition. Keep the first 1-2 weeks low in volume and eccentric load so the repeated-bout effect builds before load increases. After a layoff of more than about 6-9 months, treat the client as a beginner again for soreness purposes. Soreness that keeps getting worse, or lasts more than 5-7 days, is not normal DOMS (see Rhabdomyolysis).
**Evidence:** DOMS usually appears within 24 h and peaks around 72 h after strenuous, prolonged, unfamiliar or eccentric-heavy exercise, and lasts a few days. One eccentric bout protects against damage from a later bout. In one small study (35 male students, elbow flexors), protection on most measures lasted at least 6 months, faster strength recovery was still present at 9 months, and the effect was absent at 12 months.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Healthy adults. The time course comes from a military clinical practice guideline; the repeated-bout duration from young men doing elbow-flexor eccentrics (n=10-14 per group).
**Individual variation / conflicts:** The duration rests on one small study without a control group. DOMS magnitude varies widely and is a poor marker of training quality or muscle growth.
**Sources:** [S761], [S762], [S763]

### Training while sore is not harmful
**Rule:** Moderate DOMS is not a reason to skip a session. Train the sore area at reduced load or volume, or train other muscle groups, with a thorough warm-up; expect some temporary strength loss. Do not program maximal or test efforts on very sore muscles. Light movement can be offered as short-term pain relief, not as faster recovery.
**Evidence:** Repeating a moderate eccentric elbow-flexor bout (3x10 at 50% of maximal isometric force) 2 and 4 days after the first, while muscles were still weakened, did not worsen damage or slow recovery. Light concentric exercise of sore muscles (n=14) reduced soreness and tenderness by about 40% immediately, with a temporary drop in strength of about 15%, but neither sped up nor slowed recovery.
**Grade:** emerging
**Applies to:** Healthy young adults in small within-subject elbow-flexor models. Does not apply when rhabdomyolysis warning signs are present.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** No meta-analysis; repeat bouts were submaximal. Session quality on sore muscles is reduced even if damage is not worsened.
**Sources:** [S764], [S765]

### Recovery modalities are optional comfort tools
**Rule:** If a client wants a recovery modality, the best-supported choices are massage (including self-massage) and compression garments, with foam rolling as a cheap low-yield option, ideally before training. Do not recommend stretching for DOMS. Put sleep, nutrition and sensible load progression ahead of any gadget.
**Evidence:** A meta-analysis of 99 studies (Dupuy 2018) found massage, active recovery, compression garments, water immersion, contrast water therapy and cryotherapy reduced DOMS (g from -2.26 to -0.40), with massage most effective for DOMS and perceived fatigue; stretching and electrostimulation showed no change. Post-exercise foam rolling only slightly reduced muscle pain (+6%, g=0.47) and strength loss (g=0.21), effects "rather minor and partly negligible". A 2025 umbrella review (29 systematic reviews, 863 RCTs) found effects on pain for massage, cooling, contrast therapy, compression and others, mostly from low-quality reviews (17 critically low), and weak evidence for stretching, exercise and electrical stimulation.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Mostly young, active adults; outcomes are short-term soreness and perceived recovery.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Effect sizes vary widely and quality is low. Cold modalities that reduce soreness may impair hypertrophy if used routinely after lifting (next rule). Responses are partly expectation-driven.
**Sources:** [S766], [S767], [S768]

### No cold water immersion right after lifting for muscle gain
**Rule:** For clients whose goal is muscle gain, advise against ice baths or cold water immersion in the hours after resistance sessions. If they enjoy cold exposure, schedule it on non-lifting days or well apart from lifting, and reserve post-session cold for short competition-like periods where next-day soreness matters more than adaptation.
**Evidence:** In a 12-week RCT, 21 active men who did 10 min of CWI after each session (2 per week) gained less strength and muscle mass than men doing active recovery; type II fibre area rose 17% with active recovery but did not increase with CWI, and acute anabolic signalling and satellite-cell responses were blunted. A Bayesian meta-analysis of 8 studies (ages 20-26, 7 of 8 men only, mostly 10 C for 10-20 min) found an effect of -0.22 (95% credible interval -0.47 to 0.04), a 95.7% probability of less hypertrophy; study quality was fair to poor.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Young adults, mostly men, trained and untrained. Delayed or occasional CWI, older adults and women are not covered.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Trade-off with recovery evidence (cold reduces DOMS). The credible interval includes zero.
**Sources:** [S769], [S770]

### NSAIDs are not for training soreness
**Rule:** Do not suggest NSAIDs to manage normal training soreness. Never suggest taking them before training or "to train through" pain, especially in heat, with dehydration, or in novices starting hard programmes. Medication decisions belong to the client's doctor or pharmacist; the coach only flags the issue.
**Evidence:** A meta-analysis found NSAIDs gave no benefit on performance or strength at 0 or 24 h after resistance exercise, a possibly worse outcome at 48 h (performance ES -0.42, 95% CI -0.71 to -0.12; strength ES -0.50, 95% CI -0.83 to -0.16, although reported p-values of 0.132 and 0.072 make this uncertain), and no reduction in CK. In 18-35-year-olds, 1200 mg/day ibuprofen for 8 weeks roughly halved quadriceps growth (3.7% vs 7.5% with 75 mg aspirin, P=0.029); in 64-67-year-olds, ibuprofen (1.2 g/day) or acetaminophen (4 g/day) increased hypertrophy over 12 weeks (10.9-12.5% vs 8.6% placebo). The CHAMP exertional rhabdomyolysis guideline (2025) lists NSAIDs among drugs that raise rhabdomyolysis risk, says muscle pain "should not be masked", and warns excessive NSAID and/or acetaminophen doses can cause kidney or liver toxicity, possibly more so after muscle breakdown.
**Grade:** emerging
**Applies to:** Young adults (Lilja), adults aged about 64-67 (Trappe), pooled acute trials (de Oliveira); doses at or near the over-the-counter maximum taken daily. CHAMP is military expert consensus.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Direct conflict by age: high-dose ibuprofen impaired hypertrophy in young adults but enhanced it in older adults. Effects of lower or occasional doses are unclear, and the 48 h harm is statistically ambiguous.
**Sources:** [S771], [S772], [S773], [S761]

## Warm-up, stretching and mobility

### Brief warm-up before every session
**Rule:** Start every session with a brief general warm-up (light cardio and dynamic movement for the joints being trained), then progressively heavier warm-up sets of the first main lift before the working sets. Keep it short so it does not eat into training time or adherence. Specific durations and ramp-set schemes are practical convention, not evidence-derived.
**Evidence:** A systematic review with meta-analysis of 32 high-quality studies found warm-ups that include activities other than stretching improved performance in 79% of the criteria examined, with little evidence of harm. Dynamic stretching done within minutes of activity gives small-to-moderate performance improvements (Behm 2016).
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Healthy, active individuals across many performance tasks; few studies tested resistance-training outcomes.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Few well-conducted RCTs. Optimal type and duration are not established, and evidence that warm-ups prevent injuries in gym settings is lacking.
**Sources:** [S602], [S601]

### Static stretching before lifting: keep it short
**Rule:** Static stretching before lifting is fine if the client likes it. Keep it under about 30-45 s per muscle group, then do dynamic warm-up and warm-up sets. Avoid long static holds (60 s or more per muscle) right before a strength test or heavy top set. Do not use static stretching as the whole warm-up.
**Evidence:** In a meta-analysis of 104 studies (Simic 2013), pre-exercise static stretching reduced strength by 5.4% (SMD -0.10, 95% CI -0.15 to -0.04), power by 1.9% (SMD -0.04, CI crossing zero) and explosive performance by 2.0% (SMD -0.03, CI -0.07 to 0.01); losses were larger in isometric tests and smallest with total stretch durations of 45 s or less. Behm 2016 found -1.1% for under 60 s per muscle group vs -4.6% for 60 s or more, and no clear effect when dynamic activity followed. Stretch-induced ROM gains typically last under 30 minutes.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Healthy active adults, mostly young; effects were unrelated to age, sex or fitness in Simic. Many tests were isometric or jumps, usually 3-5 min after stretching.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Claims that "static stretching ruins your lifting" overstate the evidence: effects are small, mostly with long holds, and largely disappear once dynamic activity follows. Behm notes a small (+2.2%) benefit at long muscle lengths.
**Sources:** [S603], [S601]

### Lifting through full ROM is mobility work
**Rule:** Have clients lift through the fullest ROM they can control with external load; for most of them that is their mobility work. Add dedicated stretching only for a specific ROM goal or limitation (a deep squat they cannot reach, an overhead position for a sport), or because they enjoy it. Do not make separate mobility routines compulsory for general clients.
**Evidence:** In Alizadeh 2023 (55 studies), resistance training increased ROM (ES 0.73) with no difference vs stretch training (ES 0.08, p=0.79); adding stretching gave nothing extra (ES -0.001). Gains were larger in untrained/sedentary people (ES 1.04) than trained/active ones (ES 0.43), and bodyweight-only training did not significantly improve ROM. Afonso 2021 (11 RCTs, n=452) also found no difference between strength training and stretching for ROM (ES -0.22, 95% CI -0.55 to 0.12).
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Healthy participants (no effect of age, duration, frequency, sex or contraction type in Alizadeh); Afonso covered RCTs in humans of any condition. Not specifically studied in clinical joint pathology.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Two meta-analyses with highly heterogeneous studies. Dancers or gymnasts needing extreme ROM may still need dedicated stretching.
**Sources:** [S604], [S605]

### Stretching does not meaningfully build muscle
**Rule:** Do not sell stretching as a way to build muscle for general clients; loaded resistance training is far more effective and time-efficient. If a client cannot or will not lift a body part, long-duration daily stretching may add a little, but treat that as experimental.
**Evidence:** Arntz 2024 (25 studies) found an unclear, trivial effect of chronic static stretching on hypertrophy (SMD 0.118, 95% PI -0.233 to 0.469), larger in trained people and with longer durations, with a moderate ROM effect. Warneke 2024 (42 studies, 1,318 participants) found small significant effects on hypertrophy (d=0.20) and maximal strength (d=0.30), but only at higher doses (longer durations, frequencies and programme lengths).
**Grade:** emerging
**Applies to:** Healthy individuals of mixed training status. The effective dose is often impractical.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** The two 2024 meta-analyses disagree on whether the effect is reliable; both suggest a dose-response.
**Sources:** [S606], [S607]

### Foam rolling before training: optional
**Rule:** Foam rolling the muscles about to be trained is an acceptable optional part of a warm-up if the client likes it: it gives an immediate gain in range of motion similar to stretching and does not meaningfully reduce strength or jump performance. Do not present it as required or as a performance booster, since other warm-up activities give similar range-of-motion gains. Keep it brief (a coaching convention) and follow it with the usual warm-up sets.
**Evidence:** In 26 trials of healthy adults, foam rolling had a large effect on range of motion compared with no exercise (SMD 0.74) and was not better than stretching (SMD -0.02). Across 14 pre-rolling studies, it gave small gains in sprint (+0.7%) and flexibility (+4.0%) and negligible changes in jump (-1.9%) and strength (+1.8%). In 38 studies (1134 participants), stretching or foam rolling did not improve range of motion (ES 0.01) or stiffness more than other warm-up activities. In 20 studies, foam rolling had no acute effect on isometric strength, eccentric torque or rate of force development.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Healthy, mostly young and active adults, some athletes; few studies in older or untrained adults. Effects are immediate, after a single bout.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** The range-of-motion effect may be smaller in men. Certainty is low to moderate and study quality fair. How long the gain lasts is not quantified, and the best rolling duration is unknown.
**Sources:** [S826], [S767], [S827], [S828]

## Injury risk, pain and rehabilitation

### Gym strength training is relatively safe
**Rule:** Tell anxious beginners and older adults that gym-based strength training is among the safer forms of exercise. Spend safety effort on gradual load progression and recovery, because the most common problems are strains, sprains and tendon pain. Watch shoulders, lower back, knees and elbows. Progress sport-style formats (CrossFit, strongman, Olympic lifts) more conservatively.
**Evidence:** Weight-training sports have relatively low injury rates compared with common team sports: 0.24-1 injuries per 1000 h in bodybuilding, 1.0-4.4 in powerlifting, 2.4-3.3 in weightlifting, 4.5-6.1 in strongman and 7.5 in Highland Games. A systematic review of resistance-training modes (0.21-18.9 per 1000 h across 28 studies, mostly CrossFit/HIFT) concluded that traditional strength training is the safest form and strongman the least safe, though few studies covered either. The most commonly injured sites are shoulder, lower back, knee, elbow and wrist/hand; the commonest types are strains, tendinitis and sprains.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Mostly competitive athletes in weight-training sports, from mostly retrospective studies with inconsistent injury definitions. Rates in recreational gym-goers are poorly measured.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Only 4 of 20 studies were prospective in Keogh 2017; Serafim 2023 had only 3 studies on traditional strength training and 1 on strongman. Prevalence can look high (70% point prevalence in powerlifting) when "injury" includes any pain affecting training. In female weightlifters and powerlifters, pelvic floor dysfunction (for example urinary incontinence) was reported in about 50%, vs 9.3% in males.
**Sources:** [S597], [S598], [S599]

### Strength training prevents injuries; stretching does not
**Rule:** Present strength training as the main tool for injury resilience in clients who also play sports or run. Do not prescribe pre-exercise stretching to prevent injury. If a client likes stretching, keep it for ROM or enjoyment, not as protection.
**Evidence:** In a meta-analysis of 25 RCTs (26,610 participants, 3,464 injuries), strength training cut sports injuries to less than a third (RR 0.315, 95% CI 0.207-0.480); proprioception training (RR 0.550) and multi-component programmes (RR 0.655) also helped, and stretching showed no benefit (RR 0.963, 0.846-1.095). A separate systematic review found static and PNF stretching had no clear effect on all-cause or overuse injuries.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Sports participants in injury-prevention RCTs; extrapolating to general gym-goers is reasonable but untested.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Behm 2016 recommends stretching inside a warm-up with dynamic activity to reduce muscle injuries specifically, but its overall injury data show no clear effect. The Lauersen estimate was heterogeneous and the strength-training estimate comes from few trials.
**Sources:** [S600], [S601]

### Tendinopathy: load it, do not rest it
**Rule:** If a client reports localised, load-related tendon pain (Achilles or patellar), suggest assessment by a physio or doctor to confirm the diagnosis. Meanwhile do not prescribe total rest: swap or reduce the provoking exercises and add progressive tendon loading (heavy slow calf raises or eccentrics for the Achilles; heavy slow resistance such as leg press or squat-type work for the patellar tendon) at least 2-3 times per week, progressed over 12 weeks or more. Choose among heavy-loading options by tolerance and adherence; avoid concentric-only programmes as the main approach.
**Evidence:** Progressive loading is the first-line treatment for mid-portion Achilles and patellar tendinopathy, with no single type clearly superior. For Achilles (22 studies, 1,137 participants) there was moderate evidence that eccentric exercise beats control and concentric exercise for pain, with no difference between eccentric and heavy slow resistance (HSR); the authors conditionally recommend exercise without favouring a programme. A living network meta-analysis of 29 RCTs found every active treatment seemed superior to wait-and-see at 3 months (very low to low certainty), with no clinically relevant differences between them, and suggests starting with calf exercise. For patellar tendinopathy, a 2026 network meta-analysis (10 RCTs, n=313) found no exercise intervention superior to HSR; concentric-only, flywheel and eccentric step training were estimated to do worse.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Adults with mid-portion Achilles or patellar tendinopathy, often active. Insertional Achilles and other tendinopathies (for example elbow) are not covered. Achilles trials are mostly high risk of bias.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Certainty low to very low (76% of trials at high risk of bias in van der Vlist); the patellar network is sparse. Frequency and duration are typical of trials, not tested optima. Some cases need medical management.
**Sources:** [S608], [S609], [S610]

### Some pain during exercise is acceptable in chronic, assessed pain
**Rule:** With chronic, stable musculoskeletal pain (more than 3 months, no red flags, already assessed), the goal is not zero pain. Allow mild, tolerable pain during exercise as long as it settles fairly quickly and does not progressively worsen across sessions. If pain flares and stays elevated, reduce load or volume; keep clients active in modified form rather than stopping. Pain-free and painful approaches give similar long-term results, so let the client choose what they will stick to. For acute injuries, sharp or worsening pain, or neurological symptoms, stop and refer.
**Evidence:** A meta-analysis (9 papers from 7 RCTs, n=385, chronic musculoskeletal pain) found protocols allowing or encouraging pain gave a small short-term (3 months or less) pain benefit over pain-free exercise (ES -0.27, 95% CI -0.54 to -0.05; moderate-quality evidence), with no difference in medium- or long-term pain or in function at any point; pain "need not be a barrier to successful outcomes". In an RCT in Achilles tendinopathy (n=38), continuing running and jumping under a pain-monitoring model gave the same improvement as 6 weeks of active rest (VISA-A 57 to 85 vs 57 to 91 at 12 months), with no negative effects.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Adults (mean ages roughly 40-55) with chronic shoulder pain, Achilles tendinopathy, plantar fasciitis or chronic low back pain, already diagnosed. Not acute trauma; knee conditions not represented.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** The benefit is small and short-term, from few small trials. Commonly quoted thresholds were not confirmed in the meta-analysis (see the pain-monitoring model below). Pain tolerance and fear of movement vary widely.
**Sources:** [S611], [S612]

### Pain-monitoring traffic light for tendon problems
**Rule:** For an assessed tendon problem (no rupture), keep training and use a traffic light. Green: pain 0-2/10. Amber: 3-5/10 during or right after the session, acceptable if back to baseline by the next morning. Red: more than 5/10, pain still raised the next morning, or pain or stiffness worsening week to week. On red, cut load or volume for that movement at the next session (do not stop all training) and re-check. If it stays red for 2 or more weeks, refer to a physio.
**Evidence:** In the Silbernagel/Thomee model, loading (including running/jumping) may continue if pain during or right after activity does not exceed 5/10, returns to pre-activity level by the next morning, and symptoms do not worsen over time. In an RCT of 38 patients with Achilles tendinopathy, loading under this model gave outcomes no different from 6 weeks of active rest (VISA-A-S 57 to 85 vs 57 to 91 at 12 months). A pilot RCT in patellar tendinopathy (n=15, ages 16-40) found the model feasible.
**Grade:** emerging
**Applies to:** Adults with Achilles or patellar tendinopathy, mostly in running and jumping sports. Not tested in rupture, acute strains or post-surgery. The green/amber split and the 2-week referral point are coaching conventions.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** The 5/10 ceiling and next-morning rule are pragmatic conventions from small trials. Silbernagel's 2020 review classifies "high" activities as 4-5/10 during and 5-6/10 after, so the ceiling is applied flexibly. A 2025 scoping review traces the zone scheme (under 2 safe, 2-5 acceptable, over 5 high risk, settling by the next morning) to Thomee's system for patellofemoral pain, notes the limits were chosen from clinical experience, and found no study comparing them with other limits. Record trends, not single readings.
**Sources:** [S612], [S681], [S682], [S812]

### Recovery days for painful tendon-loading activities
**Rule:** Schedule painful tendon-loading sessions (jumps, sprints, heavy calf or quad loading) by pain category: at most daily for light, every third day for medium, every fourth day for high. Fill the gaps with non-provocative training. Do not reintroduce running until walking pain is 2/10 or less and 20 hops cause 2/10 or less. Re-grade every 3-4 weeks.
**Evidence:** Silbernagel's clinical review classifies activities as light (1-2/10 during and after, daily), medium (2-3/10 during, 3-4/10 after, 2 recovery days) and high (4-5/10 during, 5-6/10 after, 3 recovery days), reclassified every 3-4 weeks. Before running, walking pain should be minimal (1-2/10), and someone with pain above 2/10 on 20 hops will struggle to start running within the guidelines.
**Grade:** emerging
**Applies to:** Adults with midportion Achilles tendinopathy in running and jumping sports; extrapolation to patellar tendinopathy is untested.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Expert clinical framework without a recommendation grade. The "high" category tolerates 5-6/10 after activity, slightly above the 5/10 ceiling often quoted.
**Sources:** [S682], [S683]

### Muscle strain: early pain-guided loading
**Rule:** After a mild-moderate muscle strain with no red flags, start gentle pain-guided loading within the first days (isometrics, then light ranges) rather than resting for weeks. Allow mild discomfort that settles quickly and progress when the previous step is tolerated. Return to heavy or explosive lifting of that muscle only when palpation, full-range strength and functional or sprint testing are pain-free and strength is roughly symmetrical. A severe strain (big bruise, palpable gap, unable to walk normally) needs medical assessment first.
**Evidence:** In 43 men with acute hamstring strain, pain-threshold rehab vs pain-free rehab did not change time to return-to-play clearance (median 17 vs 15 days, P=0.37); the pain-threshold group recovered 15% more isometric knee-flexor strength (95% CI 1-28%) and kept more biceps femoris fascicle length (+0.91 cm at 2 months), with 2 reinjuries per group at 6 months. In an RCT of 50 recreational athletes with severe thigh or calf strains, early rehab onset accelerated recovery vs delayed onset, with similar structural and strength recovery. A Delphi consensus on hamstring return to play lists absence of pain on palpation, strength and flexibility testing, and functional testing as criteria.
**Grade:** emerging
**Applies to:** Men with acute hamstring strain, recreational athletes with severe thigh or calf strains, football experts (Delphi). Generalisation to older adults or women in a gym is untested.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Return-to-play criteria still favour pain-free tests while one RCT supports pain-threshold rehab during loading. Trials are small and reinjury data underpowered.
**Sources:** [S684], [S685], [S686]

### Acute sprains and strains: protect briefly, then load
**Rule:** For a sprain or strain with no red flags: protect it for 1-3 days (reduce load, no long immobilisation), compress and elevate, and explain the expected recovery. Then reintroduce pain-guided loading and keep cardio going with other body parts. Do not forbid ice if it relieves pain, but do not present it as a healing aid. Prioritise progressive exercise over passive treatments.
**Evidence:** PEACE & LOVE replaces RICE with early protection (relative rest for about 1-3 days) followed by progressive loading; its advice to avoid anti-inflammatory modalities, including ice, is "largely based on theoretical reasoning". Some trials show short-term pain and swelling reductions with ice, but systematic reviews find limited or inconsistent evidence of functional benefit. The evidence-based ankle sprain guideline says a short immobilisation may relieve pain and swelling, but patients benefit most from tape or brace plus an exercise programme, and prefers supervised exercise over passive modalities.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Adults with acute ligament sprains and muscle strains, mostly from ankle sprain and active-adult populations.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** PEACE & LOVE is an expert editorial (Dubois & Esculier 2020), not a tested protocol. The "avoid ice" element is contested; the first sham-controlled RCT of ice within PEACE & LOVE had its protocol published in 2026, with no results yet.
**Sources:** [S698], [S704]

### Abdominal wall strain: symptom-guided return
**Rule:** An abdominal wall strain affects the rectus abdominis or obliques and comes from a direct blow or a sudden or repetitive twist, bend or extension of the trunk. It is usually managed without surgery, with symptoms guiding the return to training; apply "Muscle strain: early pain-guided loading" to it (an extrapolation from thigh data). Refer for medical assessment when abdominal pain follows a direct blow and is not settling, especially in a client on anticoagulants: the rare rectus sheath hematoma can need more than symptom-guided care. Also refer for groin and lower abdominal pain that is reproduced by a resisted sit-up or a forced cough or sneeze, or that keeps stopping training despite rest and rehab: that can be athletic pubalgia (sports hernia) rather than a simple strain. The evidence is thin: narrative reviews only.
**Evidence:** Johnson 2006: the abdominal wall muscles (rectus abdominis, external and internal obliques, transverse abdominis) are injured by direct blows or by sudden or repetitive trunk rotation or flexion/extension; apart from the rare rectus sheath hematoma that does not self-tamponade, treatment is nonoperative, with symptoms guiding rehabilitation and return to play. Liao 2021: rectus sheath hematoma is mostly traumatic or spontaneous, causes nonspecific symptoms such as abdominal pain, is often unrecognised and underdiagnosed, and its risk factors include anticoagulation and paroxysmal coughing. Drager 2020: athletic pubalgia causes groin and lower abdominal pain without a true hernia; a resisted sit-up or a forced cough or sneeze can reproduce the symptoms; first-line care is rest, anti-inflammatories and focused physical therapy, with surgery if conservative care fails.
**Grade:** emerging
**Applies to:** Athletes (narrative reviews); Liao covers rectus sheath hematoma in general patients. There are no trials in recreational lifters.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** No RCT, meta-analysis or consensus statement on abdominal wall strain rehab or return timelines was found, and no source gave imaging criteria. The loading steps are extrapolated from the hamstring and thigh data behind "Muscle strain: early pain-guided loading". A simple rectus strain may also hurt on a resisted sit-up (reasoning, not a source finding), so the pubalgia trigger relies mainly on groin location and persistence.
**Sources:** [S857], [S858], [S859]

### Balance work for ankle sprain prevention
**Rule:** For clients who play pivoting or jumping sports, and especially those who have had an ankle sprain before, add balance or proprioceptive drills (for example progressive single-leg balance work) alongside strength work, as part of a multi-component warm-up or programme. Present it as lowering ankle sprain risk, not as a replacement for strength training.
**Evidence:** Hubscher 2010 (7 well-conducted studies): multi-intervention training reduced lower limb injuries (RR 0.61, 95% CI 0.49-0.77), acute knee injuries (RR 0.46, 0.28-0.76) and ankle sprains (RR 0.50, 0.31-0.79); balance training alone reduced ankle sprains (RR 0.64, 0.46-0.9) but not injuries overall (RR 0.49, 0.13-1.8, P = 0.28); exercise worked better in athletes with a history of sports injury. Schiftan 2015 (7 RCTs, 3726 participants): proprioceptive training reduced ankle sprains (RR 0.65, 95% CI 0.55-0.77), including in people with a previous sprain (RR 0.64, 0.51-0.81). Wang 2023 (17 studies, general population): proprioceptive training reduced lateral ankle sprains (RR 0.59), more so after a previous sprain (RR 0.49). Lauersen 2014 (25 RCTs, 26 610 participants): proprioception training RR 0.550 (0.347-0.869) and strength training RR 0.315 (0.207-0.480) for sports injuries.
**Grade:** strong
**Applies to:** Mainly adolescent and adult athletes in sport, often pivoting sports; Wang 2023 covered the general population. The benefit is clearest in people with a previous ankle sprain and matters little for gym-only clients who do no sport.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Primary prevention in people with no previous sprain is inconclusive: Schiftan's pooled RR 0.57 came from two non-significant trials. Lauersen found strength training more protective than proprioception, so strength remains the main tool (see "Strength training prevents injuries; stretching does not"). The meta-analyses probably share many trials.
**Sources:** [S846], [S847], [S848], [S600]

### NSAIDs after acute injury: no clear advantage
**Rule:** Do not prescribe or recommend specific medications. If a client asks, say the evidence suggests NSAIDs offer no clear pain advantage over paracetamol for sprains and strains, that topical gels have the best benefit-to-harm profile, and that they should check with a pharmacist or doctor, especially if over 65, with stomach, kidney or heart problems, or on other medicines. Never use painkillers to train through a red-flag or sharp injury pain.
**Evidence:** A Cochrane review (20 RCTs, 3,305 participants, mostly young adults) found oral NSAIDs gave no better pain relief than paracetamol at 1-2 hours or days 1-3 (high certainty) and may slightly increase gastrointestinal adverse events (low certainty); findings should not be extrapolated to adults over 65. A network meta-analysis (207 RCTs, 32,959 participants) found topical NSAIDs had the best benefit-harm ratio, followed by oral NSAIDs and paracetamol, with modest effects (about 1 cm on a 10-cm scale); opioids caused the most harm. The ankle sprain guideline cautions that NSAIDs "may suppress the natural healing process".
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Adults with acute sprains, strains and contusions, mostly young (about 60% male in Cochrane); thin evidence over 65.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** The healing-impairment caution has no quantified human outcome data in the guideline, while the NMA ranks NSAIDs as the best analgesics. Individual GI, renal and cardiovascular risk dominate.
**Sources:** [S705], [S706], [S698]

## Back pain

### Chronic non-specific low back pain: keep training
**Rule:** For a client with chronic non-specific back pain who has been medically cleared, keep them training: choose movements and loads they tolerate (including deadlifts and squats if tolerable), progress gradually and reassure them. Avoid prescribing rest. No single exercise type needs to be favoured, so use what they enjoy. Do not recommend lumbar braces or supports as a back-pain treatment. Refer if pain is new and severe, follows trauma or comes with red-flag signs.
**Evidence:** The WHO 2023 guideline on chronic primary low back pain (pain for more than 3 months) says structured exercise may be offered to adults, including older people (low certainty), and advises against traction, lumbar braces, belts or supports (very low certainty) and against opioids in routine care. In the Cochrane review (249 trials), exercise reduced pain vs no treatment, usual care or placebo by -15.2 on a 0-100 scale (moderate certainty, clinically important); the effect on function (-6.8) was below the threshold for clinical importance. Guidelines summarised in the Lancet series recommend education, staying active and exercise first, and warn against overusing rest and imaging.
**Grade:** strong
**Applies to:** Adults with chronic primary (non-specific) LBP; Cochrane participants had a mean age of 43.7 and were 59% female. Not specific or acute LBP.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Effects are modest; exercise was not better than manual therapy (MD 1.0). WHO's recommendation is conditional. The WHO advice against braces concerns treatment, not lifting belts used for performance.
**Sources:** [S613], [S614], [S615]

### Back pain without red flags: stay active, no routine scans
**Rule:** For back pain without red flags, keep the client training: modify load and range rather than resting, reassure them and do not suggest scans. Refer to a GP or physio if pain is not improving after about 4 weeks, if leg symptoms suggest radiculopathy, or if new or changed symptoms appear.
**Evidence:** NICE NG59 says to consider alternative diagnoses (cancer, infection, trauma, inflammatory disease such as spondyloarthritis), advises against routine imaging in non-specialist settings, recommends continuing normal activities and supports exercise programmes; manual therapy only as part of a package with exercise (amended 2026). An overview of 15 international guidelines agrees: reassurance, return to normal activities, no bed rest, imaging only if serious pathology is suspected, and referral if no improvement after 4 weeks or suspected radiculopathy.
**Grade:** strong
**Applies to:** Adults aged 16 and over with low back pain with or without sciatica.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Guidelines differ on medication. Exercise type should follow preference.
**Sources:** [S695], [S696]

### Do not teach fear of a rounded back
**Rule:** Teach controlled, consistent technique and appropriate loads, but do not tell clients that a rounded back will injure them or that one "bad" rep has damaged their spine; that messaging is not supported and may feed guarding in people with back pain. Hold back on near-maximal loads with a lot of lumbar flexion until the client has tolerated a gradual build-up.
**Evidence:** Low-quality evidence suggests greater lumbar flexion during lifting is not a risk factor for onset or persistence of low back pain and does not distinguish people with and without LBP. In a meta-analysis of 4 studies using intralumbar angles, peak flexion did not differ (1.5 degrees, 95% CI -0.7 to 3.7 in the longitudinal study); studies using thoracopelvic angles found people with LBP lifted with about 6 degrees less flexion. In 9 of 11 studies there was no between-group difference.
**Grade:** emerging
**Applies to:** Lifting tasks in general and occupational populations, not heavy barbell lifts near maximal load.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Few, mostly cross-sectional, low-quality studies that do not address very heavy or fatiguing loaded flexion. Load tolerance varies.
**Sources:** [S616]

### Cauda equina red flags: emergency
**Rule:** Stop and send the person to emergency care the same day, with no training, if back or leg pain comes with any of: numbness in the saddle, genital or anal area; new difficulty passing urine or loss of bladder control; loss of bowel control; new sexual dysfunction; or numbness or weakness in both legs or getting worse. Never wait to see whether it settles.
**Evidence:** In a systematic review (7 studies, n=569, MRI reference), the classic cauda equina red flags (saddle anaesthesia, urinary retention or incontinence, bowel incontinence, reduced anal tone) had low pooled sensitivity (0.19-0.43) but higher specificity (0.62-0.88); when present they justify prompt workup, and their absence does not rule the syndrome out.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** All adults with back pain, with or without sciatica.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Low sensitivity means early cauda equina syndrome can occur without classic signs, so any new neurological change needs urgent review. The IFOMPT framework notes most red flags lack high-quality accuracy data.
**Sources:** [S687], [S688]

### Back pain plus a history of cancer
**Rule:** Ask every client with back pain about previous cancer. Back pain plus a history of cancer means stop loading the spine and get a medical review promptly (within days, sooner with neurological signs). For other isolated features, such as age over 50 or pain lasting more than a month, do not alarm the client: refer to a GP if several features cluster (for example unexplained weight loss plus feeling unwell plus constant non-mechanical pain) or if pain is progressively worsening.
**Evidence:** Spinal malignancy is rare in primary-care low back pain (0-0.7%). Across Cochrane and BMJ systematic reviews, a previous history of cancer is the most informative red flag (post-test probability about 33%, 95% CI 22-46%). Most other red flags, including age over 50, insidious onset and failure to improve after 1 month, have high false-positive rates, and malignancy should not be suspected on one single red flag.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Adults with low back pain, mainly in primary care; prevalence is higher in emergency settings.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Guidelines still list many red flags with little accuracy evidence; combinations are barely studied and no review could pool data.
**Sources:** [S689], [S690], [S691]

### Back pain after trauma in older adults: suspect fracture
**Rule:** New back pain after a fall or other significant trauma, or after minor trauma in an older client (especially a woman over 70, or anyone on long-term oral corticosteroids or with known osteoporosis), needs medical assessment before any loaded spinal exercise.
**Evidence:** For vertebral fracture in low back pain, the informative red flags in primary care are significant trauma (+LR 1.93-12.85), age over 70 (+LR 11.19) and corticosteroid use (imprecise, +LR up to 48.5). Older age plus female sex gave +LR 16.17, and contusion or abrasion was informative in tertiary care (+LR 31.09). In the BMJ review, the probability of fracture reached 90% (95% CI 34-99%) with multiple red flags.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Adults with low back pain; risk mainly in older adults, especially women, and long-term steroid users.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Mostly single studies with wide CIs and no meta-analysis; uncritical use produces many false positives.
**Sources:** [S692], [S690]

### Isolated "red flags" are reasons to ask, not emergencies
**Rule:** Treat night pain, age over 50 or "it has lasted a while" on their own as reasons to ask more questions, not as emergencies. Escalate when red flags cluster or progress, with a history of cancer, with systemic illness (fever, feeling unwell, unexplained weight loss), or when neurological signs appear. Escalation means a GP within days, or emergency care for cauda equina signs.
**Evidence:** A review of 16 national low back pain guidelines found 46 different red flags with little agreement; most used significant trauma and steroids for fracture, history of cancer and unintentional weight loss for malignancy, and often night or rest pain. Accuracy data were rarely provided; 8 guidelines based their choice on consensus or earlier guidelines and 5 gave no reference. The 2013 Cochrane fracture review warns many red flags have high false-positive rates and uncritical use would affect cost of care and patient outcomes.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Adults with low back pain in primary care or community settings.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Guidelines and the IFOMPT framework still advise screening for these features while accuracy reviews show most are weak alone; the framework resolves this with cluster-based clinical reasoning. The Williams 2013 Cochrane review was updated in 2023.
**Sources:** [S693], [S694], [S688]

## Acute injuries that need imaging or urgent care

### Ankle roll: Ottawa ankle rules
**Rule:** After an ankle roll, if the client cannot take 4 steps, or pressing on the back edge or tip of either ankle bone, the navicular or the base of the 5th metatarsal is tender, send them for medical assessment and X-ray and do not train the limb. If the rule is negative, manage as a sprain with early protected loading.
**Evidence:** Under the Ottawa ankle rules, X-ray is indicated if the person cannot walk 4 steps (right after injury or when assessed) or has bone tenderness at the posterior edge or tip of either malleolus; for the midfoot, the same walking test or tenderness at the navicular or base of the 5th metatarsal. A meta-analysis of 27 studies (15,581 patients) found pooled sensitivity 97.6% (95% CI 96.4-98.9) and negative likelihood ratio 0.08 (0.07 in children); at 15% prevalence a negative rule leaves under 1.4% probability of fracture. Specificity is modest (median about 32%).
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Adults and children with acute ankle or midfoot injury, mostly studied in emergency departments. The coach screens, not diagnoses.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Many rule-positive people will not have a fracture; the rule exists to rule fractures out. The ankle sprain guideline adds that ligament damage is best graded 4-5 days after injury.
**Sources:** [S697], [S698]

### Knee injury: Ottawa knee rule
**Rule:** After a knee injury, stop the session and refer for X-ray if the client is 55 or older, has tenderness only over the kneecap or over the fibular head, cannot bend the knee to 90 degrees, or cannot put weight on the leg and walk a few steps, either right after the injury or now.
**Evidence:** The Ottawa knee rule indicates X-ray after acute knee trauma for age 55 or over, isolated patellar tenderness, fibular head tenderness, inability to flex to 90 degrees, or inability to bear weight both immediately and at assessment. Three meta-analyses agree on sensitivity of 98-99% (Bachmann 2004: 98.5%, 6 studies, 4,249 adults; Sims 2020: 0.99, 8 studies, 7,385 patients; Kazemi 2023: 0.98, 18 studies, 6,702 patients), with specificity 43-49% and negative likelihood ratio 0.05-0.12.
**Grade:** strong
**Applies to:** Adults with acute knee injury assessed in emergency settings. A coach applying it informally is outside the validated setting, so any doubt means referral.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** The rule only rules out fracture; it does not detect ligament or meniscal injury. Low specificity means many flagged people will not have a fracture.
**Sources:** [S699], [S700], [S701]

### Knee injury: signs of ligament or meniscus damage
**Rule:** After an acute knee injury, stop loading that leg and advise medical assessment (not training through it) if there was a pop with swelling within 1-2 hours, the knee gives way or feels unstable, it locks or cannot fully straighten, or the Ottawa criteria apply. This is triage, not diagnosis. Long-standing catching in an older knee with osteoarthritis is not an emergency; after a clinical check it is usually managed with exercise.
**Evidence:** A review in American Family Physician gives a table of clues adapted from a New Zealand guideline: ACL tear: pivoting or leaping injury, audible pop, instability, early swelling within one to two hours; meniscal tear: twisting injury, giving way, locking and catching, loss of extension. It recommends suspecting internal derangement in knee trauma with effusion (SORT C). A 2023 JAMA review notes that meniscal tears follow acute twisting trauma in people under 40 or are degenerative in people 40 or older with osteoarthritis, that exercise therapy is first-line for degenerative tears, and that severe traumatic (bucket-handle) tears likely need surgery.
**Grade:** emerging
**Applies to:** Adults with acute traumatic knee injury seen in primary care or emergency settings; the clue table itself is ungraded.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Clinical clues, not a validated decision rule; the Ottawa rule detects fractures only. Neurological symptoms after knee injury were not found as a listed red flag in the opened sources.
**Sources:** [S815], [S816]

### Suspected Achilles rupture
**Rule:** If a client feels a sudden "kick" or "snap" at the back of the heel (often during a jump, lunge or sprint), with weak push-off, a gap you can see or feel, or the calf-squeeze test does not move the foot, stop immediately. Send them for same-day medical or emergency assessment, keep weight off the leg, and do not stretch or test it further.
**Evidence:** Acute Achilles ruptures are commonly missed. In 66 surgically confirmed acute ruptures, all had an abnormal Thompson test, decreased resting tension and a palpable defect (sensitivity 100%); clinical examination was more sensitive than MRI, and waiting for MRI delayed care.
**Grade:** emerging
**Applies to:** Adults, typically middle-aged recreational athletes.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** A single retrospective series plus small ultrasound studies; partial ruptures may not show all signs.
**Sources:** [S702], [S703]

## Habits and behaviour change

### Intention is not enough
**Rule:** Do not treat a client's motivation or "I'll start Monday" as a plan. Always turn intention into a concrete when/where/how plan, with a barrier plan and a tracking method (see Action planning).
**Evidence:** About half of people who intend to be active do not follow through. Rhodes & de Bruijn 2013 (10 studies, N=3,899) found an intention-PA gap of 46% (36% unsuccessful vs 42% successful intenders). A 2023 update (25 samples, N=29,600) found a gap of 47.6% (33.0% unsuccessful, 38.7% successful). The authors describe successful translation of intention into behaviour as "nearly at chance".
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Adults in observational studies of PA at guideline levels; general population, not gym-specific.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** The 2013 abstract reports 46% in its results but 48% in its contribution statement. Both meta-analyses come from the same group with likely overlapping samples, so they are not independent confirmations. Individual gaps vary widely.
**Sources:** [S622], [S623]

### Action planning and if-then plans
**Rule:** For each planned session, have the client write a specific plan (day, time, place, workout) plus 1-2 "if [barrier], then [backup]" plans (for example "if I finish work late, then I do the 25-min home version"). Revisit and renew the plans at check-ins rather than setting them once.
**Evidence:** Implementation intentions: 26 studies, SMD 0.31 (95% CI 0.11-0.51) post-intervention and 0.24 (0.13-0.35) at follow-up, larger with barrier management (coping plans) and in student and clinical samples. Planning interventions in the general population: 35 RCTs, SMD 0.35 (0.25-0.44), I2 61.4%, larger in patients, males, for action planning, face-to-face delivery and with reinforcement during follow-up. A meta-analysis of 13 RCTs reported a significant effect (0.25, 95% CI 0.05-0.45) in the subgroup where implementation intentions were reinforced.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Adults aged about 18-76, including students and clinical groups (coronary disease, obesity, diabetes). Limited evidence for gym resistance training specifically.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Effects are modest and heterogeneous. A one-off planning exercise may fade. Effects may be smaller in healthy non-student adults than in patients.
**Sources:** [S624], [S625], [S626]

### Self-monitoring tied to a goal and a review
**Rule:** Have every client track one or two key behaviours (sessions done, steps, protein or vegetable servings, and food logging if willing). Always pair tracking with a goal agreed with the client and a planned, regular review of the log with feedback; logging with no goal or review is weaker. Because relapse is common, help the client prepare simple if-then plans for lapses, and keep monitoring and feedback going at regular intervals after a programme ends. For clients aged 60 and over, keep tracking and relapse planning light and supportive and watch whether they lower the client's confidence.
**Evidence:** In a meta-regression of 122 evaluations (N=44,747; pooled ES 0.31, 95% CI 0.26-0.36, I2 69%), self-monitoring explained more between-study heterogeneity than any other technique (13%), and combining it with at least one other control-theory technique (goal setting, feedback, reviewing goals) was more effective (ES 0.42 vs 0.26). In adults with obesity (mean age 40 or more, mean BMI 30 or more; 48 RCTs), goal setting and self-monitoring of behaviour predicted effects short term (6 months or less, overall ES 0.37) and long term (12 months or more, ES 0.24). NICE PH49 (2014) says interventions should aim to start and maintain change and include techniques for relapse, which is common: agree goals and coping plans with the client, support self-monitoring with feedback, plan when progress is reviewed, and keep feedback and monitoring going at regular intervals for at least a year after a programme, with rehearsed if-then plans for lapses.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Adults in community or clinical PA and diet interventions, many overweight or obese; weight-loss self-monitoring studies used mostly white women. Not trained lifters. Effects on behaviour are small to moderate. NICE PH49 covers people aged 16 and over changing alcohol, eating, activity, sexual-health and smoking behaviours.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** High heterogeneity; meta-regressions are cross-study associations. The needed dose is not established. Burke 2011 rated evidence on self-monitoring and weight as weak. In adults aged 60 and over, prompting self-monitoring was associated with lower self-efficacy and PA (French 2014). Some people find food logging burdensome or triggering, so offer lighter forms such as a habit checklist. NICE gives no effect sizes, and its at least one year of follow-up is advice for funded programmes, not a tested dose for coaching.
**Sources:** [S617], [S618], [S263], [S619], [S829]

### Goal setting
**Rule:** Set specific, short-horizon behaviour goals (for example 3 gym sessions/week, 8,000 steps/day) alongside any outcome goal (kg, waist). Review them weekly and adjust. Keep goals the client agrees to rather than imposed ones. With clients aged 60 and over, keep goals flexible and focus on building confidence.
**Evidence:** Multi-component goal-setting interventions increase physical activity with a medium effect: d=0.55 (95% CI 0.43-0.67) across 45 articles, 52 interventions and n=5,912. In adults with obesity, setting behaviour goals predicted effects short and long term, and setting outcome goals predicted effects long term.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Adults across diverse settings with PA as outcome; no direct data on gym strength training adherence. Does not apply cleanly to adults aged 60 and over.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** In community-dwelling adults aged 60 and over, behavioural goal setting was associated with lower PA and self-efficacy (French 2014). Some people react badly to rigid targets, so use ranges and let the client choose.
**Sources:** [S620], [S618], [S619]

### Older adults: lead with confidence, not targets
**Rule:** For clients aged 60 and over, lead with confidence-building (graded, successful experiences, social support, enjoyable activities) rather than heavy target-setting, tracking and comparison with others. Introduce goals and tracking gently, and only if the client finds them helpful.
**Evidence:** In community-dwelling adults aged 60 and over (24 studies), interventions increased self-efficacy (d=0.37) and physical activity (d=0.14). In moderator analyses, interventions including behavioural goals, prompted self-monitoring, relapse planning, normative information or performance feedback were associated with lower self-efficacy and lower PA; the authors conclude many techniques that work for younger adults may not work for older adults.
**Grade:** emerging
**Applies to:** Non-clinical, community-dwelling adults aged 60 and over in PA interventions; says nothing about weight loss or resistance-training programmes.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Conflicts with self-monitoring and goal-setting evidence from younger samples. Between-study associations from one review, confounded by other intervention features. Individual older adults may still benefit from tracking.
**Sources:** [S619]

### Wearables and step counters
**Rule:** Encourage a step counter or wearable for clients who want one. Use its data as feedback in weekly check-ins (trend vs goal), not as a judgement. Increase step targets gradually.
**Evidence:** An umbrella review of 39 systematic reviews and meta-analyses (163,992 participants) found activity trackers increase physical activity (SMD 0.3-0.6, about 1,800 extra steps/day or about 40 min/day more walking), improve body composition (SMD 0.7-2.0) and fitness (SMD 0.3), and reduce bodyweight by about 1 kg, with benefit judged sustained over time. In adults with obesity, adding objects to the environment (such as a step counter), feedback on outcomes and graded tasks predicted long-term effects.
**Grade:** strong
**Applies to:** All ages and clinical and non-clinical populations; effects are on walking and general PA, not resistance training.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Effects on blood pressure, lipids, HbA1c and quality of life are small and often non-significant; the weight effect is modest. Some people stop wearing devices.
**Sources:** [S621], [S618]

### How long habits take
**Rule:** Tell clients to expect roughly 2 months, and often 3-5 months or more, before training or diet behaviours feel automatic; a new gym routine of 4 or more sessions a week may start to feel habitual after about 6 weeks. Keep the behaviour in a stable context (same cue, time and place). One missed session does not undo progress; get back on track at the next opportunity ("never miss twice" is a practical heuristic, not a tested finding). Never promise "21 days".
**Evidence:** In Lally et al., 96 volunteers repeated a daily eating, drinking or activity behaviour in a fixed context for 12 weeks; for the 62 whose curve could be modelled, reaching 95% of the automaticity plateau took 18 to 254 days (median 66 days, as reported in the 2024 review), and missing a single opportunity did not materially affect habit formation. A 2024 systematic review and meta-analysis (20 studies, n=2,601; 11 at high risk of bias) found the 4 studies reporting time to habit gave medians of 59-66 days and means of 106-154 days (individual range 4-335 days); habit strength rose pre- to post-intervention (SMD 0.69, 95% CI 0.49-0.88). In 111 new gym members, exercising at least 4 times a week for 6 weeks was the minimum to establish an exercise habit.
**Grade:** emerging
**Applies to:** Adults building simple health behaviours (mean ages 21.5-73.5 in the review); one cohort of new gym members. Complex behaviours such as full workouts probably take longer.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Very large individual variation (4-335 days). Habit is self-reported automaticity; times come from 4 unpooled studies mostly at high risk of bias, and the authors call the evidence "emerging". The 6-week gym estimate is shorter than the review's 2-month median. Every source points well beyond 21 days.
**Sources:** [S627], [S628], [S629]

### What builds habit strength
**Rule:** Start with a simple, repeatable routine that the client chooses, at a fixed time and place. Make sessions enjoyable and uncomplicated, add complexity only once attendance is consistent, and build preparatory habits (packed gym bag, scheduled slot).
**Evidence:** In new gym members, habit strength grew with consistency (beta 0.21), low complexity (0.19), a supportive environment (0.17) and positive affective judgments (0.13). Across health behaviours, frequency, timing, type of habit, self-selection, affective judgements, behavioural regulation and preparatory habits influenced habit strength; morning practices and self-chosen habits were generally stronger.
**Grade:** emerging
**Applies to:** Adults forming new exercise or diet habits; one gym cohort (n=111) and a small meta-analysis mostly at high risk of bias.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** The morning advantage is a correlate, not causal; chronotype and work schedule matter. Habit and intention independently predicted behaviour, so habits complement motivation.
**Sources:** [S629], [S627]

### Autonomy-supportive coaching
**Rule:** Offer choices (exercise selection, schedule, diet approach) and explain the rationale for recommendations. Acknowledge the client's perspective and avoid guilt, pressure and shame. Build competence with achievable progressions, combine this with behaviour goals and feedback on outcomes, and help the client link training to values they care about, not just appearance.
**Evidence:** Self-determination theory interventions (73 studies) produced small-to-medium increases in autonomous motivation and health behaviour at the end of the intervention and at follow-up, with small gains in physical and psychological health; increases in need support and autonomous motivation were associated with behaviour change, controlled motivation and amotivation were not. A systematic review of 66 exercise studies found autonomous motivation consistently predicted exercise; identified regulation predicted adoption, intrinsic motivation long-term adherence, and competence satisfaction participation. In RCTs of adults with overweight or obesity, autonomy-supportive, person-centred counselling (as in Motivational Interviewing), together with goal setting and outcome feedback, explained all between-study variation in long-term effects.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Adults in health-promotion and disease-management settings; exercise evidence is mostly correlational. The Samdal meta-regression covers RCTs of 12 weeks or more in adults with mean age 40 or more and mean BMI 30 or more.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Effects are "modest, heterogeneous". Evidence on health or body-image motives and guilt-driven exercise is mixed. Some clients want directive coaching; structure and autonomy support are compatible.
**Sources:** [S630], [S631], [S618]

### Make early sessions feel good
**Rule:** For beginners and returners, keep early sessions at an intensity and volume that feel tolerable or good while doing them. Avoid "smash" sessions and severe soreness in the first weeks. Ask how the session felt while doing it, not only afterwards, and adjust exercise choice and intensity to raise enjoyment.
**Evidence:** Across 24 studies, a positive change in affect during moderate-intensity exercise was linked to future physical activity, while affect after exercise had a null relationship; affective responses correlated with affective judgments about future activity but negligibly with intention. An umbrella review of 55 reviews on adherence listed "enjoyment and absence of unpleasant experiences" among 14 factors that at least 10 reviews proposed as raising adherence (a count of recommendations, not an effect size).
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Adults, mainly aerobic or moderate-intensity exercise data; resistance-training evidence is less direct. The umbrella review covers chronic-disease patients and older adults.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Mostly observational; Rhodes & Kates is a narrative synthesis. Some people enjoy hard training, and intensity tolerance is individual.
**Sources:** [S632], [S633]

### The first 3 months are the dropout window
**Rule:** Treat the first 3 months as the high-risk window. Schedule more frequent check-ins and early wins, and proactively ask about barriers in weeks 2-12. At intake, ask about age, prior activity and motives as risk markers; the source does not say which way each one points, so watch every new client closely. Prefer supervised, autonomy-supportive contact over pressure or guilt.
**Evidence:** Among 5,240 new members of one fitness centre in Rio de Janeiro, 63% abandoned before month 3 and fewer than 4% stayed more than 12 continuous months; age, previous PA, initial BMI and motives were associated with abandonment, and even the best combination of factors carried high risk. In a meta-analysis of 14 exercise RCTs (n=369) in people with anxiety or stress disorders, pooled dropout was 22.4% (95% CI 15.0-32.0%), similar to controls; controlling motivation strategies predicted higher dropout, while supervision in every session by an exercise-prescription expert and autonomous-motivation strategies predicted lower dropout.
**Grade:** emerging
**Applies to:** One Brazilian fitness centre (observational records 2005-2014) and clinical anxiety and stress populations. No general-population dropout meta-analysis was found.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Trial dropout (about 20%) is far lower than real-world gym attrition because trial participants are selected and supported. Leaving a gym is not stopping all exercise. Predictor directions are not given.
**Sources:** [S634], [S635], [S633]

## Weight tracking and maintenance

### Self-weighing and digital tracking
**Rule:** Use weigh-ins as one part of a broader plan, never on their own, and never make them required. Daily weighing is optional: averaged weekly, it gives the cleanest trend and is what the coach's trend tool prefers; weekly weighing is acceptable. No weigh-ins at all for anyone with a positive eating-disorder screen or warning sign (see Screening: brief questions as a prompt). Review trends with the client (accountability). Expect tracking to fade, so make it as low-friction as possible (apps, smart scales).
**Evidence:** Self-weighing alone has no proven effect (one trial: -0.5 kg, 95% CI -1.3 to 0.3); adding self-weighing or self-regulation to a multi-component programme gave -1.7 kg (-2.6 to -0.8). There was no significant difference between daily and weekly weighing, and accountability increased weight loss (p=0.03). In 39 RCTs of digital self-monitoring, greater engagement was linked to weight loss in 74% of occurrences; few interventions achieved engagement on at least 75% of days, and digital tracking had higher engagement than paper in 21 of 34 comparisons.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Adults with overweight or obesity in behavioural weight-loss programmes; not people with eating-disorder history.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Weight-loss outcomes did not differ between daily and weekly weighing, so preferring daily readings is about a less noisy trend for the trend tool, not about more weight lost. Self-weighing may cause distress in some people. The digital evidence is vote-counting, not a pooled effect.
**Sources:** [S636], [S264]

### Plan the maintenance phase
**Rule:** Plan maintenance explicitly. Keep physical activity high, continue light monitoring (periodic weigh-ins, occasional food logging), and use flexible rather than rigid restraint. Agree on an early "regain response" (for example return to tracking if weight rises by an agreed amount such as 2 kg; this threshold is pragmatic, not evidence-derived). Never assume maintenance happens on autopilot.
**Evidence:** Demographic factors do not predict weight-loss maintenance; behavioural and cognitive factors that reduce intake, increase expenditure and keep monitoring do (67 articles of prospective studies). Across 5 weight control registries (52 articles), increased physical activity was the most consistent correlate of maintenance. In the US National Weight Control Registry (2,886 members followed 10 years), more than 87% were estimated to still maintain at least 10% loss at years 5 and 10; regain was associated with decreases in leisure-time PA, dietary restraint and self-weighing, and increases in fat intake and disinhibition. Mediators of medium- and long-term weight control were autonomous motivation, self-efficacy, self-regulation skills, flexible eating restraint and positive body image.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Adults with overweight or obesity who lost weight without surgery or drugs. Registry data are self-selected and self-reported (NWCR: 78% female, mean age 48); mediator studies mostly aged 25-44, many women only.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Observational data; physical activity may be a marker rather than a cause. NWCR success rates do not generalise to typical dieters. Teixeira rated overall evidence as limited; harm from rigid restraint is not established; dietary mediators were inconsistent.
**Sources:** [S637], [S638], [S639], [S560]

### Honest expectations about regain
**Rule:** Set honest expectations: partial regain is common and is not failure, and keeping off a meaningful portion of the loss is a realistic success. Keep maintenance support (check-ins, activity) going beyond the end of the weight-loss phase.
**Evidence:** In obese adults who had lost at least 5%, behavioural maintenance interventions addressing diet and activity reduced regain by only about 1.56 kg vs control at 12 months (95% CI -2.27 to -0.86; 25 comparisons, 2,949 participants). A meta-analysis of 29 US studies found that 5 years after structured programmes the average person maintained a loss of more than 3 kg and more than 3% of initial weight, but after balanced hypoenergetic diets only 2.0 kg (95% CI 1.5 to 2.5) and 2.1%, against 7.1 kg after very-low-energy diets; six studies reported better maintenance in those who exercised more. In the NWCR, larger initial losses and longer maintenance predicted better outcomes.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Adults with obesity in structured programmes. Anderson 2001 is old, US-only and based on completers; NWCR is self-selected.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Averages hide wide variation. For balanced-diet programmes like typical coaching, average 5-year maintenance was about 2 kg. The exercise link is observational.
**Sources:** [S561], [S640], [S560]

## Pre-participation screening and cardiac safety

### PAR-Q+ 2023 as the default entry screen
**Rule:** At onboarding, give the full, unmodified PAR-Q+ 7 questions (the form forbids changes). All NO: begin a gradual programme. Any YES: ask the follow-up questions for that condition. Any follow-up YES: pause any progression of intensity and send the person to the ePARmed-X+ or a qualified exercise professional or physician. Repeat the screen every 12 months and whenever the user reports a new diagnosis, medication, symptom or pregnancy.
**Evidence:** The 2023 PAR-Q+ (consensus-approved version that "supersedes all previous editions") asks 7 questions: diagnosed heart condition or high blood pressure; chest pain at rest, in daily activities or with activity; loss of balance from dizziness or loss of consciousness in the last 12 months; another diagnosed chronic condition; prescribed medication for a chronic condition; a bone, joint or soft-tissue problem now or in the past 12 months that activity could worsen; a doctor's advice to do only medically supervised activity. All NO clears the person to become more active, starting slowly; clearance lasts at most 12 months and lapses if their condition changes. Any YES leads to condition-specific follow-up questions, and any YES there to the ePARmed-X+ and/or a qualified exercise professional. It was built with systematic reviews and the AGREE process to give a risk-stratified decision tree.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Adults and older adults starting or increasing activity. Self-report, so it depends on honest answers. Only the 2023 official version was verified.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Evidence is consensus plus systematic reviews; prospective data showing it prevents events are limited and its sensitivity for clinical events is not established. ACSM 2015 uses a different algorithm, so the two can classify the same person differently.
**Sources:** [S641], [S642]

### PAR-Q+ specific thresholds
**Rule:** Anyone over 45, or with any PAR-Q+ YES, gets a qualified exercise professional or clinician review before vigorous or maximal work (HIIT, 1RM testing, all-out sprints). All-out sprint formats (SIT, REHIT) also require all PAR-Q+ answers NO and at least 4 weeks of regular moderate cardio without symptoms; they are options for already-active clients who lack time, never for sedentary beginners. Ask hypertensive users for a recent resting BP and apply the bands in Resting BP thresholds (160/90 or higher, or unknown, means GP review before starting or progressing). Postpone training during a febrile illness. Sedentary beginners start with 10-20 min walks and build towards 150 min/week; 20-60 min of moderate work, 3-5 days/week, is the target once that is established.
**Evidence:** In the 2023 PAR-Q+, people over 45 not used to regular vigorous-to-maximal effort should consult a qualified exercise professional before training at that intensity, even if they answer NO to every question. In the hypertension follow-up, resting BP of 160/90 mmHg or higher (with or without medication), or not knowing one's BP, counts as YES. The diabetes follow-up flags post-exercise hypoglycaemia, complications and plans for unusually vigorous exercise. Everyone should delay becoming more active during a temporary illness such as a cold or fever, if pregnant (talk to a health care practitioner and/or complete the ePARmed-X+ first), or if their health changes. Cleared people should start with 20-60 min of low-to-moderate exercise, 3-5 days/week, aerobic plus muscle strengthening, progressing toward 150 min/week or more of moderate activity.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Adults; the age-45 rule targets middle-aged and older adults. Thresholds are consensus cut-offs, not outcome-validated.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** ACSM 2015 uses current activity plus disease plus symptoms with no age cut-off, so the tools can disagree for a given person. The PAR-Q+ text suggests 20-60 min sessions from the start; shorter opening walks for sedentary people, the 4-week moderate-cardio prerequisite for sprints and the review for any PAR-Q+ YES are conservative product choices based on the gradual-progression evidence (see Acute cardiac risk is concentrated in the unaccustomed), not PAR-Q+ text.
**Sources:** [S641]

### ACSM 2015 screening algorithm
**Rule:** Classify every user into one of the six ACSM cells at onboarding. Inactive users with no disease and no symptoms start at light-to-moderate intensity without waiting for a doctor. Inactive users with diagnosed cardiovascular, metabolic (type 1 or 2 diabetes) or renal disease need medical clearance before starting any exercise, then start at light-to-moderate intensity. Already-active users with such disease may continue moderate work but need clearance before vigorous work. Anyone with symptoms gets no programme, or stops the current one, until a clinician has assessed them.
**Evidence:** ACSM's 2015 roundtable (Riebe et al.) removed risk-factor counting; referral rests on current physical activity, known cardiovascular/metabolic/renal disease or signs/symptoms of it, and desired intensity. "Regular exercise" means planned, structured activity of at least 30 min at moderate intensity, at least 3 days/week, for at least the last 3 months. The six cells: inactive, no disease, no symptoms: start light-moderate without clearance; inactive with known disease, no symptoms: clearance before any exercise; inactive with symptoms: clearance before any exercise; active, no disease, no symptoms: continue moderate or vigorous; active with known disease, no symptoms: continue moderate, clearance before vigorous; active with symptoms: stop and get clearance before continuing. In NHANES 2001-04 adults aged 40 and over, the algorithm referred 54.2% before any exercise and 2.6% only before vigorous exercise, vs 94.5% (AAPQ) and 68.4% (PAR-Q) with older questionnaires.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Adults; the NHANES figures cover US adults aged 40 and over, with 70.9% of people over 70 referred before any exercise.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** The authors say "additional validation is needed"; predictive ability is unproven. Self-reported symptoms in NHANES were high (54.2%, mostly shortness of breath, probably often non-cardiac), so real-world referral may be lower. Women were referred more often than men (59.8% vs 48.1%).
**Sources:** [S119], [S120]

### Cardiac warning symptoms: stop and refer
**Rule:** If a user reports any of the symptoms below, before or during a session, tell them to stop exercising and seek medical evaluation. Do not resume or adjust training until a clinician clears them. Never coach through them or reinterpret them as normal training discomfort. Separately, as general first-aid practice rather than ACSM text: chest pain at rest, fainting, or chest pain with breathlessness and sweating means calling emergency services now.
**Evidence:** Within the ACSM 2015 algorithm, these signs or symptoms at rest or during activity suggest cardiovascular, metabolic or renal disease: pain or discomfort in the chest, neck, jaw, arms or other areas that may be ischaemic; shortness of breath at rest or with mild exertion; dizziness or syncope; orthopnoea or paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnoea; ankle oedema; palpitations or tachycardia; intermittent claudication; known heart murmur; unusual fatigue or shortness of breath with usual activities. Any of these requires medical clearance before exercise of any intensity, including in people who already exercise, because exercise-related cardiovascular events are often preceded by warning signs.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** All adults, regardless of training status.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Symptoms can be atypical, especially in women, older adults and people with diabetes. The list is a consensus screen, not a validated diagnostic tool, and false positives are expected.
**Sources:** [S120], [S119]

### Acute cardiac risk is concentrated in the unaccustomed
**Rule:** Never start a sedentary or detrained user on vigorous or maximal work, including all-out sprint protocols (SIT, REHIT). Build volume and intensity over weeks, reaching regular moderate activity (at least 3 days/week) before adding vigorous sessions; all-out sprints additionally need the prerequisites in PAR-Q+ specific thresholds (all PAR-Q+ answers NO, at least 4 weeks of regular moderate cardio without symptoms, and a professional review if over 45). Treat "weekend warrior" bursts and sudden jumps in intensity as the highest-risk pattern. Reassure users that the absolute risk is tiny and the long-term benefit large.
**Evidence:** Heavy exertion (6 METs or more) raised the relative risk of MI in the following hour to 5.9 (95% CI 4.6-7.7); by habitual exercise frequency the RR was 107 for less than once a week, 19.4 for 1-2 times, 8.6 for 3-4 times and 2.4 for 5 or more times a week (Mittleman, case-crossover, n=1228 MI patients). In 21,481 male physicians free of self-reported CVD, the RR of sudden death during or up to 30 min after vigorous exertion was 16.9 (10.5-27.0), with an absolute risk of 1 per 1.51 million episodes, attenuated by habitual vigorous exercise (Albert). The AHA 2020 statement says vigorous activity, particularly in unfit people, can acutely increase SCD/MI risk in susceptible individuals.
**Grade:** strong
**Applies to:** Adults; observational case-crossover data from MI patients and US male physicians, so generalisation to women is uncertain.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Wide CIs (67-171 for the RR of 107). The AHA notes possible maladaptations at very high volumes and intensities, which concerns high-volume endurance athletes, not this population.
**Sources:** [S643], [S644], [S531]

### Regular activity is the main protection
**Rule:** When discussing cardiac risk, frame it this way: being consistently active is the main protection, and the transient risk is managed by progressing gradually, not by avoiding exercise. Do not use cardiac-risk language to discourage activity in people who have passed screening.
**Evidence:** In a meta-analysis of prospective studies (8 studies, 1193 sudden cardiac deaths among 136,298 participants), highest vs lowest physical activity gave a summary RR of 0.52 (95% CI 0.45-0.60, I2=0%), with RR 0.68 per 20 MET-h/week and no further reduction beyond about 20-25 MET-h/week; high vs low cardiorespiratory fitness gave RR 0.58. A systematic review concluded that the acute risk during exercise is outweighed by the benefits of regular vigorous exercise.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** General adult populations in the US and Europe; observational data.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Reverse causation and confounding are possible; the fitness estimate rests on 2 studies; dose-response shape is imprecise.
**Sources:** [S645], [S646]

### Over-screening is itself a harm
**Rule:** Do not tell every user to "see a doctor first": healthy, asymptomatic users without known cardiovascular, metabolic or renal disease do not need medical clearance and can start light-to-moderate activity straight away. Everyone else follows the ACSM 2015 algorithm exactly (see ACSM 2015 screening algorithm) and the PAR-Q+ follow-ups; this rule never relaxes them.
**Evidence:** ACSM dropped risk-factor profiling in 2015 because prior guidelines could cause "excessive physician referrals, possibly creating a barrier to exercise participation". In US adults aged 40 and over, older questionnaires would refer 94.5% (AAPQ) and 68.4% (PAR-Q), and the 2015 algorithm 54.2% before any exercise plus 2.6% only before vigorous exercise, so fewer but still most adults over 40. For type 2 diabetes, the ACSM 2022 consensus says pre-exercise stress testing in asymptomatic adults "remains controversial", one cited study found it did not reduce CVD events, and no evidence shows whether testing is needed before anaerobic or resistance training.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Adults; referral figures for US adults aged 40 and over; T2D statements for asymptomatic adults with T2D.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Whitfield's authors helped develop the algorithm and note it still needs validation. Some gyms, insurers or local laws still require clearance. Kanaley 2022 recommends clearance for sedentary people with T2D before moderate-to-high intensity, while ACSM 2015 asks inactive people with known metabolic disease for clearance before any exercise; the coach follows the more conservative ACSM 2015 rule.
**Sources:** [S119], [S120], [S647]

## Illness and return to training

### Head cold: optional light training
**Rule:** With head-cold symptoms only (runny or blocked nose, mild sore throat) and no fever, allow optional light-to-moderate training (RPE 5 or less, shorter session, no maximal efforts or PRs). Stop if the person feels worse. Avoid the gym if contagious, out of courtesy. Any below-the-neck or systemic symptom (fever, chills, body aches, chest tightness, productive cough, diarrhoea, marked fatigue) means rest (next rule).
**Evidence:** The popular "neck check" is expert opinion, described in a 2024 commentary as "nonscientific but may be partly useful"; a scoping review of athletes returning after COVID-19 classes runny or blocked nose, tickly throat, coughing, sneezing and sore throat as above-neck symptoms, and high temperature, tight chest, persistent cough, nausea, vomiting, palpitations, malaise, diarrhoea, breathlessness, muscle and joint aches and swollen lymph nodes as below-neck symptoms. In the only direct experimental test, 50 rhinovirus-inoculated adults aged 18-29 of moderate fitness took part: 34 randomly assigned to exercise (40 min at 70% HR reserve every other day for 10 days) and 16 separately recruited controls; symptom scores and mucus weights did not differ. In athletes and military recruits, about 80% of acute respiratory illnesses caused no more than 1 day of lost training (time loss over 1 day: 20.4%, 95% CI 15.3-25.4%); symptoms lasted a mean 7.1 days (95% CI 6.2-8.0), and days-to-return data are very limited (4 studies, 0-8.5 days).
**Grade:** emerging
**Applies to:** Young, moderately fit adults with an induced mild cold; athletes and recruits aged 15-65. Not influenza, COVID-19, older adults or people with cardiac or respiratory disease.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Thresholds are not validated; controls were not randomised. Snyders et al. found outcomes did not differ between upper and general respiratory illness, which neither supports nor refutes the neck split.
**Sources:** [S756], [S757], [S817], [S818]

### Fever and systemic illness: do not train
**Rule:** Fever (38.0 C or higher, or feeling feverish with chills), body aches or feeling systemically unwell means no training until fever-free for at least 24-48 h without antipyretics and symptoms are clearly improving. Then restart at a clearly reduced load (about 50% of usual) and build up over several sessions. Chest pain or tightness, palpitations, unusual breathlessness, dizziness or fainting during or after illness means stop and get medical assessment before any exercise.
**Evidence:** Strenuous exercise may be hazardous during an infection with fever and should always be avoided; febrile infections cause muscle wasting and impaired central circulatory function, and full recovery may take several weeks to months after week-long febrile infections. Myocarditis may or may not come with fever, malaise or catarrhal symptoms, and exercise should be avoided whenever it is suspected. The ACC (2022) says strenuous activity should be avoided for 3-6 months where COVID-19 myocarditis is diagnosed. The ECSS/ACSM consensus advises suspending or reducing training during infections and allowing time to recover.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** All adults. Mechanistic, narrative-review and consensus evidence, no RCTs; the ACC timeframe is for COVID-19 myocarditis in athletes.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** The temperature threshold, 24-48 h window and 50% restart load are pragmatic conventions. The no-strenuous-exercise-with-fever principle is consistent across sources.
**Sources:** [S758], [S759], [S747]

### Return after COVID-19 or flu-like illness
**Rule:** Start once symptoms have resolved (or after 3 days if asymptomatic). Use about 3-5 progressive stages: walking and mobility; light aerobic work at RPE 3-4 for 15-30 min; moderate aerobic plus light resistance; normal training without maximal efforts; full training. Advance only after 24 h symptom-free, and step back one stage if fatigue or symptoms return. Any cardiopulmonary symptom means stop and get a medical check. Clients with diabetes, cardiovascular or renal disease, a hospital stay, or symptoms beyond 7 days need GP clearance first.
**Evidence:** The 2022 ACC expert consensus recommends 3 days without training for asymptomatic COVID-19, withholding exercise until symptoms resolve for mild non-cardiopulmonary illness (isolated loss of smell or taste does not delay return), a graded return for everyone, and says the earlier 10-day abstention is "no longer necessary". Chest pain or tightness, breathlessness, palpitations, or lightheadedness or syncope call for ECG, troponin and echocardiogram before intense training. ACC considers it reasonable to apply this to "masters-level recreational exercise enthusiasts". Elliott 2020 (BJSM) recommended at least 10 days of rest and 7 days symptom-free; its regression rule (drop back a stage if symptoms appear, progress after at least 24 h symptom-free) remains useful, and it asks for medical assessment before starting in people with comorbidities, after hospital admission, or with symptoms worsening or lasting beyond 7 days.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Adults returning after COVID-19; guidance written for athletes, extended by ACC to recreational exercisers. Applying it to influenza is an extrapolation.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Elliott's 10-day rest is now more conservative than ACC; use ACC, but slow down for lingering fatigue (some athletes take over 3 weeks). For post-COVID conditions with post-exertional malaise, ACC cautions against upright graded exercise. Stage counts are not evidence-derived.
**Sources:** [S759], [S760]

## Exertional rhabdomyolysis

### Recognise it and send to urgent care
**Rule:** Tell the client to go to A&E or urgent care the same day if, after training, they have dark, cola- or tea-coloured urine; marked muscle swelling or tightness; pain far beyond normal soreness or pain on gentle stretching; inability to straighten or lift a limb normally (arms after high-rep curls or pull-ups, thighs after spin); numbness or tingling; or much reduced urine output. Seek medical review if soreness is still worsening after about 3 days or has not eased by 5-7 days. Stop all training until a doctor clears them, do not suggest NSAIDs, and stress hydration. The coach never "waits and sees" with dark urine.
**Evidence:** Per the CHAMP/USU guideline (2025), exertional rhabdomyolysis differs from DOMS by severe or disproportionate pain and tenderness, swelling, significant loss of range of motion, weakness (especially hip or shoulder girdle), cola-coloured urine, or pain persisting or worsening beyond 5-7 days; signs of compartment syndrome need urgent surgical assessment. CHAMP diagnosis requires severe muscle symptoms plus CK of 5,000 IU/L or more after exertion; ENMC consensus uses CK above 10,000 IU/L for exertional cases, with CK peaking 1-4 days after the event and normalising within 1-2 weeks of rest. In 93 hospitalised spin-induced cases (Singapore), 87.1% were first-time spin participants (mean age 28.6, 71% female), 73.1% had dark urine and 1.1% developed AKI. In 200 hospitalised exertional cases (Kaiser Permanente, mean age 30.5, 72.5% male), 8.5% developed AKI, none CKD or death; CK did not predict AKI, while pre-admission NSAID use (64.7% of AKI cases vs 21.9%) and dehydration were associated with it.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** All adults; data mainly from young adults in hospitalised case series and a military guideline aimed also at sports medicine. Incidence in gym-goers is unknown; older adults and statin users not specifically studied.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Expert consensus plus retrospective cohorts, no RCTs; a military CPG notes "a paucity of evidence-based guidance". CK thresholds differ (5,000 vs 10,000 IU/L). Asymptomatic athletes can have very high CK, so symptoms and urine drive referral. Recurrent episodes raise the question of genetic myopathies.
**Sources:** [S761], [S648], [S649], [S650], [S651]

### Prevention in novices
**Rule:** For beginners and returners (including after more than about 6-9 months off), keep the first 2 weeks clearly submaximal (stop sets at 3 or more RIR); avoid sets to failure, high-rep eccentric-heavy work (high-rep negatives, drop sets, 100-rep challenges), and full-intensity spin or CrossFit-style classes in session 1. Cap first sessions of any new modality and increase volume gradually rather than doubling it. Do not train hard in heat, when dehydrated, after alcohol, after poor sleep, with stimulant pre-workouts, with NSAIDs, or just after a viral illness. Warn class newcomers to self-limit and to recognise dark urine.
**Evidence:** CHAMP/USU summarises risk as "too much, too fast, and too soon". Contributing factors include high-intensity, repetitive or prolonged unaccustomed exercise; high-volume eccentric work; highly motivated individuals and inexperienced trainers; stimulants (caffeine, synephrine, yohimbine and others) and medicines including statins and NSAIDs; hot and humid climate; recent viral illness; sleep deprivation; pre-exercise hypovolaemia; and genetic factors such as sickle cell trait; consensus criteria also list alcohol. A systematic review of 97 spinning cases found most were healthy women at their first session, presenting after about 3.1 days with myalgia, dark urine and thigh weakness; 7.2% developed AKI, 2.1% needed dialysis and 4.1% fasciotomy. Among 772 athletes with exertional rhabdomyolysis, 14.8% occurred after weightlifting.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Novice and returning adults in gyms; military recruits and athletes are overrepresented, and spinning data come from case reports.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** No guideline gives numeric caps on first-session volume; the progression numbers are pragmatic. Genetic myopathies, sickle cell trait and statins change risk sharply; rhabdomyolysis after an ordinary workload needs specialist referral.
**Sources:** [S761], [S774], [S775]

### Return after rhabdomyolysis or severe DOMS
**Rule:** After diagnosed rhabdomyolysis, the client resumes only with medical clearance and follows the doctor's phase. Once cleared, weeks 1-4 use light loads well short of failure with no maximal or timed efforts, increasing toward moderate weekly only if pain, swelling, weakness or dark urine do not return. Avoiding heavy eccentric emphasis and hot or humid conditions in this period is a conservative extrapolation from the listed risk factors. Full training typically resumes after about 4 weeks or more, once strength is near normal and symptoms have gone. Specialist (neuromuscular) assessment is needed before resuming if it followed an ordinary workload, recurs, CK or symptoms have not normalised after 1-2 weeks of rest, or there is a personal or family history of rhabdomyolysis, sickle cell trait or malignant hyperthermia. Apply the same graded approach after severe DOMS.
**Evidence:** The CHAMP/USU 2025 guideline gives a phased return while stating "no specific rehabilitation protocol can be proposed based on published data". Phase 1: 72 h of strict light activity, oral fluids, no weight training, 7-8 consecutive hours of sleep, repeat CK and urinalysis at 24-72 h. Phase 2, once improving with CK below 5,000 IU/L and/or falling and normal urinalysis: light activity, no maximal or timed efforts, light resistance training avoiding failure, progressing to moderate over about 3-4 weeks with weekly follow-up. Phase 3 (full training) once there is no significant weakness, swelling, pain or soreness. Persistent CK of 5,000 IU/L or more or abnormal urinalysis at 2 weeks means expert referral; myalgia without objective findings beyond 4 weeks warrants specialty evaluation. High-recurrence markers include delayed recovery, CK above 1,000 IU/L after 1-2 weeks of rest, an episode after a low or moderate workload, peak CK above 100,000 IU/L, unresolved AKI, an irremovable drug or supplement trigger, and relevant personal or family history. ENMC recommends genetic testing guided by the "RHABDO" acronym and follow-up of physical and psychological sequelae.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Low-risk adults after exertional rhabdomyolysis or significant DOMS; written for military personnel and based on expert opinion. High-risk cases need an individual medical plan.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Phases are consensus; timelines depend on symptoms, CK trend and kidney function. Normal post-exercise CK varies with age, sex, race and exercise type.
**Sources:** [S761], [S648]

## Diabetes

### What the coach can and cannot do
**Rule:** For users with diabetes, confirm their medications and apply the ACSM 2015 algorithm: currently inactive users need medical clearance before starting any programme, and already-active users need it before progressing to vigorous work. On insulin or a sulfonylurea, require a clinician's hypoglycaemia plan and glucose checks around sessions, and stop at symptoms (shakiness, sweating, confusion). Ask about eye, kidney, foot and heart complications; if any are present, get clinician clearance and specific restrictions. Nutrition plans (deficits, carb manipulation, fasting) go through a registered dietitian and the diabetes team, not the coach alone. Programme 150 min/week or more of aerobic work plus 2-3 resistance days.
**Evidence:** ACSM 2022: for low-to-moderate activity such as brisk walking, most people with T2D need no pre-exercise medical evaluation unless symptoms of CVD or microvascular complications are present; sedentary adults should get clearance before moderate-to-high intensity. Exercise-related hypoglycaemia risk is minimal on lifestyle treatment alone but raised with insulin and secretagogues (sulfonylureas, meglitinides); euglycaemic ketoacidosis can occur with SGLT-2 inhibitors. With unstable proliferative or severe retinopathy avoid vigorous breath-holding, heavy or overhead lifting and head-down positions; exercise is contraindicated with untreated proliferative retinopathy or recent eye surgery. Neuropathy calls for daily foot checks; diabetic kidney disease for avoiding excessive BP rises. ADA Standards 2026: refer people with prediabetes or diabetes to a registered dietitian nutritionist for medical nutrition therapy (Grade A); at least 150 min/week of moderate-to-vigorous aerobic activity over at least 3 days plus 2-3 non-consecutive resistance sessions/week (Grade B, T2D).
**Grade:** strong
**Applies to:** Adults with T2D (ADA also covers T1D and prediabetes). Type 1 insulin management needs a specialist.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Pre-exercise stress testing remains controversial; clearance recommendations are consensus-based; hypoglycaemia risk depends on the drug regimen. ACSM 2022 waives evaluation for low-to-moderate activity in asymptomatic T2D, while the ACSM 2015 algorithm asks inactive people with known metabolic disease for clearance before any exercise; the coach follows the more conservative 2015 rule.
**Sources:** [S647], [S658], [S119]

### Only some users need pre-exercise carbohydrate
**Rule:** At intake, record diabetes type and medication class. Apply pre-workout glucose checks and carbohydrate rules only to clients on insulin or sulfonylureas/meglitinides. Clients with T2D on metformin, GLP-1 RA or SGLT2i alone do not need routine pre-workout carbohydrate "just in case". If a client takes a beta-blocker as well as insulin or a secretagogue, ask for a glucose check before and after the session instead of relying on symptoms.
**Evidence:** ADA Standards of Care 2026 states hypoglycaemia "is not common in those who are not treated with insulin or insulin secretagogues, and no routine preventive measures for hypoglycemia are usually advised in these cases." Beta-blockers can blunt hypoglycaemia awareness (ADA 2016 position statement).
**Grade:** strong
**Applies to:** Adults with T1D or T2D in a general gym; medication classes are self-reported and the coach does not change medication.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Glycaemic responses vary a lot; carbohydrate and medication adjustments are individual and belong to the diabetes team.
**Sources:** [S658], [S731]

### Low pre-exercise glucose: eat first
**Rule:** For insulin or secretagogue users, ask for a glucose reading (meter or CGM) before every session. Below 90 mg/dL (5.0 mmol/L), the client takes 15-30 g fast carbohydrate and rechecks before starting; a falling CGM trend arrow means more carbohydrate or waiting. Starting at 90-250 mg/dL is fine. For more than 30-60 min of steady cardio, plan carbohydrate during the session. Insulin-dose reductions are referred to the diabetes team.
**Evidence:** ADA 2026: carbohydrate may need to be ingested if pre-exercise glucose is below 90 mg/dL. ADA 2016 (T1D table): below 90 mg/dL take 15-30 g fast-acting carbohydrate before starting (brief or very high-intensity work such as weights or intervals may need none; prolonged moderate work needs 0.5-1.0 g/kg/h); at 90-150 mg/dL start carbohydrate at the onset of most exercise; at 150-250 mg/dL start and delay carbohydrate until glucose is below 150. The ideal pre-exercise range is 90-250 mg/dL. EASD/ISPAD 2020 (CGM) allows starting at 90 mg/dL, puts the ideal at 126-180 mg/dL (7-10 mmol/L), with 10-15 g carbohydrate if below target. These thresholds are expert consensus (level D).
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Adults with T1D, and by extension T2D on insulin or secretagogues. Not needed for T2D on non-hypoglycaemic agents.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Consensus-level evidence. EASD/ISPAD sets higher targets for people at higher hypoglycaemia risk and children (up to 145-216 mg/dL). Carbohydrate dose depends on body size, insulin on board, time of day and session type.
**Sources:** [S658], [S731], [S732]

### High glucose and ketones
**Rule:** Above about 250 mg/dL (13.9 mmol/L), insulin users (especially T1D) check ketones. If ketones are moderate or high (blood 1.5 mmol/L or more), or the client feels unwell (nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, unusual thirst or fatigue), there is no session today: refer to their diabetes plan, or urgent care if unwell. If ketones are negative or trace and they feel well, only light-to-moderate aerobic work, no HIIT or heavy lifting until glucose is below 250. In T2D without insulin, glucose up to about 300 mg/dL with no symptoms allows light activity with fluids.
**Evidence:** ADA 2016 (T1D): at 250-350 mg/dL test ketones; no exercise with moderate-to-large ketones, otherwise only mild-to-moderate exercise and delay intense exercise until glucose is below 250; postpone or stop if blood ketones are 1.5 mmol/L or more. ACSM 2022 (T2D): do not begin exercise above 250 mg/dL with moderate or high ketones; above 300 mg/dL without excess ketones, use caution, hydrate and start only if feeling well. EASD/ISPAD 2020 (T1D): above 270 mg/dL (15 mmol/L) measure ketones; exercise is contraindicated above 1.5 mmol/L, 0.6-1.4 mmol/L should be addressed first, and at 1.5 or less only mild aerobic exercise. ADA 2026 notes intense activity such as HIIT may raise glucose further.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Adults with T1D (most relevant) and T2D; ketone testing mainly for insulin and SGLT2i users.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Cut-offs differ (250, 270, or 250 with ketones/300 without), all expert consensus. Ketones are seldom high in T2D, hence the more lenient T2D rule. SGLT2i users are an exception (next rule).
**Sources:** [S647], [S731], [S732], [S658]

### SGLT2 inhibitors: ketoacidosis with near-normal glucose
**Rule:** For clients on SGLT2 inhibitors (the "-gliflozins"), a normal glucose reading does not rule out ketoacidosis. If the client feels unwell (nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, unusual breathlessness or fatigue), skip the session and refer them to a clinician that day. Do not prescribe ketogenic or very-low-carbohydrate diets to SGLT2i users.
**Evidence:** ACSM 2022 warns diabetic ketoacidosis "may occur with euglycemia or only moderate hyperglycemia" with SGLT2 inhibitors, so activity should only be done when people with elevated glucose feel well. ADA 2026 (rec 5.26, grade E) advises educating SGLT-inhibitor users at risk of DKA on its signs and mitigation, providing beta-hydroxybutyrate testing, and discouraging a ketogenic eating pattern.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Adults with T2D (and off-label T1D) on SGLT2 inhibitors.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Euglycaemic DKA is rare but under-recognised. Exercise-specific advice is expert consensus (grade E).
**Sources:** [S647], [S658]

### Hypoglycaemia mid-session: the 15 g rule
**Rule:** During any session with an insulin or secretagogue user, keep glucose tablets or gel on hand. If the client feels shaky, sweaty, confused or unusually weak, or the CGM reads below 70 mg/dL, stop the session, give 15 g glucose, wait 15 min and recheck, and repeat if still below 70. Resume only light work once glucose is 80-90 mg/dL or higher and stable or rising; if it went below 54 mg/dL, end the session. Never give oral carbohydrate to someone who is not alert: call emergency services. Tachycardia and sweating overlap with normal exercise signs, so check the number rather than guessing.
**Evidence:** ADA 2026 defines level 1 hypoglycaemia as below 70 mg/dL (3.9 mmol/L) and level 2 as below 54 mg/dL (3.0 mmol/L), where neuroglycopenic symptoms begin and immediate action is needed; a measured value below 70 matters regardless of symptoms. Treatment is about 15 g fast-acting carbohydrate (pure glucose preferred), recheck after 15 min, repeat if still low, and call emergency services if the person is not alert. AID-system users typically need 5-10 g, except for exercise-related hypoglycaemia. EASD/ISPAD 2020: suspend exercise below 70 mg/dL, do not restart if glucose fell below 54, restart at about 80-90 mg/dL with a flat or rising trend.
**Grade:** strong
**Applies to:** Adults with diabetes on insulin or secretagogues; adults aged 75 and over have the highest risk.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** EASD/ISPAD restart thresholds are level D. Fat in the treatment food slows correction, and acarbose users must use pure glucose.
**Sources:** [S733], [S732]

### Delayed and nocturnal hypoglycaemia
**Rule:** For insulin users, prefer morning or early-afternoon scheduling for long or hard sessions. After an evening session, the client checks glucose at bedtime, has a carbohydrate- and protein-containing snack if it is in the low-normal range, and sets the CGM low alert to about 80 mg/dL overnight. Mention the 24-48 h window after a new or unusually long session. Insulin reductions belong to the diabetes team.
**Evidence:** Exercise-induced hypoglycaemia typically occurs 6-15 h after exercise and risk can extend to 48 h, higher after evening or late-afternoon and long or high-intensity sessions (ADA 2016; EASD/ISPAD 2020). ADA 2026 says it "may occur and last for several hours due to increased insulin sensitivity". Mitigations include about 20% basal insulin reductions, a bedtime snack, a CGM nocturnal alert at 80 mg/dL (4.4 mmol/L), isCGM scans between 00:00 and 03:00, and avoiding correction boluses close to bedtime.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Mainly T1D, and T2D on insulin or secretagogues. Consensus-level guidance rather than trial outcomes.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Risk depends on fitness, hypoglycaemia awareness, recent hypos and regimen; people who do not usually exercise may be at greater risk.
**Sources:** [S731], [S732], [S658]

### Complications that change the session
**Rule:** At intake, ask diabetic clients about eye disease (retinopathy grade, recent laser or eye surgery) and foot and nerve problems. If proliferative or severe non-proliferative retinopathy is present or unknown, avoid heavy or maximal lifts, overhead lifting, Valsalva, inversions and jumping until an ophthalmologist clears them. A sudden visual change means stop and refer the same day.
**Evidence:** ADA 2026 lists conditions that might contraindicate certain exercise: inadequately managed hypertension, untreated proliferative retinopathy, autonomic neuropathy, orthostatic hypotension, peripheral neuropathy, balance impairment, and history of foot ulcers or Charcot foot; with proliferative or severe non-proliferative retinopathy, vigorous aerobic or resistance exercise "may be contraindicated" (risk of vitreous haemorrhage or retinal detachment). ADA 2016 adds avoiding jumping, jarring, head-down activities and breath-holding, no exercise during vitreous haemorrhage, and avoiding activities that sharply raise BP, such as powerlifting, with moderate non-proliferative retinopathy. ACSM 2022: exercise is contraindicated with unstable or untreated proliferative retinopathy or recent eye surgery or laser; with hypertension avoid heavy lifting and breath-holding and use RPE 10-12 (6-20 scale).
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Adults with T1D or T2D with microvascular complications. Consensus-level guidance rather than trial outcomes.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** ADA says "may be contraindicated" while ACSM says contraindicated for unstable or untreated proliferative retinopathy; the exact restriction is the ophthalmologist's call.
**Sources:** [S658], [S731], [S647]

## Hypertension

### Train it: aerobic and resistance work both lower BP
**Rule:** Hypertension is a reason to train, not to avoid resistance work. Get a recent resting BP and apply the bands in Resting BP thresholds to defer or refer. For controlled hypertension, programme 2-3 days/week of moderate-load resistance work alongside aerobic work, plus optional isometric work (wall squats, handgrip), at moderate effort with continuous breathing. As a practical choice not specified by the sources, use about 8-12 reps stopping 2-3 reps short of failure (about 13-15 on the 6-20 Borg scale), never to failure, starting on machines, and progress load once BP is controlled and breathing technique is solid. Avoid maximal lifts with Valsalva in uncontrolled or complicated hypertension. Medication changes belong to the clinician.
**Evidence:** An umbrella review for the 2018 US PA Guidelines Committee (17 meta-analyses plus 1 systematic review, 594,129 adults) found strong evidence that physical activity lowers BP in normal BP, prehypertension and hypertension and reduces CVD progression in hypertension. A network meta-analysis of 270 RCTs (n=15,827, mixed normotensive and hypertensive) found SBP/DBP reductions of -4.49/-2.53 mmHg for aerobic, -4.55/-3.04 for dynamic resistance, -6.04/-2.54 for combined, -4.08/-2.50 for HIIT and -8.24/-4.00 for isometric training. The AHA 2023 statement says controlled hypertensives can safely do low-to-moderate intensity resistance training with proper breathing; no significant cardiovascular events were reported during RT and 1RM testing in the studies, and RT lowers resting BP by about 6/5 mmHg in hypertension. The ACSM T2D consensus advises people with hypertension to avoid heavy lifting or breath-holding, and the PAR-Q+ flags resting BP of 160/90 or higher, or unknown BP.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Adults with normal BP through to controlled hypertension; trials mostly 2-6 months. The heavy-lifting caution comes from T2D guidance. The evidence that training lowers BP is strong; the grade is moderate because the rule also carries coach-chosen rep, effort and referral numbers.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** The AHA safety data rest on small numbers of events. A 2026 ambulatory-BP NMA (31 RCTs, hypertensive adults) found evidence for dynamic and isometric RT on 24-h BP "remains uncertain"; only aerobic, HIIT and combined training clearly lowered it. Isometric training lowering BP most and guidance against breath-holding are compatible: the warning targets Valsalva and maximal efforts. The BP bands are screening conventions. Expect responders and non-responders.
**Sources:** [S659], [S660], [S647], [S661], [S736], [S737]

### Resting BP thresholds to defer or refer
**Rule:** This is the single place where the coach's resting BP bands are defined; other rules and files point here. Clients with known or suspected hypertension take a seated reading after 5 min rest (properly measured, not a single rushed reading) before sessions while BP is not yet controlled. A band applies when either number reaches it. At 200 systolic or higher, or 110 diastolic or higher: no exercise; re-measure after 5-10 min and, if it stays high, see a doctor promptly; with headache, chest pain, visual change, neurological symptoms or breathlessness, refer urgently. At 180-199 systolic: no training until reviewed by a GP. At 160-179 systolic or 90-109 diastolic, or with BP unknown in someone with known or suspected hypertension: GP review before starting a programme or progressing an existing one, and light-to-moderate work only until BP is controlled. At 140-159 systolic with diastolic below 90: train at moderate intensity and recommend a GP check.
**Evidence:** A peer-reviewed narrative review summarising ACSM's Guidelines for Exercise Testing and Prescription (9th edition, 2013) reports that hypertensive people with resting BP of 180/110 mmHg or higher should see their physician for improved BP control before beginning an exercise programme, "Do not exercise if resting BP 200/110 mm Hg or higher", and BP during exercise should remain at or below 220/105. The 2023 PAR-Q+ hypertension follow-up counts resting BP of 160/90 mmHg or higher, or not knowing one's BP, as YES, which leads to a qualified exercise professional or clinician. ACSM's 2004 position stand says that while evaluation proceeds, most patients can begin moderate-intensity exercise (40 to under 60% VO2R) such as walking. The EAPC position paper uses 140/90 as the threshold for competitive sport and still advises leisure-time moderate aerobic exercise above it. A second review lists uncontrolled severe hypertension (180/110 mmHg or higher) as an absolute contraindication to aerobic and resistance training, attributing it to ACSM, AHA and ACC.
**Grade:** emerging
**Applies to:** Adults with hypertension in a gym; secondary citation of ACSM GETP 9th edition (2013), which was not opened directly; later editions were not checked.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** No trial data define these cut-offs. The 180/110 source wording says to see a physician before beginning a programme; applying "no training until GP review" to people already training is a conservative extension, and ACSM 2004 would allow moderate exercise during the workup. The PAR-Q+ cut-off is 160/90, so diastolic 90-99 falls in the GP-review band here rather than the moderate band. The 220/105 in-exercise ceiling differs from the >240/>110 ceiling in the same review. The 2004 ACSM hypertension position stand contains no resting 200/110 rule, so that figure traces to GETP 9.
**Sources:** [S734], [S641], [S735], [S746], [S783]

### In-session stop criteria
**Rule:** Stop the session immediately for chest pain or pressure, unusual breathlessness, dizziness or near-fainting, palpitations or irregular pulse, severe headache, or visual or neurological symptoms; do not resume that day and get medical clearance before the next session. If BP is measured during exercise, stop or reduce intensity when SBP exceeds about 220-240 or DBP about 105-110, or when SBP falls as workload rises. Include a gradual cool-down (5-10 min) for clients on BP medication and, as a practical extension, avoid abrupt stops and fast standing after floor work.
**Evidence:** AHA 2023: adverse signs or symptoms (dizziness, excessive dyspnoea, chest pain or pressure, palpitations) require immediate medical evaluation and stopping resistance training until cleared; patients on antihypertensives should do extended cool-downs to prevent hypotension. A cardiac-rehab review gives the upper intensity limit as a plateau or fall in SBP, SBP above 240 or DBP above 110, and onset of angina. ACSM 2004: BP stays lowered for up to 22 h after an endurance bout; the usual test-termination limits (above 250/115 mmHg) were set arbitrarily without supporting data, and during endurance training it "appears prudent" to keep BP lower, for example below 220/105.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Adults with hypertension or cardiovascular risk; numeric limits come from clinical testing and rehab, and gyms rarely measure BP during exercise.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Numeric ceilings vary and are expert-based; symptom-based stopping is more practical and better agreed. Cool-down duration and standing-up advice are not specified in the sources.
**Sources:** [S736], [S734], [S735]

### Breathing and load: avoid Valsalva in at-risk clients
**Rule:** For hypertensive, diabetic or cardiovascular-risk clients, avoid deliberate breath-holding and closed-glottis bracing. Cue continuous breathing (exhale through the effort, inhale on the lowering). Regulate effort with RIR/RPE, not %1RM (the coach has no measured 1RM): use loads the client can lift for 8 or more reps and stop sets 2-3 reps short of failure (about 13-15 on the 6-20 Borg scale), never to failure, since the pressor response rises with effort and fatigue. Older adults without symptoms may progress to 1-2 RIR after about 8 weeks (the RIR targets and the 8-week step are coaching conventions shared with the strength file, not from the sources below). Avoid true 1RM testing until BP is well controlled.
**Evidence:** In intra-arterial studies, double-leg press to failure at 80-100% max gave a group mean peak of 320/250 mmHg in 5 bodybuilders (one exceeded 480/350), and single-arm curls reached 255/190, partly due to Valsalva (MacDougall 1985). In 31 healthy men, the BP response depended on degree of effort rather than absolute force, and a brief Valsalva was "unavoidable" above about 80% MVC (MacDougall 1992). In 10 male athletes, leg press at 100% max with closed-glottis Valsalva gave 311/284 mmHg vs 198/175 with slow exhalation (p<0.005), and the pressor response at 85% was also lower with slow exhalation.
**Grade:** emerging
**Applies to:** Mechanistic data from small samples of young, normotensive men; applying it to women, older adults or hypertensive beginners is inference, though it underlies the guideline advice.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** These sources do not link transient peaks to clinical events in controlled hypertensives. For strength athletes, Valsalva is a performance and spinal-stability tool, so this is a precaution for at-risk clients, not a universal rule.
**Sources:** [S738], [S739], [S740]

### Isometric training as an add-on
**Rule:** Offer isometric resistance training as an add-on, not a replacement for aerobic and dynamic training. Typical protocols: handgrip 4 x 2 min at about 30% MVC with 1-3 min rest, or wall squat 4 x 2 min at a sustainable depth with 2 min rest, about 3 times a week. Present it as useful for office BP, especially for clients who cannot or will not do much aerobic work, and do not promise 24-h BP control.
**Evidence:** Isometric training lowers office BP: Hansford 2021 (24 RCTs, n=1143, mean age 56, 56% female) -6.97/-3.86 mmHg; Baffour-Awuah 2023 (12 studies in hypertensives, n=415) -7.47/-3.17; Edwards 2023 NMA -8.24/-4.00, ranking isometric training first for SBP (SUCRA 98.3%) with the wall squat as the top submode. A 2026 handgrip meta-analysis (31 studies, n=905) found -5.38/-2.71 with very high heterogeneity, larger in hypertensives and smaller below 30% MVC. Ambulatory effects are weaker: Hansford found a 24-h DBP reduction (-2.39) but not 24-h SBP (-2.77, CI -6.80 to 1.25); Baffour-Awuah found only a night-time reduction; a 2026 ambulatory NMA judged the evidence "uncertain". Hansford rated certainty very low to low.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Adults (mean age about 50-60) with high-normal BP or hypertension; the handgrip analysis includes normotensives. Trials mostly 12 weeks or shorter.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Office-BP results agree in direction but certainty is low and heterogeneity high; ambulatory findings conflict. The "isometric is best" ranking rests on fewer, smaller trials than aerobic training.
**Sources:** [S741], [S742], [S660], [S743], [S737]

### Isometric training: keep it low-intensity and screened
**Rule:** Keep isometric work low intensity (handgrip about 30% MVC; wall squat at a depth that can be held 2 min without straining), with continuous breathing and no maximal holds. During normal lifts, cue a relaxed grip and no breath-holding. Do not start isometric training with resting BP of 180/110 or higher, uncontrolled grade 2 or higher hypertension, unstable angina or known arrhythmia until medically reviewed.
**Evidence:** Hansford 2021 found no increased adverse-event risk (RR 1.12, 95% CI 0.47-2.68), about 1 adverse event per 38,444 bouts. The Baffour-Awuah 2023 evidence guide reports no related adverse events; at 30% MVC acute BP rises about 20-30 mmHg in normotensives and 40-50 mmHg in hypertensives, much less than with moderate aerobic exercise, while contractions of 50% MVC or more are likely to exceed 200/100 mmHg and should be avoided except in young, healthy people. Its protocols say to breathe normally, set handgrip at 30% MVC ("do not exceed 30% MVC") and wall squat at 15-25% MVC, each 4 x 2 min, 3 times a week, and flag unstable angina, uncontrolled grade II+ hypertension or arrhythmia for clinical evaluation first. A 2022 Delphi study found experts agreed on efficacy but some had safety concerns.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Middle-aged adults with hypertension, mostly medicated or stable; sparse data on very high BP or cardiac disease.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Historic reluctance to use isometrics is resolved by intensity: low-%MVC holds give modest pressor responses, maximal efforts large ones. Adverse-event reporting is often incomplete, and the Delphi panel did not fully agree on safety.
**Sources:** [S744], [S741], [S745]

### Sequencing with high or uncontrolled BP
**Rule:** When BP is elevated but below the no-training bands in Resting BP thresholds, or still being worked up, start with light-to-moderate aerobic work (talk-test pace; RPE about 10-12 on the 6-20 scale, the ACSM T2D figure for hypertension, staying at or below the ACSM moderate band of 12-13, see "ACSM intensity bands" in cardio-health.md) plus light, breathing-controlled resistance work, and progress to heavier dynamic resistance once BP is controlled (a practical inference, not a tested protocol). For time-poor clients, combined aerobic and resistance training is the best-supported choice for 24-h BP.
**Evidence:** The ACSM 2022 T2D consensus advises people with hypertension to avoid heavy lifting and breath-holding and to favour dynamic large-muscle exercise at low to moderate intensity, using RPE 10-12 when maximal HR is unknown. The ACSM 2004 position stand recommends mainly endurance activity (40 to under 60% VO2R, 30 min or more on most, preferably all, days) supplemented by resistance exercise, notes BP stays reduced for up to 22 h after an endurance bout, and says most patients can begin moderate exercise during evaluation. Aerobic, HIIT and combined training clearly reduced 24-h SBP (-4.73, -5.71 and -6.18 mmHg vs control; BJSM 2026 NMA).
**Grade:** emerging
**Applies to:** Adults with hypertension, including T2D. The 2004 position stand predates newer resistance-training data. The sequencing itself is a practical inference, not a tested protocol, hence the emerging grade.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Office-BP meta-analyses rank isometric training first while the ambulatory NMA favours aerobic and combined training; head-to-head comparisons were inconclusive. Preference and adherence also matter.
**Sources:** [S647], [S735], [S737]

### Competitive-sport clearance is not a gym threshold
**Rule:** Do not use the 140/90 competitive-sport cut-off to ban recreational gym training. Without symptoms, resting BP of 140-159 systolic with diastolic below 90 means training continues at moderate intensity with a recommended GP check; higher readings follow the bands in Resting BP thresholds to defer or refer. Hold back maximal efforts, 1RM tests and competitions until BP is controlled.
**Evidence:** The EAPC sports cardiology position paper (2018) sets competitive-sport clearance at BP below 140/90 mmHg (below 140/85 with diabetes), with temporary restriction until BP normalises, while still advising at least 30 min of moderate, mainly aerobic, exercise 5-7 days a week for leisure. Grade 3 hypertension is 180/110 mmHg or higher.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** The source addresses competitive athletes; used here only to separate sport clearance from recreational exercise.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Popular advice that any reading above 140/90 means "don't train" is not what the guidelines say for recreational exercise.
**Sources:** [S746]

## Other medical conditions and scope of practice

### Coach scope vs medical nutrition therapy
**Rule:** Give only general, population-level healthy-eating guidance and nonmedical weight management to healthy adults. If the user reports a diagnosed condition that diet affects (for example CVD, dyslipidaemia, IBS/IBD/coeliac, food allergy, PCOS, thyroid, liver or kidney disease, cancer, osteoporosis, an eating disorder, or several medications), do not write a therapeutic diet: give general principles only, say a registered dietitian or the treating doctor should set the plan, and offer to coordinate training around their advice.
**Evidence:** In a survey of 286 Australian registered exercise professionals, 88% were working outside their scope of practice by giving individual nutrition advice to clients across fitness and medical issues; the authors recommend that the nutrition training of exercise professionals be limited to general non-medical nutrition information in line with national guidelines, with onward referral to other health professionals for issues beyond that scope. A client survey found that client expectations may push personal trainers beyond the recommended scope and called for awareness of that scope. North Dakota law defines general nonmedical nutrition information (principles of good nutrition, menu planning, essential nutrients and amounts, food sources) and treats "medical weight control" as MNT but "nonmedical weight control" for healthy groups as not.
**Grade:** emerging
**Applies to:** Non-licensed coaches working with adults; the legal boundary differs by country and US state. The surveys are Australian and their recommendations are the authors', not a professional-body statement.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** No formal ACSM, ISSN, Academy, BDA or Dietitians of Canada scope-of-practice statement was found or opened, so the referral list (diagnosed conditions that diet affects) is a conservative coaching convention built on the general-versus-medical split. Some practitioner sources are stricter. PCOS, thyroid, liver, cancer, allergy and polypharmacy fall under the generic wording; no source names them.
**Sources:** [S796], [S797], [S664]

### What a dietitian referral adds
**Rule:** Recommend a dietitian for scope and safety (disease-specific MNT, medication interactions, malnutrition, eating disorders, dyslipidaemia), not because it guarantees large extra weight loss: the average extra benefit was about 1 kg. For healthy adults with general goals, coach-delivered general guidance is a reasonable default (an inference these trials did not test).
**Evidence:** In RCTs of individual dietitian care for adult weight management vs minimal or usual care, participants lost an additional 1.03 kg (95% CI 0.66-1.40; 5 studies, n=1,598) and reduced BMI by an additional 0.43 kg/m2 (95% CI 0.26-0.59; 4 studies, n=1,224). In high-risk cardiovascular patients in primary care (10 RCTs, n=1,530), dietitian care lowered triglycerides more than comparators (6 studies) but showed no significant difference for total and LDL cholesterol (7 studies); both groups improved.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Adults in weight-management trials and high-risk primary-care patients; risk of bias unclear to high, few studies pooled.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Effects are modest and imprecise. Both reviews come from one group largely of accredited dietitians. Benefit may be larger in complex conditions not captured here.
**Sources:** [S679], [S680]

### Malnutrition and unintended weight loss: no deficit, refer
**Rule:** Before setting any deficit, ask about weight change, appetite and intake over the last 3-6 months. Do not prescribe a deficit or weight-loss goal, and suggest a GP or dietitian review, if: BMI is under 18.5 (under 20 if aged 70 or over); BMI is under 20 (under 22 if over 70) with any recent unintended loss; or unintentional loss exceeds 5% in the past 6 months. Weight lost through planned dieting the user chose is not unintentional; loss with poor appetite, illness or a much lower intake is, even if the user was also dieting.
**Evidence:** GLIM (2019) diagnoses malnutrition from at least 1 phenotypic and 1 aetiologic criterion after screening: weight loss over 5% within 6 months or over 10% beyond 6 months; BMI under 20 (under 70 years) or under 22 (over 70), with Asian cut-offs of 18.5 and 20. Severe is loss over 10% within 6 months or over 20% beyond 6 months, or BMI under 18.5 (under 70) or under 20 (70 and over). The 2025 GLIM update suggests no revision of these criteria and cites 10 systematic reviews indicating strong validity. NICE CG32 says to consider nutrition support with BMI under 18.5, unintentional loss over 10% in 3-6 months, or BMI under 20 with loss over 5% in 3-6 months, and that screening should assess BMI and percentage unintentional loss (MUST may be used).
**Grade:** strong
**Applies to:** Adults in clinical and community settings. Thresholds are for clinicians; coaches use the phenotypic thresholds only as referral triggers, never to diagnose.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** GLIM uses over 5% in 6 months; NICE uses over 10% in 3-6 months or over 5% with BMI under 20; use the more sensitive one. BMI cut-offs are lower for Asian populations, and low muscle mass can coexist with normal or high BMI. GLIM writes the age cut as ">70" in one table and "70 or over" in another.
**Sources:** [S665], [S666], [S667]

### Older adults: muscle before weight loss
**Rule:** For users aged about 65 and over, screen weight history, appetite and intake at intake using the malnutrition triggers above. Prioritise resistance training and adequate protein (at least 1.0-1.2 g/kg/day, 1.2-1.6 g/kg/day when lifting, as in the nutrition file) over weight loss. Do not set a deficit unless obesity with a weight-related health problem is present, and then use only a modest deficit paired with resistance training and that protein intake. Refer to a GP or dietitian if malnutrition triggers are met, appetite is poor, or the user has several chronic diseases or medications.
**Evidence:** ESPEN (2022, 82 recommendations) recommends routine malnutrition screening of all older persons, says dietary restrictions should generally be avoided, that weight-reducing diets should be considered only in obese older persons with weight-related health problems and only combined with exercise, and that interventions should be individualised and multidisciplinary. PROT-AGE recommends at least 1.0-1.2 g/kg/day of protein for people over 65 and at least 1.2 g/kg/day for those who exercise or are active.
**Grade:** strong
**Applies to:** Older adults (generally 65 and over).
**Individual variation / conflicts:** For sarcopenia criteria see "Older adults: signs of probable sarcopenia"; MNA cut-offs were not opened. Decisions depend on frailty, comorbidity and goals. The 1.6 g/kg upper end for lifters is the nutrition file's range, not a PROT-AGE figure; kidney disease overrides these protein targets (next rule).
**Sources:** [S668], [S192]

### Older adults: signs of probable sarcopenia
**Rule:** For clients 65 and over, suggest a clinician assessment for sarcopenia if grip strength is below 27 kg (men) or 16 kg (women), five chair rises take more than 15 s, or they report falls, feeling weak, slow walking, trouble rising from a chair or unintended weight loss. Keep them training with progressive resistance work and adequate protein meanwhile. Do not diagnose sarcopenia.
**Evidence:** EWGSOP2 (2019): low strength (grip below 27 kg in men and 16 kg in women, or a chair-stand time over 15 s for five rises) identifies probable sarcopenia; confirmation needs low muscle quantity (appendicular skeletal muscle mass below 20 kg in men and 15 kg in women), and low physical performance (gait speed of 0.8 m/s or less, SPPB of 8 or less) indicates severity. Case finding starts when a person reports falls, feeling weak, slow walking, difficulty rising from a chair or weight loss.
**Grade:** strong
**Applies to:** Older adults; European consensus definition. A grip test needs a dynamometer, and coach measurements are screening prompts, not clinical tests.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Other groups (FNIH, AWGS) use different cut-offs. MNA-SF cut-offs were not confirmed as referral triggers in any opened source.
**Sources:** [S814]

### Chronic kidney disease: protein targets from the renal team
**Rule:** If a user reports CKD, reduced eGFR or proteinuria, do not apply standard high-protein muscle-building targets (such as 1.6-2.2 g/kg). Protein, sodium, potassium and phosphorus targets come from their nephrologist or a renal dietitian; until they set one, do not raise protein above general-population intake (about 0.8 g/kg/day) without clinician approval. As a conservative precaution, do not recommend NSAIDs, creatine or other supplements without nephrology approval. Encourage 150 min/week of moderate activity within their tolerance.
**Evidence:** KDIGO 2024 recommends moderate-intensity activity for a cumulative 150 min/week or more, or as tolerated (1D), and avoiding sedentary behaviour; protein of 0.8 g/kg/day in CKD G3-G5 (2C); avoiding high protein (over 1.3 g/kg/day) in adults at risk of progression (practice point); renal dietitians to tailor sodium, phosphorus, potassium and protein; sodium under 2 g/day (2C); and higher protein and calorie targets for frail or sarcopenic older adults. KDIGO calls for medication review and lists NSAIDs among drugs to review or avoid. KDOQI 2020 frames CKD nutrition as medical nutrition therapy.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Adults with CKD; protein recommendations target G3-G5 and people at risk of progression.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Protein (2C) and activity (1D) recommendations are low certainty; the 1.3 g/kg cap is an ungraded practice point and is context only, not a coach rule. The creatine caution is a precaution, not from KDIGO.
**Sources:** [S366], [S662]

### GLP-1 receptor agonist users
**Rule:** If a user is on a GLP-1 or dual agonist, coordinate with the prescriber, and recommend a registered dietitian especially with very low intake, significant GI symptoms or eating-disorder history. Ask about eating-disorder history before starting. Focus on strength training at least 3 times a week plus at least 150 min/week of moderate aerobic activity, as tolerated. Use protein-forward meals (about 1.2-1.6 g/kg/day unless a clinician says otherwise; never below about 0.5 g/kg/day), fluids and fibre. Coach inference, not stated in the sources: do not add a coach-imposed calorie deficit on top of the drug-induced appetite reduction; the prescribing clinician owns the weight plan, and this overrides the general deficit rules. The coach's job is resistance training, protein and red flags.
**Evidence:** A 2025 joint advisory (ACLM, ASN, OMA, TOS) reports placebo-adjusted weight reductions of 5-18% with GLP-1-based drugs, with GI side effects, nutritional deficiencies, muscle and bone loss, and regain after stopping (up to two-thirds within 1 year). In STEP 1, 5.3 kg (38%) of the 13.6 kg lost was lean mass, estimated as about 20% muscle; modelling suggests 10-15% of weight lost is muscle in women and 20-25% in men without structured strength training. The advisory recommends baseline screening for eating disorders and sarcopenia/osteopenia, a structured exercise programme (strength training at least 3 times weekly plus 150 min/week moderate aerobic), and dietitian counselling as support; protein of 1.2-1.6 g/kg/day has been proposed during active loss, not below 0.4-0.5 g/kg/day. A rapid review (25 studies, narrative) found limited data; for most people eating behaviours may improve or stay unchanged.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Adults with obesity (or overweight with complications) on GLP-1-based therapy. The advisory is an expert appraisal, not a systematic review; the rapid review also included adolescents and people with T2D.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Unclear whether protein targets should use actual, adjusted or fat-free mass. Evidence on ED risk is limited (emerging); GLP-1s "could theoretically reduce or exacerbate" ED symptoms. Several authors disclose industry ties; an erratum was published in 2026.
**Sources:** [S361], [S669]

### Bariatric surgery patients
**Rule:** For any user who has had or is planning bariatric surgery, the bariatric team or its dietitian sets the nutrition plan, including any staged diet, protein targets and supplements. The coach supports training and adherence to that plan and does not prescribe diets or supplements.
**Evidence:** The 2019 AACE/TOS/ASMBS/OMA/ASA bariatric guideline (85 numbered recommendations) says a team approach to perioperative care is mandatory, with special attention to nutritional and metabolic issues.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Adults before or after bariatric procedures.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Only the abstract was opened, so no specific diet-stage or micronutrient content is claimed. Of its recommendation statements, 101 (41%) are Grade D (expert opinion) and only 31 (13%) Grade A.
**Sources:** [S670]

## Eating disorders and low energy availability

### Screening: brief questions as a prompt, not a diagnosis
**Rule:** Do not run a diagnostic eating-disorder screen or label users. At intake, ask brief SCOFF-style questions (making yourself sick, loss of control over eating, recent large weight loss of about one stone or 6 kg in 3 months, believing you are fat when others say you are thin, food dominating life; the item wording is the standard SCOFF and is not taken from the opened sources). There is no mandatory universal screening tool; these questions are a prompt, never a diagnosis. Any positive answer, or any warning sign (next rule), means the coach sets no calorie deficit, no weight targets and no weigh-ins, and gently recommends a GP or eating-disorder professional (see How to raise a concern). Never tell a user they do or do not have an ED based on the answers, and do not treat a negative screen as reassurance, especially in men or when binge eating is suspected. If the PAR-Q+ reveals a diagnosed ED, the same applies and the treating team leads.
**Evidence:** USPSTF (2022, I statement) found insufficient evidence to judge the benefits and harms of screening asymptomatic people aged 10 and over (normal or high BMI) for eating disorders; its red-flag list, which excludes people from "asymptomatic" status, includes rapid weight loss or gain, bradycardia, oligomenorrhoea and amenorrhoea. Its evidence review found SCOFF sensitivity 84% and specificity 80% in adults (cut-point 2 or more, 10 studies, n=3,684) and no studies directly assessing benefits or harms of screening. A meta-analysis of 25 studies gave SCOFF pooled sensitivity 0.86 (95% CI 0.78-0.91) and specificity 0.83 (0.77-0.88), best in young women with anorexia or bulimia and lower in men, binge-eating disorder and community samples, with insufficient evidence to use it across all DSM-5 eating disorders. NICE says not to use screening tools such as SCOFF as the sole way to decide whether someone has an eating disorder. Lifetime prevalence in adult women is 1.42% for anorexia, 0.46% for bulimia and 1.25% for binge-eating disorder, and lower in men (0.12%, 0.08% and 0.42%). Exercise addiction is about 3.7 times more common in people with indicated eating disorders (OR 3.71, 95% CI 2.00-6.89; 9 studies, n=2140, mean age 25, 70.6% female).
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Adolescents and adults. SCOFF evidence comes mostly from young women; male, muscle-focused and binge-eating presentations are under-detected.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** "Screen everyone with SCOFF" outruns the evidence: USPSTF finds it insufficient and there is no direct outcome evidence for screening. False negatives are more likely in men, binge-eating disorder and community samples such as gym members. The exercise-addiction meta-analysis has high heterogeneity (I2=81%). Acting on any single positive answer is stricter than the usual SCOFF cut-off of 2 or more; that is a precautionary product policy, as is withholding deficits, weight targets and weigh-ins. The underlying sources were rated moderate and strong; the lower grade is kept for the merged rule.
**Sources:** [S656], [S350], [S657], [S349], [S671]

### Warning signs that warrant referral
**Rule:** Treat any of the following as a trigger to refer the user to a GP or eating-disorder service: vomiting, laxatives, diuretics or diet pills used to control weight; exercise to "compensate" for food, or distress when unable to train; rigid food rules or growing "clean eating" rigidity; binge episodes with loss of control; missed periods; fainting, dizziness or palpitations; rapid weight change; or marked fear of weight gain. For male users, also ask about excessive training and anabolic steroid use. If any appear, set no calorie deficit, weight targets, body-composition goals or weigh-ins (the same rule as a positive screen); pausing weight-loss programming while the concern is open is a precautionary product policy, not a recommendation stated in these sources.
**Evidence:** NICE NG69 (2017, updated 2020), rec. 1.2.6, lists factors to consider when assessing or referring: unusually low or high BMI or weight for age; rapid weight loss; worrying dieting or restrictive eating (such as dieting when underweight); social withdrawal around food; disproportionate concern with weight or shape; menstrual or other endocrine disturbance; signs of malnutrition (poor circulation, dizziness, palpitations, fainting, pallor); compensatory behaviours (laxatives, diet pills, vomiting, excessive exercise); unexplained electrolyte imbalance or hypoglycaemia; atypical dental wear; and high-risk activities (professional sport, fashion, dance, modelling). Rec. 1.2.10: if an eating disorder is suspected after initial assessment, refer immediately to a community-based, age-appropriate service. The AED Guide to Medical Care (2021) adds rigid exercise patterns, a growing focus on "healthy" or "clean" eating, cold intolerance, bradycardia, amenorrhoea or oligomenorrhoea, stress fractures and rigidity around social engagements and mealtimes, and says the time to worry is "as soon as you suspect an eating disorder".
**Grade:** strong
**Applies to:** All ages and sexes. NG69 is a UK clinical guideline, the AED guide international clinician guidance, MEED UK college guidance; coaches use them as recognition and referral triggers, not to diagnose.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** No single sign is diagnostic, and a life-threatening eating disorder may occur without obvious physical signs. MEED advises asking males specifically about excessive training and anabolic or androgenic steroid misuse.
**Sources:** [S671], [S672], [S673]

### Do not judge risk by appearance or BMI
**Rule:** Never dismiss a concern because the user "looks healthy", is in a normal or high BMI range, or is very fit. A user with obesity who loses weight very fast through restriction or purging is also a referral case.
**Evidence:** NG69 says not to use single measures such as BMI or duration of illness to decide whether to offer treatment. The AED says most people with eating disorders are not visibly underweight, people at any weight may be malnourished, and normal lab results do not exclude a serious eating disorder. MEED notes bulimia nervosa can cause life-threatening emergencies without underweight, that its "green" BMI band does not denote a healthy weight, and that rapid weight loss at any weight, including in obesity, is a risk marker.
**Grade:** strong
**Applies to:** All adults and adolescents.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** None noted.
**Sources:** [S671], [S672], [S673]

### Urgent medical red flags
**Rule:** Tell the user to seek same-day urgent medical care, and stop the session and the plan, if they report any of: fainting or near-fainting; chest pain or palpitations; confusion; eating almost nothing (under about 500 kcal/day) for 2 or more days; vomiting several times a day; a resting HR under 40; or losing 1 kg or more per week while already thin. With symptoms, direct them to the emergency department or emergency services. Do not continue coaching until they are medically cleared.
**Evidence:** The MEED framework (RCPsych CR233, May 2022) rates adult findings red, amber or green. Red (high impending risk to life) includes weight loss of 1 kg/week or more for 2 consecutive weeks in an undernourished person, or rapid loss at any weight; BMI under 13 (amber 13-14.9); awake HR under 40 (amber 40-50); standing systolic BP under 90 with recurrent syncope and a postural drop over 20 mmHg or HR rise over 30; temperature under 35.5 C tympanic or 35.0 C axillary (amber under 36 C); potassium under 2.5 mmol/L; glucose under 3 mmol/L; acute food refusal or intake under 500 kcal/day for 2 or more days; multiple daily vomiting or laxative abuse (amber 3 or more times a week); and dysfunctional exercise over 2 h/day in the context of malnutrition (amber over 1 h/day). One or more red or two or more amber ratings should probably be considered high risk. People may appear very energetic right up to collapse, and should not be reassured that their risk is low.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Adults (18 and over); children and young people need age- and sex-adjusted parameters. The framework supports clinical judgement and is not a standalone tool.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** No single parameter is an adequate risk indicator. Thresholds are UK expert consensus; other bodies use different cut-offs (SAHM uses daytime HR under 50 as a hospitalisation criterion). Coaches cannot measure most items and should act on reported symptoms.
**Sources:** [S673], [S671]

### A low resting heart rate is not automatically fitness
**Rule:** If a user's resting HR (from a wearable) is under 50 and they also restrict food, have lost weight quickly, or show other ED signs, recommend medical review within days. If it is under 40, or they have symptoms (dizziness, fainting), recommend same-day care. Never praise a low resting HR as fitness in that context.
**Evidence:** The AED states bradycardia is the most common arrhythmia in eating disorders and should not be automatically attributed to athleticism in people who are underweight or have lost weight rapidly. SAHM 2022 lists severe bradycardia (under 50 bpm daytime, under 45 at night) among factors supporting hospitalisation of adolescents and young adults with eating disorders. MEED rates an awake adult HR under 40 as red and 40-50 as amber.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Adolescents, young adults and adults with suspected restrictive eating. A low HR in a well-fed, weight-stable endurance-trained person without other signs is a different situation.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Thresholds differ by body and purpose (SAHM hospitalisation criterion vs MEED adult risk rating); context decides.
**Sources:** [S672], [S674], [S673]

### Adolescents: no deficits
**Rule:** Product policy based on the cited guidance (not a direct recommendation of these sources): under-18s are out of scope of this template; refer them to a paediatric professional (paediatrician, GP or paediatric dietitian). The coach sets no calorie deficits, weight-loss targets, body-composition goals, weigh-ins or calorie tracking for them, gives general healthy-eating and training guidance only, and involves a parent or guardian where appropriate.
**Evidence:** NG69 says eating-disorder risk is highest between ages 13 and 17, and faltering growth and delayed puberty may be presenting features. MEED says adult parameters cannot be applied to young people without adjusting for age and sex, using %median BMI (under 70% rated red). SAHM notes complications of atypical anorexia nervosa and ARFID can be as severe as anorexia nervosa, and that boys and men with restrictive eating disorders are often underdiagnosed.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Adolescents (outside the core adult population). Supervised weight management for adolescents with obesity belongs to paediatric clinicians.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** The no-deficit rule for minors is a conservative product choice, not a guideline recommendation; paediatric obesity services do use supervised weight management.
**Sources:** [S671], [S674], [S673]

### Missed periods and low energy availability
**Rule:** If a menstruating user reports any new missed or irregular period during dieting or heavy training (outside pregnancy or hormonal contraception effects), or a man reports low libido with heavy training and dieting, ask about intake, recommend a GP visit and stop increasing the deficit. If periods have been absent for 3 months or more, or there are fewer than 6 a year, stop the deficit, raise intake towards maintenance and refer to a GP (and a sports dietitian). Do not treat amenorrhoea as a normal side effect of getting lean.
**Evidence:** The Endocrine Society guideline describes functional hypothalamic amenorrhoea as chronic anovulation linked to stress, weight loss, excessive exercise or a combination; it is a diagnosis of exclusion needing medical work-up and multidisciplinary care, with complications including bone loss and infertility. The 2023 IOC consensus frames low energy availability as causing health and performance harms (REDs) in women and men. The AED notes REDs can overlap with or evolve into an eating disorder; if intake does not rise after education, or exercise is compulsive, an eating disorder should be strongly considered. The 2025 Female Athlete Triad update rates no menses for more than 90 days, or fewer than 6 in 12 months, as high risk.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Recreationally active women and men; REDs evidence comes mostly from athletes.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** REDs as a syndrome is debated (for example a 2024 Sports Med critique, not opened). The IOC tools were built for athletes. The two-tier response (act on any new change, stop the deficit at 3 months or fewer than 6 periods a year) is a product policy built on these risk-stratification cut-offs, not a diagnostic threshold.
**Sources:** [S675], [S336], [S672], [S348]

### REDs: big deficit plus high training volume
**Rule:** When a user combines a sizeable calorie deficit with high training volume, watch for persistent fatigue, falling performance, frequent illness, bone or stress-fracture pain, menstrual changes (women) or low libido (men), and mood changes. If several are present, reduce the deficit or training load and refer to a sports physician or registered dietitian. Diagnosing REDs requires clinical assessment, not a coach.
**Evidence:** The IOC 2023 consensus defines REDs as a syndrome of health and performance impairments in female and male athletes caused by low energy availability (intake inadequate for exercise expenditure), with newer data pointing to a role for low carbohydrate availability and mental health; it introduced the REDs Clinical Assessment Tool v2. A meta-analysis found low energy availability in 44.7% of athletes (44.2% female, 49.4% male; 46 studies, n=6118) and REDs risk in 63% (8 studies, n=730); LEA was associated with reduced performance and more illness-related training absence, and most studies concluded it impairs bone health and raises bone-stress-injury risk.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Athletes, largely endurance, aesthetic and weight-class sports; generalising to recreational gym-goers in an aggressive cut is plausible but unquantified.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Prevalence depends on measurement method; male data are newer and thinner; results on general injury risk are mixed; data on non-athletes are lacking.
**Sources:** [S336], [S347]

### Compulsive or driven exercise
**Rule:** Ask about and watch for exercise driven by guilt or "earning" or "burning off" food, distress when a session is missed, training through injury or illness, and rising volume during a deficit. If present, do not add volume or cardio for fat loss; discuss it openly and refer if it persists or comes with restriction.
**Evidence:** A meta-analysis of 66 studies (135 effect sizes, N=21,816) found self-reported compulsive exercise is associated with eating-disorder symptoms (overall r=0.35, dietary restraint r=0.42, bulimic symptoms r=0.19), more strongly in clinical, younger and thinner samples, and for restraint in female samples. MEED treats dysfunctional exercise as a compensatory behaviour (red over 2 h/day, amber over 1 h/day in the context of malnutrition), says males should be asked about excess training and anabolic steroid use, and that patients may appear very energetic right up to collapse.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Adults and adolescents, female and male; mostly cross-sectional data. MEED thresholds are for people with eating disorders and malnutrition, not healthy gym-goers.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Causality is unknown. High training volume alone is not pathological; motive and rigidity matter. Do not apply the MEED hour thresholds to well-nourished trainees.
**Sources:** [S676], [S673]

### Calorie and weight tracking: depends on the user
**Rule:** Calorie or macro tracking and regular weigh-ins are acceptable options for low-risk adults, not requirements; offer non-tracking alternatives (portion guides, habit goals). Do not prescribe calorie counting, weigh-ins of any frequency, body measurements or progress photos to anyone with a current or past ED, a positive screen, a warning sign, compensatory behaviours or distress around numbers, and stop them if distress appears.
**Evidence:** A 2024 meta-analysis (28 studies, n=17,370; 9 meta-analysed, n=13,507) found no significant association between weight-related self-monitoring and global eating-disorder scores in general-population adults (r=0.13, 95% CI -0.02 to 0.28, p=0.08). An RCT (n=200 low-risk undergraduate women) found about one month of MyFitnessPal use did not change ED risk, anxiety, depression or body satisfaction, possibly because of the low-risk sample. In a cross-sectional survey of 105 people diagnosed with an eating disorder, about 75% used MyFitnessPal and 73% of those felt it contributed to their disorder.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Adults; null data from low-risk samples, harm signal from a clinical sample (self-perceived, cross-sectional).
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Claims that tracking causes eating disorders outrun the evidence for the general population, while clinical samples report perceived harm. No opened source tested body-check or photo prompts; avoiding them in at-risk users is precautionary.
**Sources:** [S353], [S356], [S358]

### Structured weight management rarely triggers EDs, but monitor
**Rule:** A moderate, structured fat-loss plan is reasonable for adults with overweight or obesity. Screen at intake (ED history, binge eating, compensatory behaviours) and re-check every few weeks for new rigid rules, bingeing, purging or distress, and switch to referral if they appear. This evidence does not support aggressive deficits.
**Evidence:** A meta-analysis of 49 behavioural weight-management trials (n=6,337 adults with overweight or obesity) found eating-disorder symptoms fell on average (global g=-0.27, binge eating g=-0.66, maintained at follow-up), but 4 studies reported new symptoms in 0-6.5% of participants, and the authors advise monitoring.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Adults with overweight or obesity (81% female) in supervised, structured programmes of 4 weeks to 18 months.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** High heterogeneity (I2 67-83%); within-group changes; structured programmes differ from self-directed aggressive dieting; some authors have commercial ties.
**Sources:** [S677]

### How to raise a concern
**Rule:** When a red flag appears, say privately and calmly what you have noticed (one or two specific observations), express care, ask how they are feeling, and avoid comments about body, weight or food "goodness" (the private, calm setting and the one-or-two-observations format are coaching conventions, not tested). Then suggest a GP or eating-disorder service and give helpline details (for example Beat in the UK, ANAD in the US). If they deny it, pause weight-focused programming anyway and offer to revisit. If any urgent red flag is present, prioritise safety.
**Evidence:** NICE NG69 notes that people may find it difficult or distressing to discuss an eating problem and may feel stigma and shame, says eating disorders should be assessed and treated at the earliest opportunity, warns that screening tools such as SCOFF should not be the sole basis for deciding, and lists signs that should prompt referral, including rapid weight loss, restrictive eating, withdrawal from social situations involving food, disproportionate concern about weight or shape and compensatory behaviours such as excessive exercise. An uncontrolled evaluation of an online course for 150 coaches, trainers and fitness instructors found large gains in confidence and knowledge about warning signs and how to approach someone at risk; the course teaches communicating without negative comments on weight, shape or food, which was not itself tested. The AED adds that denial should not stop consideration of a possible eating disorder.
**Grade:** emerging
**Applies to:** Adults and adolescents; guidance for non-clinicians, adapted to coaching.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** NG69 is written for healthcare professionals, not coaches; the course study had no control group and two of its authors work for the organisation that developed it. No peer-reviewed source tested how a coach should word the conversation; language may need adapting to culture and preference.
**Sources:** [S671], [S798], [S672]

### Diabetes or pregnancy combined with an eating disorder
**Rule:** For users with diabetes or coeliac disease who show ED signs (including skipping or reducing insulin to control weight), and for pregnant or postpartum users with a current or past eating disorder: no weight-loss coaching. Refer them to their diabetes or maternity team and a dietitian, and limit coaching to activity those teams have approved. This is a cautious extrapolation; NICE addresses clinicians, not coaches.
**Evidence:** NICE NG69 lists problems managing a chronic illness that affects diet, such as diabetes or coeliac disease, among factors to consider when assessing or referring. For an eating disorder with diabetes it advises a low threshold for glucose and ketone monitoring and addressing insulin misuse. For women with an eating disorder planning to conceive, it advises education including stopping binge eating, vomiting, laxatives and excessive exercise, a dedicated professional to support them through pregnancy and the postnatal period, and considering more intensive prenatal care for current or remitted anorexia nervosa.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** People with diabetes or coeliac disease; pregnant and postnatal women with a current or past eating disorder. UK guideline for healthcare professionals.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Single-guideline recommendations relying largely on committee consensus. No opened source gave pregnancy nutrition-referral criteria outside the ED context.
**Sources:** [S671]

## Pregnancy

### Default is to be active
**Rule:** If a user is pregnant, tell her to clear her plan with her obstetric care provider. Once cleared, aim for at least 150 min/week of moderate activity across 3 or more days, mixing aerobic and resistance work. Previously active women can keep familiar activities, including vigorous ones they already did. As a coaching precaution, progress new activities gradually rather than introducing novel high-intensity work. Switch from supine positions if symptomatic. Never encourage weight loss during pregnancy.
**Evidence:** The 2019 SOGC/CSEP guideline (GRADE methods) recommends that all pregnant women without contraindications be active throughout pregnancy, accumulating at least 150 min/week of moderate activity over at least 3 days, with a variety of aerobic and resistance training; pelvic-floor training may be done daily, and women who feel unwell flat on their back should avoid the supine position. A meta-analysis of 46 studies found very low quality evidence of no increased odds of miscarriage (OR 0.88, 95% CI 0.63-1.21) or perinatal mortality (OR 0.86, 0.49-1.52), with no association with exercise volume, intensity or frequency, though most studies examined moderate intensity up to 60 min. ACOG (CO 804) states activity in uncomplicated pregnancy is safe and desirable and that previously active women can continue. WHO 2020 includes recommendations for pregnant and postpartum women.
**Grade:** strong
**Applies to:** Pregnant women without contraindications; sparse data on very high volumes, durations over 60 min or elite training.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Safety evidence on fetal death is very low quality; guidelines agree in direction; individual obstetric history governs everything.
**Sources:** [S652], [S653], [S654], [S016]

### Contraindications: the obstetric provider decides
**Rule:** Ask pregnant users whether their obstetric provider has flagged any complication. With any absolute contraindication, provide no exercise programming beyond daily living activity. With a relative one, programme only what the obstetric provider has explicitly approved. Bleeding, fluid leakage, contractions, chest pain, dizziness or reduced fetal movement mean stop and contact the obstetric team. The coach never makes the call.
**Evidence:** The Canadian guideline lists absolute contraindications (ruptured membranes, premature labour, unexplained persistent vaginal bleeding, placenta praevia after 28 weeks, pre-eclampsia, incompetent cervix, intrauterine growth restriction, high-order multiple pregnancy, uncontrolled type 1 diabetes, hypertension or thyroid disease, other serious cardiovascular, respiratory or systemic disorders), with daily living activities still allowed, and relative ones (recurrent pregnancy loss, gestational hypertension, history of spontaneous preterm birth, mild/moderate cardiovascular or respiratory disease, symptomatic anaemia, malnutrition, eating disorder, twin pregnancy after 28 weeks, other significant conditions). A 2020 systematic review (44 studies) found most rest on expert opinion with minimal evidence of harm, identified 11 conditions (such as gestational hypertension and twins) where activity may help, and 8 with strong potential for harm: severe cardiorespiratory disease, placental abruption, vasa praevia, uncontrolled type 1 diabetes, IUGR, active preterm labour, severe pre-eclampsia, cervical insufficiency.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Pregnant women; lists are consensus-based.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Open conflict between guideline lists and Meah 2020, which argues several "contraindications" may not warrant restriction; individual obstetric judgement resolves it.
**Sources:** [S652], [S655]

### Warning signs to stop exercise
**Rule:** Before every session with a pregnant client, ask about bleeding, fluid leakage, contractions, headache, dizziness, chest pain, calf pain or swelling, unusual breathlessness at rest and (later in pregnancy) whether the baby is moving as much as usual. If any appear before or during exercise, end the session and tell her to contact her midwife or obstetrician the same day. For bleeding, fluid gushing, chest pain, regular painful contractions or a swollen, painful calf, tell her to seek urgent care. Never coach through these symptoms.
**Evidence:** ACOG patient guidance (from CO 804) lists vaginal bleeding, feeling dizzy or faint, shortness of breath before starting exercise, chest pain, headache, muscle weakness, calf pain or swelling, regular painful contractions, and fluid gushing or leaking. Of 11 national guidelines from 9 countries, 6 gave stop criteria: all included dizziness or presyncope, dyspnoea before exertion and vaginal bleeding; 5 included abdominal or back pain, amniotic fluid leakage and excessive breathlessness; 4 included decreased fetal movement, which the ACOG patient list omits. An ACSM Exercise is Medicine patient handout (2024) gives the same core list: bleeding or fluid from the vagina, chest pain, dizziness or faintness or breathlessness that does not settle with rest, regular painful contractions, and calf pain or swelling.
**Grade:** strong
**Applies to:** All pregnant clients; lists come from expert consensus. The coach screens and refers.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Lists differ slightly between guidelines, and the comparison covers guidelines up to 2012. When unsure, treat a symptom as a stop signal.
**Sources:** [S707], [S708], [S813]

### Keep resistance training in
**Rule:** Keep resistance training in the programme throughout an uncomplicated pregnancy, alongside aerobic work, aiming for at least 150 min/week of moderate activity in total. For beginners, start with light-to-moderate loads and progress gradually. Adjust exercise choice and position as pregnancy advances, but do not drop strength training by default.
**Evidence:** A 2025 systematic review and meta-analysis (50 studies, 47,619 participants, 45 RCTs) found resistance training, alone or in multicomponent programmes, was associated with lower odds of gestational hypertension (OR 0.42, 95% CI 0.27-0.66), gestational diabetes (OR 0.62, 0.48-0.79), perinatal mood disorders (OR 0.48, 0.32-0.73) and macrosomia (OR 0.67, 0.50-0.88); 90% were multicomponent programmes with low-to-moderate doses. SOGC/CSEP 2019 and WHO 2020 both recommend combining aerobic and muscle-strengthening activity, at least 150 min/week of moderate activity (SOGC/CSEP over 3 or more days).
**Grade:** strong
**Applies to:** Healthy pregnant women without contraindications, beginners to recreationally trained; mostly supervised low-to-moderate dose programmes.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Guideline agreement is strong; the specific outcome benefits rest on one meta-analysis (moderate on its own), and the share due to resistance training itself is unclear. Fatigue and nausea, especially in the first trimester, may mean doing less for a time.
**Sources:** [S709], [S652], [S016]

### Heavy lifting in pregnancy for experienced lifters
**Rule:** For an experienced lifter with an uncomplicated pregnancy and provider approval, heavy lifting can continue; do not ban it by default. Reduce load or change the exercise when technique, comfort, balance, pelvic-floor symptoms (leaking, heaviness) or any warning sign appear, and use RPE or reps-in-reserve rather than chasing 1RM numbers. Do not introduce heavy or Olympic lifting for the first time to beginners during pregnancy; keep them on moderate loads with controlled technique.
**Evidence:** In an online survey of 679 people who lifted 80% 1RM or more during pregnancy (mainly recreational athletes; 61% CrossFit), 66% of respondents (388/589) reported no pregnancy or delivery complications, and outcomes did not differ with Olympic lifts, Valsalva or supine lifting; 57% (241/426) reported urinary incontinence after pregnancy, and those who kept their pre-pregnancy level until delivery reported fewer reproductive complications (p=0.006). A 2026 retrospective survey of 359 first-trimester resistance trainers found frequency fell from 4.8 to 4.0 days a week; 80% (127/159) of Olympic lifters continued, 62% (126/203) maintained or increased load, loads stayed above 75% of estimated 1RM in 41 with logs, fatigue and nausea affected training in 77%, and 3% (10) reported a miscarriage during enrolment; the authors judged it "well tolerated".
**Grade:** emerging
**Applies to:** Experienced recreational lifters (largely CrossFit and weightlifting) in self-selected online surveys with varying denominators. Not novices or complicated pregnancies.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Observational, self-reported data; the 2023 abstract's figure for the share of recreational athletes is internally inconsistent (it reuses the weightlifting count), so no percentage is used here. Reverse causation is likely. Traditional cautions rest on expert opinion, and these data cannot prove safety. The 57% incontinence rate justifies pelvic-floor screening.
**Sources:** [S710], [S711]

### Valsalva and bracing in pregnancy
**Rule:** A brief breath-hold (bracing) during a heavy rep is not, on current data, an emergency or a reason to stop an experienced pregnant lifter. As a default, still cue exhaling on effort and avoid sustained or repeated maximal breath-holds. Stop lifting with dizziness, headache, visual disturbance, or leaking or heaviness in the pelvic floor; pelvic-floor symptoms during bracing mean lowering the load and referring to a pelvic-health physiotherapist.
**Evidence:** In 10 healthy pregnant women (about 26 weeks) doing back squat, bench press and deadlift at 70-90% of 10RM and then 90% with Valsalva, maternal HR peaked with Valsalva (squat about 137 bpm), with no fetal bradycardia, unchanged fetal HR before vs after, and normal umbilical blood-flow indices. In 15 pregnant women (about 23 weeks) vs non-pregnant women doing semi-reclined leg press at 20-60% of 10RM, cardiac responses were largely proportionate, and at 40% with and without Valsalva there were no between-group differences.
**Grade:** emerging
**Applies to:** Healthy second- and early third-trimester pregnancies; very small acute samples; fetal measures taken before and after, not during. Not hypertensive pregnancies or pelvic-floor outcomes.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Most guidelines still advise avoiding Valsalva on expert opinion; these studies are underpowered for rare events. Avoid Valsalva with gestational hypertension or pre-eclampsia risk, which were not studied.
**Sources:** [S712], [S713]

### Supine exercise after about 20 weeks
**Rule:** From mid-pregnancy (about 20 weeks), replace long or heavy supine work with an incline bench, side-lying or standing alternatives (the exact angle is a practical choice). Allow short supine bouts if she feels well. Stop immediately and change position (for example to side-lying) if she feels light-headed, nauseous or unwell. Also avoid long periods of standing still.
**Evidence:** A systematic review (7 studies, n=1759; very low to low quality) found no link between supervised programmes including supine exercise and low birth weight, but during acute supine exercise 20 of 65 fetuses (31%) showed abnormal heart rate patterns and uterine blood flow fell 13% from left-lateral rest to dynamic supine exercise; it concluded evidence is insufficient either way. SOGC/CSEP 2019 recommends only that women who feel unwell flat on their back change position. ACOG advises avoiding lying flat or standing still as much as possible because these may briefly lower blood pressure. In the survey of 679 heavy lifters, 71% (306/437) lifted supine, with no difference in reported outcomes.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Pregnant women, especially in the second half of pregnancy; very low to low quality evidence.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** "Never lie on your back after the first trimester" outruns the evidence; harm is not shown and safety not proven. ACOG's advice is broader than CSEP's symptom-based rule.
**Sources:** [S714], [S652], [S707], [S710]

### Gauge intensity by talk test and RPE, not a heart-rate cap
**Rule:** Use the talk test and RPE (or reps-in-reserve for lifting) as the main intensity gauges, with "can talk but not sing" or RPE 13-14 as the moderate target. Treat heart rate as secondary, not a hard ceiling, and do not apply a 140 bpm cap. If she cannot speak a sentence she is exercising vigorously, which is acceptable for previously active women; tell beginners to ease off.
**Evidence:** Pregnancy guidelines most often recommend Borg RPE, the talk test and heart rate. Moderate intensity is RPE 13-14 on the 6-20 scale, or able to talk but not sing; vigorous is not able to say more than a few words without pausing (CSEP GAQ-P). Both blunted and normal HR responses occur in pregnancy, so US guidance states RPE may be more effective than HR. ACOG's 1985 cap of 140 bpm was removed in 1994 but is still passed on.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** All pregnant clients; a synthesis of guideline content (a scoping review of 22 guidelines and an 11-guideline comparison), not outcome trials.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Some guidelines still give HR limits (Japan under 150, Spain under 140 as of 2014, age-adjusted zones in Canada and the UK). Guidelines contradict each other above moderate intensity. Pair RPE with symptoms.
**Sources:** [S715], [S716], [S708]

### Vigorous work: avoid near-maximal efforts
**Rule:** Vigorous cardio is acceptable for women who were already vigorously active before pregnancy. Avoid all-out efforts and long intervals near maximum (above about 90% HRmax, or RPE 18 or higher); cap conditioning at "hard but controlled" and stop at any warning sign. Have beginners build up from moderate intensity.
**Evidence:** A meta-analysis (10 cohorts, n=32,080; 5 RCTs, n=623) found vigorous exercise continued into the third trimester did not change birthweight (mean difference 8.06 g) or risk of small-for-gestational-age or low birth weight, with slightly longer gestation (+0.21 weeks) and slightly lower prematurity; the authors said exercise above 90% HRmax needs more research. In 6 pregnant Olympic-level endurance athletes (23-29 weeks), fetal HR stayed normal below 90% of maternal HRmax, while fetal bradycardia and high umbilical pulsatility index appeared above 90% when uterine blood flow fell below 50% of baseline, normalising quickly after stopping.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Healthy pregnancies, mostly observational; the 90% signal comes from one session in 6 elite athletes, an extreme case.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Safety of vigorous exercise (moderate evidence) and the 90% caution (emerging) rest on different evidence. Guidelines disagree on upper limits, and CSEP advises consulting a provider before taking up vigorous activity.
**Sources:** [S717], [S718], [S707]

### Heat in pregnancy
**Rule:** A normal air-conditioned gym session is fine. Encourage drinking water before, during and after, and light clothing. Avoid hot yoga or hot Pilates, and long hard sessions outdoors in hot, humid weather. In heat, lower intensity and duration, add rest breaks, and stop if she feels overheated, dizzy or unwell.
**Evidence:** A systematic review (12 studies, n=347) found no pregnant woman reached the 39.0 C core temperature regarded as teratogenic (highest 38.9 C; highest land-exercise mean 38.3 C), concluding women can safely do up to 35 min at 80-90% HRmax in 25 C and 45% humidity, exercise in water up to 33.4 C for up to 45 min, and sit in hot baths (40 C) or dry saunas (70 C) for up to 20 min. In a controlled trial (15 pregnant vs 15 non-pregnant), 45 min of moderate exercise at 32 C and 45% humidity produced a peak rectal temperature of 37.93 C with no thermoregulatory impairment. ACOG still advises avoiding overheating, especially in the first trimester, and avoiding hot yoga, hot Pilates and very hot or humid outdoor exercise.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Healthy pregnant women; very hot and humid conditions, long durations and maximal efforts are not well studied.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Data suggest hyperthermia fear is overstated for typical sessions, but guidelines stay cautious because environmental heat exposure is linked with adverse birth outcomes. Heat tolerance varies.
**Sources:** [S719], [S720], [S707]

## Postpartum

### When and how much to resume
**Rule:** Start with walking and pelvic-floor muscle training in the first weeks. Move to structured moderate-to-vigorous activity, including resistance training, once wounds have healed, lochia does not increase with activity and she has no symptoms. Aim for at least 120-150 min a week by the end of the first year. Progress load and impact by symptoms, not the calendar. Breastfeeding is not a reason to avoid training.
**Evidence:** The 2025 Canadian postpartum guideline recommends at least 120 min/week of MVPA over 4 or more days, including aerobic and resistance training, starting or returning within the first 12 weeks to support mental health; early light activity (walking, pelvic-floor training), progressing once incisions or tears have healed and lochia does not increase with exercise, individually and gradually. Postpartum activity was associated with lower odds of depression (45%), urinary incontinence (37%) and type 2 diabetes (28%), with no increased harm, including to breast-milk quality or quantity. WHO 2020 recommends at least 150 min/week of moderate activity plus muscle strengthening.
**Grade:** strong
**Applies to:** Postpartum women in the first year without contraindications, after vaginal and caesarean birth (caesarean needs wound healing and symptom-gated progression).
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Targets differ (120 vs 150 min). The routine "6-week check" is not an evidence-based exercise gate. Sleep, mental health, caesarean healing and pelvic-floor status vary.
**Sources:** [S721], [S016]

### Postpartum red flags
**Rule:** Run a postpartum intake screen (modelled on the Get Active Questionnaire for Postpartum). Any listed red flag means pausing moderate-to-vigorous training and getting clearance from a primary-care provider. Calf pain or swelling, chest pain, breathlessness or fainting means urgent medical care. Screen pelvic-floor symptoms, mood and sleep, and refer as appropriate.
**Evidence:** A Delphi study (95-120 experts, consensus at 75% or more) and the 2025 Canadian guideline list relative contraindications to MVPA in the first postpartum year: severe abdominal pain; vaginal bleeding not linked to menstruation; postpartum cardiomyopathy; caesarean wound symptoms that worsen with MVPA; unstable BP above 140/90; eating disorder or malnutrition; excessive fatigue suggesting anaemia or low energy availability; calf pain or swelling suggesting DVT; breathing difficulties; new chest pain with exertion; dizziness during MVPA; loss of consciousness; neurological symptoms; acute systemic infection with fever; kidney disease; fractures or significant musculoskeletal injury; haemodynamic instability. Pelvic-floor and abdominal-wall function, mental health, sleep and wound healing need extra screening.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Women in the first 12 months after birth; expert consensus adopted into a national guideline.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** These are relative contraindications; most women can proceed with modifications once cleared.
**Sources:** [S722], [S721]

### Return to running and impact
**Rule:** Keep postpartum clients on low-impact training (walking, cycling, strength work, pelvic-floor training) for about the first 12 weeks. Start running, jumping or plyometrics only after she passes the load-and-impact tests without symptoms. Use walk-run progressions, and step back if leaking, heaviness, dragging or pain appear. Treat 3 months as a minimum, not a guarantee.
**Evidence:** The Goom/Donnelly/Brockwell 2019 guidance (UK physiotherapists; key recommendation Level 4, expert opinion) advises low-impact exercise for the first 3 months and running at 3-6 months at the earliest, later with pelvic-floor symptoms. Before running she should complete without pain, heaviness, dragging or incontinence: 30 min walking; 10 s single-leg balance; 10 single-leg squats per side; 1 min jogging on the spot; 10 forward bounds; 10 hops per leg; 10 single-leg "running man" reps per side. Strength benchmarks are 20 reps each of single-leg calf raise, bridge and sit-to-stand, though weakness alone is not a barrier. Risk factors include being under 3 months postpartum, breastfeeding, caesarean or perineal scarring, obesity, pre-existing pelvic-floor or lumbopelvic dysfunction, hypermobility, RED-S and psychological drivers. A systematic review of elite athletes found very low-certainty evidence that they return before 6 weeks more often than controls (OR 6.93, 95% CI 2.73-17.63) and may have higher injury risk (14 injuries, including 7 stress fractures).
**Grade:** emerging
**Applies to:** Postpartum women returning to running or high-impact training; expert thresholds; elite data (11 studies, 372 athletes) may not generalise.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** The Canadian guideline encourages MVPA within 12 weeks while Goom delays running until 3 months; they fit together (general activity early, impact later and symptom-gated). Breastfeeding, delivery type, pelvic floor and running history change the timeline.
**Sources:** [S723], [S724]

### Pelvic-floor screening and referral
**Rule:** Ask every pregnant and postpartum client directly and routinely about leaking (including during lifts, jumps or coughing), urgency, vaginal heaviness or bulging, pelvic or back pain, and pain with intercourse. Any "yes" means referral to a pelvic-health physiotherapist; meanwhile reduce the load, impact or bracing demand that brings on the symptom. Encourage daily pelvic-floor training, ideally taught by a physiotherapist. Do not treat leaking during exercise as normal or something to push through.
**Evidence:** Goom 2019 lists signs of pelvic-floor or abdominal-wall dysfunction (urinary or faecal incontinence, hard-to-defer urgency, heaviness, pressure, bulge or dragging, pain with intercourse, obstructed defecation, pendular abdomen, separated abdominal muscles or reduced abdominal strength, lumbopelvic pain), recommends offering every postnatal woman a pelvic-health physiotherapy assessment from 6 weeks, and specifically advises referral for heaviness or dragging, leaking or poor bowel control, a pendular abdomen or noticeable midline gap, pelvic or low-back pain, or non-menstrual blood loss beyond 8 weeks. The 2025 Canadian guideline recommends daily postpartum pelvic-floor training with physiotherapist instruction; SOGC/CSEP 2019 says it may be done daily in pregnancy to reduce incontinence risk; the CSEP provider form includes referral to a physiotherapist for pain, impairment or a pelvic-floor assessment.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Pregnant and postpartum women, including years after birth; referral thresholds are UK/Irish expert consensus.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** No trials of screening itself; access to pelvic-health physiotherapy varies. Symptoms may be load-specific, so changing load or using a pessary or support garment under physiotherapist guidance may allow her to continue.
**Sources:** [S723], [S721], [S725]

### Pelvic-floor training prevents more than it treats
**Rule:** Encourage all pregnant clients to start structured pelvic-floor training early, ideally taught by a physiotherapist, as prevention. For a client who already leaks, do not present generic Kegel exercises as the fix; refer her to a pelvic-health physiotherapist for individual assessment and treatment.
**Evidence:** A Cochrane review (46 trials, 10,832 women) found antenatal pelvic-floor training in continent women probably lowers urinary incontinence in late pregnancy (RR 0.38, 95% CI 0.20-0.72; moderate quality) and slightly lowers it 3-6 months postpartum (RR 0.71, 0.54-0.95; high quality). For women already incontinent there was no clear treatment effect (for example RR 0.55, 0.29-1.07 at 6-12 months postpartum; low quality), and population-wide postnatal programmes are unlikely to help. Adverse effects were rare (2 of 43 women withdrew for pelvic-floor pain).
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Pregnant and postpartum women; prevention is clearest in continent women starting early.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Guidelines recommend daily training for everyone while Cochrane evidence for treating existing perinatal incontinence is uncertain. Effects depend on supervision and adherence.
**Sources:** [S726]

### Diastasis recti: common, usually not dangerous
**Rule:** Treat diastasis recti as common and usually not dangerous. Do not tell clients to avoid crunches or all abdominal work forever. Check for doming or sinking at the midline during core and lifting tasks; if present, regress load or exercise until she can control it and progress gradually. Refer to a pelvic-health physiotherapist if the gap is wide or functionally limiting, there is pain or a hernia-like bulge, or no improvement by about 6-12 months. Judge progress by function and symptoms, not finger-width measurements.
**Evidence:** In 300 first-time mothers, diastasis (palpated separation of 2 finger-widths or more) affected 33.1% at 21 weeks of pregnancy, 60.0% at 6 weeks postpartum, 45.4% at 6 months and 32.6% at 12 months; lumbopelvic pain did not differ at 12 months, and frequent heavy lifting (20 or more times a week) was associated with higher odds (OR 2.18, 1.05-4.52). A 2025 network meta-analysis (21 RCTs, 1195 women) found exercise alone reduced the gap by 0.48 cm (95% CI 0.16-0.80), more with electrical stimulation (1.12 cm); a 2026 meta-analysis (15 studies, 3 pooled) found no effect of transversus-focused exercise (MD -0.06 cm, 95% CI -1.19 to 1.08), concluding exercise alone has limited anatomical but possibly functional benefit. Goom 2019 allows return to running with a "functional" diastasis if she can control intra-abdominal pressure and transfer load; midline doming or sinking, lateral trunk shift or marked rib flare signal failed load transfer.
**Grade:** emerging
**Applies to:** Postpartum women; Norwegian first-time mothers diagnosed by palpation; small, heterogeneous trials; functional criteria are expert opinion.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** The meta-analyses conflict on whether exercise closes the gap. Claims that particular exercises "close" it, or that crunches worsen it, outrun the evidence.
**Sources:** [S727], [S728], [S729], [S730], [S723]

### Diastasis is not a marker of pelvic-floor dysfunction
**Rule:** Do not infer pelvic-floor dysfunction from the presence of diastasis, or rule it out because diastasis is absent. Screen pelvic-floor symptoms (leaking, heaviness or bulging, urgency, pain) separately in every postpartum client.
**Evidence:** In a prospective cohort of 300 first-time mothers, women with diastasis did not have weaker pelvic-floor muscles or more urinary incontinence at 6 weeks, 6 months or 12 months postpartum, and at 6 weeks prolapse was less common with diastasis (4.1%) than without (15.9%).
**Grade:** emerging
**Applies to:** Norwegian first-time mothers in a single cohort, diagnosed by palpation.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Goom 2019 advises treating diastasis as a potential pelvic-floor risk factor until research shows otherwise, which contrasts with the cohort data.
**Sources:** [S728], [S723]

## Conflicts and open questions

- **Leptin and ghrelin after short sleep.** The popular hormone story is weakly supported: one meta-analysis found no strong evidence of mean changes, another found small changes with leptin rising, against the story. [S572], [S574]
- **Size of the sleep-loss intake effect.** Estimates range from about 250 to 385 kcal/day; Capers 2015 found expenditure also rose and weight effects were inconsistent. [S571], [S572], [S573]
- **Evening exercise.** Stutz 2019 found no harm, a 2026 meta-analysis found more time awake after sleep onset with evening exercise, especially at higher intensity. [S590], [S591]
- **Sleep loss and strength.** Craven 2022 found strength reduced after acute sleep loss; a systematic review of resistance studies found little effect of total deprivation but losses after consecutive restricted nights. [S576], [S577]
- **Sleep hygiene vs CBT-I.** Hygiene checklists are popular but untested as treatment and conditionally not recommended alone for chronic insomnia. [S584], [S585], [S586]
- **Deloads and "planned overreaching".** Fixed deloads every 4-6 weeks come from surveys and consensus, not outcome trials; evidence for supercompensation after planned overreaching is mixed. [S134], [S136], [S137], [S747], [S748]
- **Overtraining or under-fuelling.** Many apparent overload cases may be low energy availability; no marker other than sustained performance decline is reliable. [S749], [S750]
- **Cold water immersion.** It reduces soreness but routine use after lifting may blunt hypertrophy. [S766], [S769], [S770]
- **NSAIDs and muscle growth.** High-dose ibuprofen reduced hypertrophy in young adults and increased it in older adults; NSAIDs may also raise rhabdomyolysis risk. [S771], [S772], [S773], [S761]
- **Ice and PEACE & LOVE.** The "avoid ice" element rests on theoretical reasoning; trials show short-term relief and a sham-controlled RCT has no results yet. [S698], [S704]
- **Static stretching and lifting.** "Stretching ruins your lifting" overstates small, duration-dependent effects; stretching does not prevent injuries; whether chronic stretching builds muscle is contested. [S601], [S603], [S600], [S606], [S607]
- **Pain-free vs pain-allowed rehab.** Pain-allowed exercise gives a small short-term benefit; the 5/10 pain ceiling and next-morning rule are conventions from small trials; return-to-play criteria still favour pain-free tests. [S611], [S612], [S682], [S684], [S686]
- **"Never round your back".** Low-quality evidence does not link lumbar flexion during lifting to back pain, but heavy loaded flexion is unstudied. [S616]
- **Back pain red flags.** Guidelines list 46 different flags with little accuracy evidence; history of cancer and trauma in older adults are the informative ones. [S689], [S690], [S693], [S694]
- **Habit formation.** "21 days" is not supported; estimates range from 4 to 335 days, and gym-specific data suggest about 6 weeks at 4 or more sessions a week. [S627], [S628], [S629]
- **Self-regulation techniques in older adults.** Goal setting and self-monitoring help younger adults but were associated with lower self-efficacy and activity in adults aged 60 and over. [S617], [S620], [S619]
- **Daily weighing.** Daily weighing is optional: weight-loss outcomes did not differ between daily and weekly weighing, but daily readings averaged weekly give a cleaner trend. [S636]
- **Tracking and eating disorders.** No association in general-population samples, but clinical ED samples report perceived harm. [S353], [S356], [S358]
- **Eating-disorder screening.** Universal SCOFF screening is not supported (USPSTF insufficient), and SCOFF misses men and binge eating. [S656], [S350], [S349]
- **PAR-Q+ vs ACSM screening.** The tools use different logic (age 45 cut-off vs activity plus disease plus symptoms) and can disagree; neither is validated against events. Blanket "see a doctor first" advice is itself a barrier for healthy asymptomatic people, but inactive people with known cardiovascular, metabolic (including T2D) or renal disease need clearance before any programme under ACSM 2015, which the coach follows over the softer ACSM 2022 T2D wording. [S641], [S119], [S120], [S647]
- **Neck check for colds.** Expert opinion with one small, partly non-randomised trial. [S756], [S757]
- **Post-COVID return.** Elliott 2020 recommended 10 days of rest; ACC 2022 says this is no longer necessary. [S759], [S760]
- **Rhabdomyolysis CK thresholds.** CHAMP uses 5,000 IU/L, ENMC 10,000 IU/L; symptoms and urine drive referral. [S761], [S648]
- **Diabetes glucose thresholds.** Upper cut-offs differ (250, 270, or 300 without ketones), all expert consensus. [S647], [S731], [S732]
- **Hypertension: isometric vs aerobic.** Isometric training ranks first for office BP, but only aerobic, HIIT and combined training clearly lower 24-h BP; in-exercise BP ceilings differ (220/105 vs 240/110). [S660], [S737], [S741], [S734], [S735]
- **Valsalva.** Heavy lifting with breath-holding produces extreme transient BP, but no link to clinical events in controlled hypertensives was shown; small pregnancy studies suggest brief bracing is tolerated. [S738], [S739], [S740], [S712], [S713]
- **Pregnancy contraindications.** Guideline lists vs a review arguing several (gestational hypertension, twins) may not warrant restriction. [S652], [S655]
- **Supine exercise and heart-rate caps in pregnancy.** "Never lie on your back" and the 140 bpm cap outrun the evidence; harm is not shown and safety is not proven. [S714], [S707], [S715], [S716]
- **Heavy lifting in pregnancy.** Survey data suggest it is tolerated by experienced lifters, but they cannot prove safety. [S710], [S711]
- **Pelvic-floor training.** Recommended daily for all, but evidence for treating existing perinatal incontinence is uncertain. [S721], [S726]
- **Diastasis recti.** Meta-analyses conflict on whether exercise narrows the gap; expert guidance treats it as a pelvic-floor risk factor while cohort data do not. [S729], [S730], [S728], [S723]
- **CKD protein.** The 1.3 g/kg/day cap is an ungraded practice point, and frail older adults may need more protein. [S366]
- **GLP-1 protein and ED risk.** Unclear which body mass to base protein on; evidence on eating-disorder effects is limited. [S361], [S669]

## When to refer out

- **Same day / emergency:** chest pain, fainting, palpitations or breathlessness at rest or with mild effort [S120], [S119]; cauda equina signs [S687]; suspected fracture by the Ottawa rules or Achilles rupture [S697], [S699], [S702]; dark urine or disproportionate muscle pain and swelling after training [S761], [S648]; pregnancy warning signs such as bleeding, fluid leakage, contractions or a swollen painful calf [S707], [S708]; postpartum calf pain, chest pain, breathlessness or fainting [S722]; hypoglycaemia in someone not alert, or high glucose with ketones and feeling unwell, or feeling unwell on an SGLT2 inhibitor [S733], [S731], [S647]; resting BP 200/110 or higher with symptoms [S734] (without symptoms: no exercise and a prompt doctor visit; full bands in Resting BP thresholds to defer or refer); MEED red flags (intake under about 500 kcal/day for 2 or more days, repeated vomiting, resting HR under 40, fainting) [S673].
- **GP or clinician within days:** any PAR-Q+ follow-up YES, ACSM cell requiring clearance, or resting BP of 160/90 or higher or unknown (180 systolic or higher: no training until that review) [S641], [S119], [S734]; back pain with a history of cancer, after trauma in older adults, clustered or progressive red flags, or not improving after about 4 weeks [S689], [S692], [S695]; eating-disorder warning signs, missed periods or REDs signs [S671], [S672], [S675], [S336]; malnutrition triggers or unintended weight loss [S665], [S667]; persistent performance decline after 2 weeks of reduced load [S747]; chronic insomnia (CBT-I) [S584]; daytime sleepiness with loud snoring, witnessed apnoeas or hypertension [S810]; probable sarcopenia on grip or chair-stand tests [S814]; acute knee injury with a pop and early swelling, giving way or locking [S815].
- **Dietitian or specialist team:** any diagnosed condition that diet affects, including diabetes, CKD, GLP-1 therapy and bariatric surgery [S796], [S658], [S366], [S361], [S670]; pelvic-floor symptoms or functionally limiting diastasis (pelvic-health physiotherapist) [S723]; tendinopathy or strains not responding to graded loading (physio) [S608], [S612].
